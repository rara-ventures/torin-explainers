# Why is Corsica fighting for autonomy from France?

**Source:** [https://torinrussell.com/explainers/why-is-corsica-fighting-for-autonomy-from-france](https://torinrussell.com/explainers/why-is-corsica-fighting-for-autonomy-from-france)

Corsica may be part of France, but it wasn’t always that way, and now the island is on the verge of gaining autonomy. But how would autonomy even work for a place that’s so reliant on mainland France?

Corsica is roughly 80km from Italy’s Tuscan coastline and about 170km from the French city of Nice, and this positioning helps to explain both its current political dynamics and its history.

After winning the naval Battle of Meloria against Pisa in 1284, the Republic of Genoa started taking control of the island over the course of several generations.

Genoa was a hugely influential Mediterranean power at the time with permanent settlements reaching far east to modern Turkiye and Ukraine. But Corsica was less a trading hub for Genoa and more a strategic foothold and a source of tax revenue.

But heavy taxes and local revolts grew into a rebellion from 1729 onward and pushed Corsica to declare independence. In 1755, the revolutionary Pasquale Paoli founded the Corsican Republic and gave it a written constitution drafted in Italian, one of the first modern constitutions anywhere in the world. The resulting representative democracy controlled much of the island’s interior, but Genoa held onto its coastal citadels.

But Genoa was financially exhausted and militarily incapable of suppressing the drive for independence. It had already been leaning on troops from France, its ally, just to maintain its Corsican holdings. Genoa owed them a lot of money. So in the 1768 Treaty of Versailles, Genoa handed Corsica to France. It was technically reversible if Genoa ever paid the debt back, but the republic was in terminal decline and never could, making the transfer permanent.

Napoleon was actually born on the island the following year, which made him a subject of France from birth instead of being Genoese.

The French military pushed into the island and overwhelmingly defeated the Republic of Corsica, which had not managed to acquire sufficient backing from foreign powers like the British. Corsica remains French to this day.

But it held onto its independent spirit. During WWII, Corsica was occupied by Italy, and after Italy's armistice in September 1943, German forces moved in. The island was liberated largely by its own resistance and Free French forces rather than through British or American ground involvement.

In 1975, about fifty Corsican nationalists occupied a wine cave near the town of Aleria in protest against a wine adulteration and fraud scandal and French land policy. In response, France sent 1200 gendarmes with armored vehicles and helicopters to end it. This is commonly considered the beginning of the modern Corsican nationalist movement.

Corsicans in that period were not happy with France for a variety of reasons, one of which was the resettlement in 1962 of about 20k French citizens repatriated from Algeria, which had recently gained independence. They received most of the state-developed farmland on the eastern plain, which transformed the local economy but seriously strained native Corsicans. Scandals, demographic and economic decline, clan politics, and a general disregard for linguistic and cultural differences were also major grievances.

For nearly four decades starting in 1976, the Corsican National Liberation Front, or FLNC, carried out thousands of bombings targeting symbols of the French state and second homes owned by people not from Corsica. The group funded itself partly through a so-called revolutionary tax, which is really just extortion.

The main FLNC movement announced an end to its armed struggle in 2014 without laying down arms. It cited the IRA in Ireland and the ETA in the Spanish Basque country as models going forward, and it shifted to a new stage of political conquest. Nonetheless, kinetic action never fully stopped. A reconstituted FLNC claimed roughly twenty bombings in a single night in October 2023, and a newer underground group has been second homes.

In 2022, the famous Corsican nationalist Yvan Colonna, who was serving life in prison for assassinating France’s prefect on the island back in 1998, was attacked by a fellow inmate at Arles prison and died three weeks later. Riots broke out across the island, and as a result the French interior minister opened up talks and said outright that France was ready to go as far as autonomy.

Nationalist parties have won solid majorities in recent Corsican elections, and polls show most Corsicans want more autonomy, though only a minority wants full independence. The nationalist camp isn't unified anymore either. The old autonomist-independentist alliance broke up, and the autonomists have governed largely on their own since 2021.

In 2023, French president Macron addressed the Corsican Assembly and openly endorsed a form of autonomy. Then in early 2024, both sides agreed to draft constitutional text that would formally acknowledge Corsica’s historical, linguistic, and cultural differences and give it room to adapt French laws and regulations locally with some oversight.

In July 2025, the French Council of Ministers adopted the bill and sent it to the Senate first. Then in April 2026, the government pulled it back and rerouted it through the National Assembly, which passed it in June 2026 by 271 votes to 202, with 64 abstentions. As of now, it's pending Senate approval, with debate scheduled for late October 2026, and this is really the main obstacle because the Senate leans more conservative, any constitutional change has to pass both chambers word for word and then clear a three-fifths vote, and the French political environment in general is unstable right now.

Meanwhile, Corsica is metropolitan France's poorest region by GDP per capita, and it's the most tourism-dependent region in the country. Tourism consumption runs at nearly 40% of regional GDP, though some local economists argue the true value add from the tourism industry is a lot lower. Local trade, services, and construction are the next largest sectors.

Corsica has about 365k people, and its growth, which is the fastest in mainland France, is driven entirely by in-migration, because deaths on the island now outnumber births. Most of the new arrivals are working-age adults and returning islanders, and the population is aging quickly.

What this means is that it is vulnerable to rising prices in general, but especially when it comes to housing. Second homes make up about 37% of all dwellings on the island, among the highest rates in France, and between those purchases and holiday rentals, many locals are getting priced out.

Corsica also has more homicides per capita than anywhere else in metropolitan France. In fact, the risk of being assassinated is about six times higher in Corsica than in Sicily, where the mafia is notoriously active. About a third of people own a firearm, and there are about 25 active criminal gangs. In truth though, this is less a result of simple poverty and more about organized crime feeding off land speculation, the value of the tourism economy, construction and waste contracts, and a very weak response from the state.

But Corsica is disproportionately important to France. The island hosts Air Base 126 at Solenzara. During WWII, the whole island earned the nickname USS Corsica because it functioned as an unsinkable Allied aircraft carrier, and that strategic logic still holds. It’s a French air and space force forward base that’s used heavily for NATO tactical training, hosting strategic transport aircraft, tankers, fighters, and all the support infrastructure they would need. It’s supported operations over Bosnia, Kosovo, and Libya and serves as a permanent base for projecting force into the Mediterranean more broadly.

France is afraid of losing Corsica, and it's willing to make concessions to keep the island inside the Republic. The recent crisis in New Caledonia, a French territory with a powerful independence movement, made it clear that island territories with their eyes set on independence can have an outsized impact on France itself. But French constitutional doctrine holds that the Republic is one and indivisible, and France's constitutional court has ruled there's no separate Corsican people within the French people. Autonomy is always going to be limited from a legal standpoint. Ultimately, France will have to balance between these two dynamics.
