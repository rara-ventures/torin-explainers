# 16th Century CE

The sixteenth century ends the Indianized state system and begins its afterlife. Portuguese Melaka, Islamic Demak, Aceh and Banten partition the old Sanskritic heartland, while Gelgel's Bali conserves Indo-Javanese ritual, text and temple form. Champa is reduced to Panduranga, and the mainland courts recast a Brahminical inheritance in Theravada and Confucian idioms.

## What happened

Albuquerque's fleet took Melaka between 25 July and 24 August 1511 against a much larger garrison. Sultan Mahmud Shah withdrew to Bertam, then Bintan, and died at Kampar in 1528; the Portuguese built A Famosa over the palace and kept the port's tax machinery, the *bendahara* office and much of the Malay commercial code. What ended was not trade but the Malay court that had made Sanskritic kingship and Islam compatible (Subrahmanyam 1993; Reid 1988).

Java's conversion was completed by conquest. Demak, the first Muslim state on the north coast, took the Majapahit capital at Daha in 1527 under Sultan Trenggana; courtiers, priests and artisans fled to Bali and Blambangan. Demak's own unity did not survive Trenggana's death in 1546: a civil war ended with Jaka Tingkir founding Pajang in 1568, and from the 1580s Sutawijaya built Mataram at Kota Gede. The new courts kept the *wayang* repertoire, the Śaka calendar, gamelan and the old administrative titles; the ruler styled himself *Senapati ing Alaga Sayidin Panatagama*, commander in war and regulator of religion (Ricklefs 2008; Pigeaud and de Graaf 1976).

On Sumatra, Aceh Darussalam rose from c. 1514, absorbed Pasai's trade in 1524, and under Alauddin Riayat Shah al-Qahhar (r. 1537–1571) became the region's leading "gunpowder empire," attacking Portuguese Melaka repeatedly without taking it. Banten, conquered by Demak in 1526–27 and independent from 1552, grew rich on pepper sold to Chinese, Gujarati and Portuguese buyers.

Bali is the century's most consequential survivor. Gelgel reached its height under Dalem Baturenggong, whose court hosted the Brahmin reformer Dang Hyang Nirartha. Nirartha's promotion of the *padmasana* altar, the Śaiva *pedanda* lineage and the Sanskrit *lontar* canon gave Balinese religion the shape it retains. Gelgel claimed suzerainty over Blambangan, Lombok and Sumbawa, and *subak* irrigation confederations bound water temples to royal authority (Vickers 1989; Creese 2016).

Elsewhere the contraction was military. Champa held only Panduranga and Kauthara, paying tribute both north and south. Bayinnaung took Ayutthaya in 1569; Naresuan restored Siamese independence in 1584. Siam sacked Longvek in 1594, completing the shift of Khmer gravity away from Angkor.

## Indianization evidence

Bali supplies the densest evidence because the century's upheaval made preservation self-conscious. The *Nagarakretagama* survives only in Balinese manuscripts, one copied in East Bali in 1740; Old Javanese *kakawin*, Sanskrit mantras and the Śaiva–Buddhist priestly vocabulary entered the *lontar* corpus as ancestral authority rather than foreign import. The *padmasana*, the *pura* network and the *subak* water temples are Indian-derived forms reorganised under Gelgel (Vickers 1989).

In Java the evidence is lexical and institutional. Islamic Mataram's titles, its Śaka-derived calendrical reckonings and its shadow-theatre epics are Sanskritic in origin, and the *Kutaramanawa* legal tradition continued to circulate. Aceh wrote Malay in Arabic script but kept the title *Sultan* and a ceremonial vocabulary from the same Sanskritic stock (Reid 1988).

Portuguese Melaka is the negative case: an administration that took the revenues and left the temple and *kakawin* behind. In Champa, Po Nagar and its *mukhaliṅga* cult outlasted the kingdom itself, as did the Sanskrit regnal names of Panduranga's kings.

## Debates & confidence

The "fall of Majapahit" is two events, not one. 1478 is Ranawijaya's coup at Trowulan; 1527 is Demak's capture of Daha. Calling 1527 the end of an already fragmented state is convention, not finding (Ricklefs 2008).

Gelgel's chronology rests almost entirely on the *Babad Dalem*, an eighteenth-century compilation. Baturenggong's reign is placed c. 1520–1550 and Nirartha's arrival variously in 1537, 1546 or later; the sequence is secure, the dates are not. Confidence: medium on the reform, low on its dating.

The "Bali as Hindu refuge" thesis is the century's most contested inheritance. Vickers and Creese argue that Balinese Hinduism was actively reformed and politically produced, not a fossil carried out of Java; the refuge narrative flatters colonial scholarship and Indonesian cultural politics. Confidence in continuity of personnel and texts: high. In the metaphor of the museum: low.
