# 5th Century CE

The 5th century is when the Sanskrit package jumps from the mainland coast into the archipelago. Seven sacrificial posts in eastern Borneo and footprint inscriptions in western Java record Brahmin ritual and hydraulic kingship where no Indic epigraphy existed before. Meanwhile Funan's court claims to have rewritten its laws on the Indian model, and the Mon of the Chao Phraya begin raising the Wheel of the Law.

## What happened

Kutai, in East Kalimantan's Mahakam valley, is the surprise. Seven stone yupas from Muara Kaman carry Sanskrit in Pallava script and commemorate a great Vedic sacrifice sponsored by King Mūlavarman, with gifts of buffalo, gold and land to Brahmins. The king-list is the giveaway: Kuṇḍungga, a name with no Sanskrit etymology, is followed by Aśvavarman and Mūlavarman. A local chiefly line adopted Indic names, Indic ritual and an Indic script within two generations (Vogel 1918; de Casparis 1956).

West Java applies the same package to water. The Tarumanagara inscriptions of Purnavarman — Ciaruteun, Kebon Kopi, Jambu, Tugu, Cidanghiang — are Sanskrit in Pallava letters. Ciaruteun compares the king's footprints to Vishnu's; Tugu records that he cut a canal about 11 km long in 22 days, with Brahmins overseeing the ritual that sanctified the work (de Casparis 1956). Kingship, script, cult and irrigation engineering arrive together, as one technology of rule.

Faxian supplies the only eyewitness. Sailing home from Sri Lanka in 413–414, he was driven by storm to a land he calls Yavadvipa, and the record he left notes Brahmins flourishing there while Buddhism was held in little esteem (Coedès 1968). A snapshot, not a survey, but the earliest external description of Java's religious market.

In the delta the evidence turns epigraphic. The *Liang shu* states that Kaundinya II, reigning around the 420s, "changed all the laws to conform to the system of India" — the first explicit claim of legal Brahmanization anywhere in the region (Pelliot 1903). The Tháp Mười stele K.5, from the mid-5th century, records a prince Guṇavarman of the Kauṇḍinya line donating to Vishnu, in Sanskrit and Pallava-Grantha (Coedès 1968). The dynasty's own monuments now speak the cosmopolitan language.

On the mainland, Chenla was forming inland while Funan held the coast; the Mon of central Thailand were building moated settlements at U Thong, Chansen and Si Thep and erecting dharmachakra pillars; Kedah's Bujang Valley entered the record. In Sri Lanka, Buddhaghosa reorganized the Sinhala commentaries into Pali, fixing the Theravada canon that would later travel to the mainland (Skilling 2003). Rome fell in 476; the Hephthalites pressed on Gandhara. The sea route did not care.

## Indianization evidence

This is the century the vault's second phase is named for: the package stops being a coastal import and starts reproducing itself in new ecologies.

Script: Sanskrit in Pallava script now appears in Borneo, Java and the Mekong delta. The vernacular stage has not arrived — Old Malay and Old Khmer epigraphy begin in the next century — so the script is still a court language rather than a local one.

Kingship: the yupas, the *-varman* names, the Vishnu footprints and the canal together describe a ruler who is both sacrificer and hydraulic manager, legitimating labour through ritual. That is a full Indic royal theology, not a veneer.

Cult: Vedic sacrifice in Kutai, Vaiṣṇavism in Java and Funan, Theravada in Dvaravati and Sri Lanka. The plurality is itself evidence — different Indian models were selected, not one exported whole.

Law: Kaundinya II's reform is the first explicit legal claim, but it survives only in a Chinese summary written two centuries later.

Three criteria are met in at least four polities. The transplant is real.

## Debates & confidence

**Kutai's date** is disputed between the 4th and 5th centuries, and the exact Vedic rite named in the yupas is uncertain.

**Kaundinya II** may be a Chinese compression of a Funanese dynastic claim; the "laws of India" line is evocative but unverifiable.

**Faxian's Yavadvipa** has been placed in Java, Sumatra and Borneo, and his remark on Buddhism describes one port, not an island.

**"Dvaravati"** is a coin legend and an art style, not a documented kingdom; the "proto-Dvaravati" phase is an archaeological convenience.

**Chenla's rise** is dated after 550 by most scholars, so 5th-century "Chenla" is better understood as Funan's inland faction.

Confidence: high on the inscriptions' content, medium on Kutai's chronology, low on Kaundinya II.
