# 15th Century CE

The fifteenth century is where the Sanskritic order contracts without disappearing. Melaka converts to Islam and inherits the strait; Angkor is abandoned as a capital; Vijaya falls to Đại Việt and Champa is reduced to a coastal rump. Yet Sanskrit titles, Dharma-derived law and Brahminical ritual survive inside the new Islamic and Theravada states, and Bali begins to assemble the archive that will preserve them.

## What happened

Melaka is the century's hinge. Parameswara, the last ruler of Singapura, fled a Majapahit attack in 1398 and established a settlement at the Bertam river around 1402. He placed it under Ming protection, and Zheng He's fleets used it as a warehouse and staging base between 1405 and 1433; the ruler travelled to the Ming court in 1411. Conversion to Islam followed around 1414, and the court kept the commercial machinery intact while adopting the sultanate form. Under Muzaffar Shah and Mansur Shah (r. 1459–1477) Melaka controlled the peninsula's tin ports and the Sumatran pepper coast, administered by four *shahbandars* and a *bendahara*, with a written maritime and commercial code (Reid 1988; Miksic 2013).

On the mainland, Ayutthaya absorbed Sukhothai in 1438 and, under Borommatrailokkanat (r. 1448–1488), codified the Palatine Law and staged a fully Brahminical coronation. Its chronicles place the sack of Angkor in 1431, after which the Khmer court moved to Phnom Penh and later Longvek; Theravada continued at Angkor Wat regardless (Chandler 1983).

Champa's end came by invasion, not conversion. Lê Thánh Tông of Đại Việt mobilised a force reportedly exceeding 100,000 and took Vijaya between 18 and 22 March 1471. Court annals claim 40,000 dead and 30,000 captured; king Maha Sajan (Trà Toàn) died in captivity. The northern provinces were annexed as Quảng Nam, and Champa survived only as the small polities of Panduranga and Kauthara (Kiernan 2017; Lafont 2007).

Java's transition was slower and largely internal. The Paregreg civil war of 1404–1406 drained Majapahit's treasury; Suhita (r. 1429–1447) was the last strong ruler. After 1468 the court split between Trowulan and Daha, and in 1478 Ranawijaya killed Kertabhumi and briefly reunified it, commemorated in the Jiyu charter of 1486. Meanwhile the north coast ports — Gresik, Demak, Cirebon — passed to Muslim lords who retained Javanese regnal titles and much of the old administrative vocabulary (Ricklefs 2008).

Bali received the outflow: the Majapahit-appointed line at Samprangan shifted to Gelgel late in the century, and after 1478 Javanese priests, scribes and nobles arrived with manuscripts and ritual expertise.

## Indianization evidence

The evidence here is unusually clear: Indian forms survive a change of religion. Parameswara is *parama-īśvara*, "supreme lord," a Sanskrit theophoric title borne by a Muslim sultan; his dynasty traced itself through Sri Tri Buana (*tri-bhuwana*, the three worlds) to Iskandar Zulkarnain. The *bendahara* takes his title from Sanskrit *bhāṇḍāgāra*, the treasury, and the *Undang-Undang Melaka* retains Sanskrit-derived legal terms beneath its Islamic and adat layers (Reid 1988).

In Champa, the fall of Vijaya did not end Śaiva worship: Po Nagar continued to receive royal patronage, and the *mukhaliṅga* cult was carried south to Panduranga rather than abolished. In Cambodia, Theravada monasticism inherited Angkor's sacred geography, with Buddhist monks occupying temples built for Viṣṇu and Śiva.

Bali is the clearest case of continuity by relocation. The exodus after 1478 transferred manuscripts, genealogies and priestly lineages; the *Nagarakretagama* itself survives only in Balinese copies, one colophon written in East Bali in 1740.

## Debates & confidence

Three disputes dominate. First, Melaka's founding: 1400, 1402 and earlier dates have all been defended since Ferrand and Rouffaer argued the question in 1918–21, on Portuguese retrospect and a later Malay chronicle. Confidence in "c. 1400–1402" is moderate; in the sequence, high.

Second, Angkor's abandonment. The 1431 date comes from Siamese and Khmer chronicles; archaeology shows continued occupation and slow shrinkage, and hydraulic failure or drought may matter more than war. Confidence in 1431 as an event: low; in a fifteenth-century demographic collapse: high (Chandler 1983).

Third, the 1471 casualty figures. The 40,000 dead and 30,000 captured derive from court annals with reason to magnify. The annexation and the collapse of Vijaya are certain; the numbers are not (Kiernan 2017).
