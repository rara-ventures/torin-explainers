# 7th Century CE

The seventh century is the epigraphic threshold. In 671 the Chinese pilgrim Yijing spent six months at Palembang and found a Buddhist scholarly centre; twelve years later a local ruler cut the Kedukan Bukit charter in Old Malay, the first substantial text in the Malay language. Between these dates Srivijaya, Chenla, and Champa all produced inscriptions showing Indian script, Sanskrit vocabulary, and Buddhist or Śaiva institutions being used for Southeast Asian political purposes.

## What happened

Srivijaya's founding archive is a set of Old Malay stones from the Musi basin and Bangka. Yijing, sailing to Nalanda in 671, stopped at Shilifoshi (Palembang) for six months and reported more than a thousand monks studying Sanskrit grammar and Vinaya "as they do in the Middle Country"; he advised Chinese monks to learn there before proceeding to India (Yijing 1896). The Kedukan Bukit inscription, dated 1 May 683, records that Dapunta Hyang took a *siddhayatra*—a prosperous or magical journey—with 20,000 troops, 200 boats and 1,312 on foot, from Minanga Tamwan to the Upang region, and ends with the formula "Śrīvijaya, victorious, prosperous, abundant forever" (de Casparis 1956). Talang Tuwo (23 March 684) records the king's foundation of the Śrīksetra park, with a prayer that all beings might attain bodhi and a diamond body. Kota Kapur (686) curses officials who treasonously support rivals and announces a campaign against Java (Griffiths 2011). The Telaga Batu stone, with seven nāga heads and a water spout, was an oath object over which liquid was poured.

On the mainland, Isanavarman I (r. 616–637) made Isanapura the Chenla capital and left a foundation inscription dated 13 September 627 (K.604); his eulogy claims rule over Suvarṇabhūmi "as far as the sea". Jayavarman I (r. c. 657–681) was the last ruler of a united Chenla; after his death the inscriptions thin and the polity fragments. In the Chao Phraya plain, Xuanzang's seventh-century geography places "To-lo-po-ti" between Isanapura and Sri Ksetra, the earliest external notice of Dvaravati; Yijing adds that its people revere the Three Jewels (Coedès 1968).

In Champa, Prakāśadharman/Vikrāntavarman I (r. 653–686) issued C.96 on 18 February 658, the first inscription to name Campāpura and to claim descent from the Brahmin Kaundinya and the nāga princess Somā. He built at Mỹ Sơn, took the title *rāja-di-rāja*, and sent five embassies to the Tang court between 653 and 670 (Goodall and Griffiths 2013). The Kauthara sanctuary of Po Nagar was already ancient when its earliest surviving stele was cut in 781; its mukhaliṅga and goddess cult belong to this wider seventh-century Śaiva landscape.

## Indianization evidence

The Srivijayan inscriptions are the clearest single body of evidence for localization. The language is Old Malay, the script is Pallava-derived, and the vocabulary of rule mixes Sanskrit and Malay—*siddhayatra*, *śrīvijaya*, *kadatuan*—yet the grammar, the oath formulae, and the curses are Malay. The king does not build a temple-mountain or claim descent from an Indian dynasty; he takes a journey, gathers followers, and binds them by oath (Wolters 1999). Buddhism supplies the cosmology but not a theocratic state.

Champa shows the other pattern: a Sanskrit epigraphic culture in which the king claims the Kaundinya–Somā myth, uses *-varman* titles, and builds brick liṅga sanctuaries. Chenla's K.604 and Dvaravati's Pali inscriptions show the same selective adoption in mainland courts and monasteries. In every case the imported element is a technology of legitimacy—script, genealogy, consecration, monastic learning—not an administration. The vector is carried by monks, Brahmins, and merchant communities, and the receiving polity determines what is kept.

## Debates & confidence

Srivijaya's capital is unresolved: Palembang remains the Coedès–Manguin orthodoxy, but Muara Jambi and Chaiya have advocates, and no site has produced a convincing urban core (Kulke 1993). The 20,000 troops of 683 and the 1,312 foot soldiers are probably rhetorical; *siddhayatra* may mean "prosperous journey" rather than a magic potion. Yijing's "more than a thousand monks" is an impression, not a census. Telaga Batu is dated anywhere from the seventh to the ninth century. Champa's unity is contested: Vickery (2005) treats "Champa" as a cover-name for several river-mouth polities, and Xuanzang never visited Dvaravati, so his notice is hearsay.
