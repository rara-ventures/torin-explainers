# 6th Century CE

The sixth century is where mainland Southeast Asia's documentary record becomes legible politically. Funan's maritime supremacy fades, Chenla's inland princes push down the Mekong, and the first dated Old Khmer inscription (612) shows an imported script already doing local administrative work. In Champa the same decades brought a rebuilt Śaiva sanctuary, a catastrophic Chinese invasion, and the first layers of a durable Sanskrit epigraphic culture.

## What happened

Funan's decline is visible through Chinese notices. The delta polity sent its last embassy to the Liang in 539, and Rudravarman (r. 514–c. 545) lost authority upstream (Coedès 1968). The *Sui shu*, compiled in 636, describes a vassal state called Chenla whose rulers Bhavavarman I and his brother Citrasena—crowned Mahendravarman—attacked Funan; the *Xin Tang shu* later credits Isanavarman I (r. 616–637). Both compress a long shift from delta to Tonle Sap, not a single war (Vickery 2003; Jacques 1979). Isanavarman's capital, Isanapura, is Sambor Prei Kuk, whose brick prasats define the pre-Angkorian style; an inscription there is dated 13 September 627 (K.604). At Angkor Borei, Old Khmer is first dated in 612.

In Champa, Sambhuvarman (r. 572–629) rebuilt the Bhadreśvara sanctuary at Mỹ Sơn after a fire recorded in 535/536. In 605 the Sui general Liu Fang invaded Linyi. Liu's troops broke the Cham elephant line and sacked the capital; the victors removed eighteen golden tablets and a Buddhist library of 1,350 works (Taylor 1983). The occupation collapsed at once, and the Cham sent tribute to the Tang in 623. The episode matters less for conquest than for the lost archive and the shift of the capital to Simhapura (Trà Kiệu).

On the peninsula, the Bujang Valley around Merbok emerged as a port complex with brick shrines, iron smelting at Sungai Batu, and a stone bar inscribed with the *ye dharmā* formula in a Pallava-type script (Murphy 2018). Arab writers later called it Kalah, a ninth- and tenth-century label. Moated Mon centres were producing Buddhist sculpture and the earliest written Old Mon, dated late sixth century at Wat Phra Rang (Skilling 2003). The century's most quoted religious event is the arrival at Luy Lâu of the Indian monk Vinītaruci in 580, traditionally the first patriarch of Vietnamese Thiền; it survives only in the fourteenth-century *Thiền uyển tập anh* and is probably a later lineage construction (Nguyen 1997).

## Indianization evidence

Four classes of evidence matter. Script is the most decisive: the 612 Angkor Borei text uses an imported South Indian-derived alphabet to write Khmer, not Sanskrit, so the technology was adopted for local administration within a generation (Wolters 1999). Sanskrit titulature supplies the second—the *-varman* names—encoding royal protection by a deity and a claim to Brahmanical legitimacy. Architecture supplies the third: the brick prasats of Sambor Prei Kuk and the liṅga sanctuary at Mỹ Sơn are Indian in form but sited and scaled to local cults. Institutions supply the fourth: the Buddhist library at Linyi and the monastic networks behind Vinītaruci's tradition.

None of this requires Indian rule; no source attests one. The evidence shows a few specialists—Brahmins, monks, scribes—serving local kings who wanted a cosmopolitan vocabulary of power. The *ye dharmā* bar and Old Mon fragments show the same process among merchants and monasteries.

## Debates & confidence

Chenla's "conquest" of Funan rests on single remarks in Chinese annals; Vickery (2003) treats the dynastic sequence as an artefact of annalistic convention, and Jacques (1979) denied that either name denoted a unitary state. The Mỹ Sơn A1 kalan, often called sixth-century, is now dated to the tenth or eleventh century (Baptiste 2009); the dancer pedestal belongs to E1 and is seventh- or eighth-century, so the older A1 story is a misattribution. The Vinītaruci tradition is legendary (Nguyen 1997). Kedah's chronology is unsettled, with Sungai Batu radiocarbon ranges read as both mid-first-millennium BCE and early centuries CE (Khaw and Gooi 2021). Sui loot figures are court rhetoric.
