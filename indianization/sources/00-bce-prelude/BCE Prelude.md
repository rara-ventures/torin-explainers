# BCE Prelude (500 BCE – 1st c. CE)

The BCE Prelude is the long fuse: the three centuries between Ashoka's missions and the first Roman ships in the Indian Ocean, when the ingredients of Indianization were assembled in India and Sri Lanka but had not yet crystallized in Southeast Asia. It covers the Mauryan imperium, Buddhism's spread to Lanka, the first Tamil-Brahmi ports and the invention of Suvarnabhumi. The Southeast Asian evidence is almost entirely archaeological and pre-epigraphic.

## What happened

The prelude begins with Mauryan unification. Ashoka ruled c. 268–232 BCE and converted the empire's ideology after the Kalinga war of c. 261 BCE (Thapar 1961). His rock and pillar edicts, cut in Brahmi and Kharosthi, are the first substantial Indian inscriptions and the first royal public text (Falk 2006). Rock Edict 13 lists five Hellenistic kings as recipients of his dhamma missions—Antiochos, Ptolemy, Antigonos, Magas and Alexander. It is often cited, wrongly, as a reference to Suvarnabhumi. The Land of Gold belongs to a later textual horizon.

The most consequential mission was to Sri Lanka. The Mahavamsa places Mahinda's arrival at Mihintale in 246 BCE, during the reign of Devanampiya Tissa, and credits the mission with founding the island's Buddhist monarchy (Geiger 1912). Anuradhapura became the capital and the Thuparama one of its earliest relic monuments. In the first century BCE the Pali canon was written down at Aluvihara, fixing Theravada's textual base (von Hinüber 1996).

South India developed in parallel. The Pandya, Chola and Chera chiefdoms controlled the Kaveri and Vaigai valleys and the Malabar coast; their poets produced the Sangam anthologies, and their ports produced Tamil Brahmi. Arikamedu has yielded Tamil Brahmi, Rouletted Ware, amphorae and beads from the second century BCE onward (Mahadevan 2003). The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, composed in the first century CE, describes the route from the Red Sea to the Malabar coast and names the ports and goods of the Indo-Roman trade (Casson 1989). Roman amphorae, glass and coins at Arikamedu attest the trade's scale (Tomber 2008).

Southeast Asia was being drawn into the same network. Khao Sam Kaeo on the Kra Isthmus, occupied c. 400–100 BCE, shows Indian materials and technical styles in locally made objects. Ban Don Ta Phet in central Thailand has produced Indian beads in burials of the fourth to second centuries BCE. By the first century CE, Oc Eo and Angkor Borei were emerging as substantial canal-linked settlements in the Mekong Delta (Stark 2007). None has produced a Sanskrit inscription, an Indian temple or a documented Indian ruler. The route, the goods and the myths exist; the institutions do not yet.

## Indianization evidence

Script is the prelude's most durable export. Brahmi and Kharosthi were mature by the Mauryan period; Tamil Brahmi adapted Brahmi to a Dravidian language; the Pallava script that carried Sanskrit to Southeast Asia is fourth-century CE (Salomon 1998). Script travels with religion and trade, but not yet in Southeast Asia.

Kingship is the second strand. Ashoka's model is a ruler who governs through dhamma and sends missions beyond his borders. The Lankan monarchy that adopted Buddhism made the king the protector of the sangha, a formula that would recur across Southeast Asia. The Tamil chiefdoms show fragmented warrior patrons and poet-client relationships. The Funan legend of the Brahmin and nāga princess belongs to a later text but is the kind of charter myth the prelude makes possible.

Cult and law remain largely Indian. Buddhist monastic discipline, Brahmanical ritual and Jain asceticism were institutionalized in India; Sri Lanka received the Buddhist version. In Southeast Asia the evidence is limited to goods, craft techniques and settlement patterns. There is no inscription, temple or legal text.

## Debates & confidence

Suvarnabhumi's location is unresolved: the toponym appears in the Mahavamsa, Jatakas, Milindapanha and Ramayana, and has been placed from Bengal to the Malay Peninsula (Coedès 1968). The timeline's claim that Ashoka's edicts mention Suvarnabhumi is incorrect; Rock Edict 13 names Hellenistic kings. The Mahavamsa's Mahinda dates are traditional and unverified (Geiger 1912). The Pali canon's writing date is likewise a chronicle date, though the first-century BCE context is accepted (von Hinüber 1996). The Periplus is dated c. 40–70 CE, but its port identifications are debated (Casson 1989). The scale of Indo-Roman trade is contested (Tomber 2008). Confidence is high for the Mauryan and Lankan narrative, medium for the Tamil and Roman sequence, low for Southeast Asian institutions.
