# 3rd Century CE

This is Funan's century in the Chinese record and India's century in the epigraphic record. A Funanese general seizes a throne and sends missions to Eastern Wu; two Wu envoys write the first eyewitness description of a Southeast Asian state. At the same moment the Ikṣvāku kings of Andhra abandon Prakrit for Sanskrit, creating the idiom Southeast Asian courts will later borrow.

## What happened

Funan's expansion is known from Chinese histories compiled long after the fact. Fan Shiman, a general raised to the throne, is credited with a fleet, a reformed bureaucracy and conquests across "five or six thousand li"; he died campaigning against Chin-lin, the "Frontier of Gold". His successors — Chin-cheng, Fan Chan, Ch'ang, Fan Hsun — took power by assassination, and the *Liang shu* credits Fan Chan with missions to India and China in 243 (Pelliot 1903; Coedès 1968).

The Wu embassies are the hinge. Cut off from the northern silk route, Eastern Wu needed the southern sea lane; its governor in Jiaozhi, Lü Dai, received Funanese missions in 225–230 and 243, then sent Kang Tai and Zhu Ying to Funan around 245–250. Their reports survive only in quotation and paraphrase, which is why their date is still argued (Wheatley 1961). They describe a walled capital, a ranked court, taxes in gold, silver, pearls and aromatics, stilt houses, archives — and a population that went naked until the envoys taught the court to wear cloth.

The same reports place Tun Sun on the Malay Peninsula, an independent kingdom seized by Funan and by 245–250 its vassal: five royal cities and a market where "east and west meet" (Wheatley 1961). Its location is unresolved.

Óc Eo's infrastructure belongs to this horizon. A rampart and moat roughly 1.5 by 2 km enclose the site; an axial canal linked it to the Gulf, a second ran north to Angkor Borei (Malleret 1962; Stark 2007). Urban hydraulics at scale, requiring corvée labour and a court able to mobilise it.

In India the Ikṣvāku dynasty (c. 225–325) ruled from Vijayapuri, modern Nāgārjunakoṇḍa. Its queens and laywomen funded Buddhist monuments, and its inscriptions switch from Prakrit to Sanskrit — the shift Pollock makes the opening of the "Sanskrit cosmopolis" (Pollock 2006). The Pallavas appear at Kanchipuram around 275; the Sasanians took Ctesiphon in 224 while Rome slid into crisis, re-routing but not ending western trade.

Buddhism travelled with merchants. Kang Senghui, born in Jiaozhi to a Sogdian merchant father, reached the Wu court in the 240s, enshrined a relic at Jiankang and translated sutras; the earliest Chinese Buddhist apologetic written on Vietnamese soil, the *Lihuo lun* attributed to Mâu Tử, belongs to the same milieu (Zürcher 2007).

## Indianization evidence

The 3rd century is where the four-part test starts to bite — but in India, not yet in Southeast Asia.

Script: no Southeast Asian inscription is securely dated to this century. Võ Cạnh may be one, and if so it is decisive. The model was being manufactured upstream: Ikṣvāku inscriptions use Sanskrit in a formal courtly register, and the Pallava chancery at Kanchi was developing the rounded southern Brahmi that becomes Pallava Grantha (Pollock 2006).

Kingship: the Chinese notices give the first dense description of a Southeast Asian court — walls, hierarchy, tribute, stored wealth, writing — but no Sanskrit title, *-varman* name or royal linga. Funan looks like a wealthy mandala with Indian courtiers, not yet a dharma-kingdom.

Cult: Óc Eo has yielded Amaravati-style Buddha bronzes and a mukhalinga, conventionally dated to this horizon (Malleret 1962; Ray 1989). A Buddhist presence is probable; a state cult is not demonstrated.

Law: absent. Nothing shows dharmaśāstra, Indic procedure or Sanskrit legal formulae.

The century deepens contact and supplies the vocabulary. Adoption — a local ruler dating and legitimating himself in Sanskrit — comes in the 4th.

## Debates & confidence

**Kang Tai's report** survives only in quotation, and the *Liang shu* was compiled in 636 from earlier compilations; the mission is dated anywhere from 226 to 250 (Wheatley 1961). **Fan Shiman's** chronology is equally soft, and Coedès's identification of him with Śrī Māra is now widely rejected.

**Tun Sun's location** is unresolved: Kra Isthmus, Tenasserim and the Gulf shore all have defenders.

**The Sanskrit shift** is real but gradual; Pollock's "cosmopolis" describes a centuries-long process, and Prakrit and Sanskrit coexist in Ikṣvāku records rather than replacing one another cleanly.

Confidence: high that Funan was a wealthy maritime polity in contact with India and China; medium on the Wu mission's content; low on Funan's borders, Fan Shiman's dates and Tun Sun.
