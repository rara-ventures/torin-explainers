# 2nd Century BCE (200–101 BCE)

The second century BCE is the first in which a Greek king becomes a Buddhist patron and Tamil ports begin to write. Neither development occurs in Southeast Asia, but together they supply two templates—bilingual royal coinage and a Brahmi-derived script—that later structure Indianized polities. It is also the hinge between Mauryan collapse and the Buddhist art of Bharhut and Sanchi.

## What happened

The century opens with Mauryan fragmentation. Demetrius I of Bactria crossed the Hindu Kush around 200 BCE, and by mid-century the Indo-Greek kingdom dominated Gandhara and the Punjab. Its best-known ruler, Menander I Soter, reigned c. 165/155–130 BCE, and his coins are the period's most informative (Bopearachchi 1991). Menander is credited by tradition with converting to Buddhism; the Milindapanha, a dialogue between the king and the monk Nāgasena, is a Buddhist classic whose surviving form dates from the early centuries CE (Lamotte 1988). Whether historical or literary, the coinage shows the result: Greek legends on the obverse, Kharosthi or Brahmi on the reverse, and Indian religious symbols—elephant, bull, Bodhi tree—alongside Greek deities.

In India the Shunga dynasty, founded by Pushyamitra around 187 BCE, replaced the Mauryas and presided over cultural consolidation rather than political unity (Thapar 1961). The Bharhut stupa and the railings of Sanchi Stupa 2 belong to this horizon; both are among India's earliest narrative sculptures (Quintanilla 2007). At Besnagar around 113 BCE the Greek ambassador Heliodorus erected a Garuda pillar in honour of Vāsudeva, the first epigraphic record of a foreigner adopting a Brahmanical cult (Salomon 1998). The Hathigumpha inscription of Kharavela records a Jain king's public works and campaigns; its date is disputed (Salomon 1998).

South India carries the century's long-run significance. Tamil Brahmi, a regional adaptation of Brahmi, appears on potsherds, cave beds and trade goods from roughly the third century BCE to the first century CE (Mahadevan 2003). Arikamedu, the port identified with the Periplus' Podouke, has produced Tamil Brahmi, Rouletted Ware, amphorae and beads from the second century BCE (Mahadevan 2003).

In Southeast Asia the evidence is still artifactual. Khao Sam Kaeo, on the Kra Isthmus, was occupied c. 400–100 BCE and has yielded Indian-style beads, glass, stone-working debris and locally made pottery in Indian techniques (Bellina 2014). Ban Don Ta Phet in central Thailand has produced Indian beads and bronze vessels from the fourth to second centuries BCE (Glover 2011). Neither site has yielded an Indian inscription, temple or political institution. This is the century of the bead and crucible, not the Brahmin and king.

## Indianization evidence

Script evidence is decisive but indirect. Brahmi and Kharosthi are established by the Mauryan period; Tamil Brahmi is a southern adaptation, and the Pallava script that carried Sanskrit to Southeast Asia is fourth-century CE (Salomon 1998). It matters because it shows script used for royal display, religious dedication and trade—functions that later reappear in Southeast Asian inscriptions. It does not show that script in Southeast Asia.

Kingship is transitional. The Indo-Greek kings combine a Hellenistic royal title with Indian symbols and languages; the Shungas and Satavahanas combine Brahmanical patronage with regional power; Kharavela presents himself as a Jain ruler. These are not yet the Sanskritic devaraja, but they establish the repertoire that Southeast Asian courts would later adapt.

Cult is the most visible export. Buddhism, Vaishnavism and Jainism all produced monuments and inscriptions, and Buddhist reliquary and image traditions were crystallizing. Yet the Indian presence in Southeast Asia remains a matter of goods and techniques: beads, glass, bronze, pottery. No Indian priest, Sanskrit text or temple is attested before the early centuries CE. Law is visible only in Indian texts; Kharavela's civic provisions have no Southeast Asian counterpart.

## Debates & confidence

Menander's dates are numismatic approximations; the c. 165/155–130 BCE range is conventional (Bopearachchi 1991). The Milindapanha is a composite text and cannot be treated as a transcript (Lamotte 1988). The Heliodorus pillar is securely dated, but "Bhagabhadra" and Heliodorus' status remain debated (Salomon 1998). Tamil Brahmi chronology is contested between a third-century BCE start and a first-century BCE consolidation (Mahadevan 2003). The timeline's "early Pallava script ancestor at Arikamedu" is anachronistic: the script is Tamil Brahmi, and Pallava is fourth-century CE. Khao Sam Kaeo's "Indian" craftsmen may have been local specialists working in transferred techniques (Bellina 2014). Confidence is high for Gandhara, medium for the trade sequence, low for institutional Indianization.
