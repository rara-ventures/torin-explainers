# 13th Century CE

The thirteenth century ends the classical world. Pagan collapses, Angkor's last great builder dies and his Buddhist works are smashed by a Shaiva reaction, Champa's Sanskrit epigraphy stops, and the Mongols fight every major mainland state to a draw or worse. Out of the wreckage come Sukhothai, Majapahit and the first Islamic sultanate in Sumatra.

## What happened

At Angkor, Jayavarman VII died around 1218; Jayavarman VIII (1243–1295), a Shaivite, defaced his predecessor's Buddhism: images in the Bayon and Preah Khan were destroyed and the 3.6 m Mucalinda Buddha thrown down a well (Coedès 1968: 181, 191–192). He imprisoned Yuan envoys in 1281, bought peace with tribute in 1283, and was overthrown in 1295 by his son-in-law Indravarman III, under whom the court turned Theravada. Zhou Daguan, a Yuan diplomat, spent eleven months in the capital from August 1296 and left the only eyewitness description of Angkorian life — markets run by women, monasteries, a barefoot king (Zhou 2007).

Pagan's end was fiscal before it was military. By 1280 a third to two-thirds of Upper Burma's cultivable land had been donated tax-free to the saṅgha, stripping the crown of revenue to buy loyalty (Lieberman 2003: 119–120). Narathihapate (1256–1287) lost Ngasaunggyan in 1277, abandoned the capital in 1283–85 and was killed by a son in 1287. The Mongols probably never reached Pagan; the empire dissolved, Wareru declaring Mon independence at Martaban in January 1287.

On the mainland the Tai filled the vacuum. Sukhothai, independent of Khmer Lavo in 1238, adopted Sinhalese Theravada through Nakhon Si Thammarat; under Ramkhamhaeng (1279–1298) it controlled the upper Chao Phraya. The Ram Khamhaeng inscription of 1292 describes a paternal king, a bell hung at the gate for petitions, and a Thai script invented in 1283; it became the founding document of Thai historiography. Mangrai founded Chiang Mai in 1296.

At sea the century belongs to Java and the Mongols. Kertanegara of Singhasari sent the Pamalayu expedition against Malayu in 1275, refused Yuan tribute and mutilated an envoy in 1289. Jayakatwang of Kediri killed him in 1292; the Yuan then sent 20,000–30,000 troops and 500–1,000 ships, which took Daha on 26 April 1293 before Raden Wijaya turned on them on 26 May and founded Majapahit. Champa resisted a Yuan invasion in 1283–85 under Indravarman V, but its Sanskrit epigraphy had stopped in 1253 and no major temple was built after 1300 (Vickery 2011). In Sri Lanka, Magha sacked Polonnaruwa in 1215; the court moved to Dambadeniya in 1232.

## Indianization evidence

The clearest index is epigraphic. Cham Sanskrit inscriptions cease in 1253 and are replaced by Old Cham; Khmer Sanskrit eulogy thins to nothing after Jayavarman VII; Javanese courts keep the *kakawin* but their architecture becomes retrospective. The Sanskrit cosmopolis does not collapse — it stops being the language of new political claims.

Theravada supplies the replacement universal. Sukhothai's adoption of Sinhalese ordination via Nakhon Si Thammarat, Pagan's realignment and Angkor's post-1295 conversion form one network in which Pali, not Sanskrit, carries legitimacy. The Ram Khamhaeng inscription is the type specimen: a vernacular law-and-kingship text in a script derived from Khmer, itself Pallava-derived, with Buddhist merit as the basis of rule.

Architecture follows. Sukhothai's *wat* replaces the temple-mountain; the lotus-bud finial, stucco Buddha and ordination hall are Indic forms reorganised around monastic rather than royal-cosmological use. Champa's last *kalan* towers repeat the Śaiva formula without new Sanskrit foundations.

Majapahit is the exception: it keeps the *candi* and the Hindu-Buddhist state cult, and only at the northern Sumatran ports does a post-Indic religion, Islam, establish itself with the 1297 tomb of Malik al-Ṣāliḥ.

## Debates & confidence

The Ram Khamhaeng inscription is the field's most famous authenticity dispute. Vickery's 1987 "Piltdown Skull" paper and Piriya Krairiksh's case for forgery by Mongkut were rebutted by a 1990 electron-microscopy study finding the stele consistent with other Sukhothai inscriptions; the debate is unresolved, though most specialists accept partial authenticity (Chamberlain 1991).

Pagan's fall is contested in causation, not date: the chronicles blame the *Taruk*, Lieberman and Aung-Thwin emphasise fiscal exhaustion, and recent work suggests Mongol armies never reached the capital. The 1293 expedition's size rests on the *Yuan Shi*, whose figures are unreliable. Zhou Daguan's text survives as perhaps a third of the original.
