# 14th Century CE

The fourteenth century is Majapahit's imperial generation and the point at which the classical Sanskritic order begins to thin at its edges. Java produces its greatest Sanskritic court poem while Sinhala Theravada, carried through Mon Martaban, reorganises the mainland. Champa briefly becomes the aggressor against Dai Viet, and Islam takes institutional root in the Straits.

## What happened

Majapahit entered the century as the largest polity in the archipelago and left it sliding into civil war. Tribhuwana Wijayatunggadewi appointed Gajah Mada *mahāpatih* in 1336; the *Pararaton* credits him with the Palapa oath, a vow to forgo *palapa* — pleasure, or rest — until the archipelago was subdued. Under Hayam Wuruk (r. 1350–1389) Trowulan became a brick capital of canals, markets, Śaiva and Buddhist precincts and a deified-ancestor cult. In 1357 the Sundanese royal party was massacred at Bubat after a marriage negotiation collapsed, an episode the court poem suppresses and the *Kidung Sunda* preserves. Prapanca's *Deśavarṇana*, completed in 1365, describes the capital's ceremonial calendar and lists ninety-eight tributary names from Sumatra to New Guinea; it is a eulogy, not a cadastre (Robson 1995; Pigeaud 1960–63).

On the mainland the decisive change was the consolidation of Sinhala Theravada. Sukhothai's Li Thai composed the *Traiphum Phra Ruang* around 1345, turning Buddhist cosmology into political doctrine. Mon Martaban became the relay through which the Lankan Mahāvihāra ordination reached the Tai: Sumana Thera returned with Mon monks and installed the Sihala sect at Wat Pa Mamuang, dated 1331 in some reckonings and 1361 in the *Jinakālamālī* (Jayawickrama 1968). Ayutthaya, founded in 1351 at a site named for Ayodhya, fused this Theravada with Khmer Brahminical ritual: Ramathibodi I's Thammasat recast the Dharmaśāstra as Siamese law, while Brahmin priests continued to consecrate kings (Baker & Phongpaichit 2017). At Angkor the Sanskrit epigraphic habit fades and Theravada becomes the court religion, though Brahmins remained for *devarāja* rites.

Champa reversed the usual direction of pressure. Chế Bồng Nga (r. 1360–1390) sacked Thăng Long in 1371, killed the Trần ruler in a campaign against Vijaya in 1377, and was killed himself in 1390. Pasai was an established Islamic court by Ibn Baṭṭūṭa's visit of 1345, and Temasek on Singapore hill supported a cosmopolitan settlement whose ceramics and Indian carnelian run through the century (Miksic 2013).

Bali was drawn formally into the Majapahit system after Gajah Mada's 1343 campaign installed Śrī Aji Kresna Kepakisan at Samprangan, imposing the Javanese Siwa–Buddha dyarchy on an island that already had Sanskritic epigraphy (Coedès 1968).

## Indianization evidence

The century's Indian-derived material is mostly *reused*, not newly imported, which is what makes it diagnostic. Majapahit charters still open with Sanskrit invocations and curse formulas, still grant *sīma* estates on the Indian *agrahāra* model, and still title the ruler *śrī mahārāja* and *-wardhana*; but the working language of law and administration is Old Javanese (Pigeaud 1960–63). The state cult is a Javanese compound, Siwa–Buddha, with twin Śaiva and Buddhist clergy — a configuration with no Indian precedent. The *Nagarakretagama* is a Sanskrit *kāvya* form filled with Javanese topography (Robson 1995).

On the mainland the vector reverses: Indian authority arrives at second hand, as Lankan Pali Buddhism transmitted by Mon monks. That the Tai took ordination from Sri Lanka rather than India is the clearest evidence that Indianization was never a single pipeline. Ayutthaya's Brahminical coronation and Dharmaśāstra-derived law are Indian in origin but Khmer in transmission (Baker & Phongpaichit 2017). In Champa, Sanskrit epigraphy and Śaiva patronage continue while the political vocabulary absorbs Vietnamese tributary idiom.

## Debates & confidence

The Palapa oath is the century's most over-read document. It survives only in the *Pararaton*, a late compilation; its date floats between 1334 and 1336; and even *amukti palapa* is disputed between "enjoy pleasure" and "take rest." Confidence in the oath as an event is low; in Majapahit's naval reach, high. The *Nagarakretagama*'s ninety-eight names are eulogistic, and Sastrawan (2020) argues direct administration barely extended beyond core Java. The Sumana date rests on a single chronicle tradition, and confidence there is low. The Ramkhamhaeng stele is contested. Angkor's fourteenth-century "conversion" is inferred from iconography and later Pali texts, not from a dated decree.
