# 8th Century CE

The eighth century is the moment Indian-derived sacred architecture becomes territorial. In central Java the Sailendra kings built the Tārā temple at Kalasan (778), the Mañjuśrī sanctuary at Kelurak (782), and began Borobudur; on the Khmer plain Jayavarman II consecrated the devaraja on Phnom Kulen in 802. In Champa the Kauthara sanctuary was rebuilt after a raid, and on the Malay Peninsula a Sailendra inscription at Ligor shows the network reaching the isthmus.

## What happened

Java's temple programme opens with Sanjaya's Śivaliṅga at Canggal (732), but the decisive turn is Buddhist. The Kalasan inscription, dated 700 Śaka (778), records that the king's *guru*—"jewel of the Śailendra family"—persuaded Mahārāja Panangkaran to build the Tārābhavana for the goddess Tārā and a *vihāra* for the saṅgha, endowing the village of Kalasa to maintain it. It is the first dated use of the dynastic name. The Kelurak inscription (704 Śaka = 782) records a Mañjuśrī sanctuary, generally identified as Candi Sewu, built for King Indra; it equates Buddha, Dharma, and Saṅgha with Brahmā, Viṣṇu, and Maheśvara, a Vajrayāna move, and names a teacher from Gauḍa (Bengal), conventionally Kumāraghoṣa (Coedès 1968; Jordaan 1998).

Borobudur was begun around 780 and built over five decades. It is a stepped pyramid on a natural hill: six square platforms, three circular ones, 2,672 relief panels, 504 Buddha images, and 72 perforated stupas around the summit. Its plan is a Mahāyāna mandala, but its terraces continue the Javanese *punden berundak* ancestor-mound form, and its reliefs were carved by local workshops from imported texts (Soekmono 1976; Miksic 1990). At Ligor, on the Kra isthmus, the Kawi face of the stele (775) names a Śailendra mahārāja, evidence that the dynasty's authority reached the peninsula ports.

On the mainland, Chenla divided into Land and Water Chenla after 706/707. Jayavarman II, a prince possibly raised at the Javanese court, moved through the Mekong principalities and in 802 was consecrated on Phnom Kulen (Mahendraparvata) by the Brahmin Hiraṇyadāma. The rite established the *kamrateṅ jagat ta rāja*, "the lord of the world who is king". The only detailed source is the Sdok Kak Thom inscription (K.235), carved in 1053 by a priestly family pressing its hereditary claim, and its statement that Jayavarman "came from Java" is disputed (Kulke 1978; Griffiths 2013).

In Champa, a raid traditionally dated 774 carried off the jewelled mukhaliṅga of Po Nagar. Satyavarman restored it in 784, and the goddess Bhagavatī began to eclipse the liṅga as the sanctuary's principal cult. The episode is recorded in C.38, the earliest substantial epigraphy from Kauthara, and it shows a Cham polity rich enough to attract maritime raiders and organized enough to rebuild.

## Indianization evidence

Java in this century is the strongest case against a single "Pallava script" model. The Kalasan and Kelurak charters are in Prāṇāgarī, a northern Indian script, not the Pallava-derived script of Sumatra and the peninsula; the Bengal connection is explicit in the Gauda teacher and in the Tārā and Mañjuśrī cults (Jordaan 1998). The vector is institutional: a *guru* persuades the king, the king endows land, the saṅgha maintains the foundation. Buddhism arrives as a network of masters with texts and consecration rights, not as a colonial administration.

The localization is equally visible. Borobudur is a mandala in plan but a Javanese ancestor-mound in section, sited between volcanoes and rice fields. The Khmer *kamrateṅ jagat ta rāja* is not the Sanskrit *devaraja* of Coedès's reading but a territorial guardian spirit, tied to local *neak ta* and nāga ancestry (Kulke 1978). In Champa, the imported mukhaliṅga is paired with the indigenous goddess Bhagavatī, who eventually displaces it.

## Debates & confidence

Borobudur's dates are inferred, not attested: no foundation charter survives, and the c. 780–833 span rests on carving-style comparison and on identifying the 824 *Jinalaya* with the monument. Kalasan is dated 778 in most editions but 779 in some. The "Java" of K.235 may be the island, the Malay Peninsula, or Kedah; Griffiths (2013) argues for Java, while Suzuki and Higham prefer a peninsula location. Kumāraghoṣa's name and Bengal origin rest on a damaged inscription. Po Nagar's raid is dated 774 in the Cham text but the stele is 781, and the restoration 784. Chenla's division into Land and Water is a Chinese administrative label, not a witnessed event.
