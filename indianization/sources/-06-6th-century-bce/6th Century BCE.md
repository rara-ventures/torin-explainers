# 6th Century BCE (600–501 BCE)

The sixth century BCE is the deepest layer of the Indianization story, and almost none of it is Southeast Asian. Achaemenid Persia annexed the Indus valley and made Gandhara a satrapy, opening the corridor through which Aramaic administration and an Indic script later moved east. In the Ganges plain it saw the second urbanization and the careers of the Buddha and Mahavira; in Southeast Asia the record is entirely local.

## What happened

The political frame was Persian. Cyrus the Great began the absorption of the northwest around 535 BCE, and Darius I completed it around 518 BCE, pushing to the Jhelum (Briant 2002). The conquered lands became the satrapies of Gandāra, Hindush and Sattagydia. Darius commemorated the order at Behistun, where Gadāra appears among the empire's lands (Kent 1953). Herodotus places the Gandarians and their neighbours in the seventh tax district and lists Indian and Gandharian contingents in Xerxes' army (Herodotus 1998). Taxila, the largest city of the region, is conventionally treated as the satrapal seat, though the identification is inferential (Marshall 1951).

Administration mattered more than battles. Achaemenid chanceries used Aramaic, and Aramaic was current in the northwest: a fourth-century BCE inscription was found at Sirkap, and Kharosthi is generally derived from Aramaic with extensive modification (Salomon 1998). No intermediate forms survive, but the relationship is accepted. Kharosthi became the script of Gandharan Buddhism and of Ashoka's northwestern edicts. It is not the script that later reached Southeast Asia, but it establishes that script travels with empire and religion.

In India the century saw the second urbanization. The Ganges plain filled with fortified cities, Northern Black Polished Ware, iron tools and punch-marked coins. The sixteen mahajanapadas competed, and Magadha under Bimbisara and Ajatashatru began the expansion that produced the Mauryan state two centuries later (Coningham & Young 2015). The Buddha taught in this setting; traditional dates place his death around 483 BCE, a later chronology around 400 BCE (Bechert 1991). Mahavira and the Jain community belong to the same horizon.

Southeast Asia was not empty, but it was not Indianized. The Dong Son culture of the Red River and the Sa Huynh culture of central Vietnam were bronze-and-iron societies with long-distance maritime links (Bellwood 1997; Higham 2014). No Indic script, Sanskrit inscription, Indian cult image or settlement of this date is known. The claim that iron-working at Sungai Batu in Kedah began around 535 BCE rests on a single early charcoal outlier; the Bayesian model and brick structures point to the second to tenth centuries CE (Abd Halim, Mokhtar & Masnan 2023). The corridor is open; the cargo has not arrived.

## Indianization evidence

Script is the clearest trace. Kharosthi's dependence on Aramaic is a case of adaptation rather than wholesale import; vowel order, direction and letter forms point west (Salomon 1998). But this is a Gandharan phenomenon. It does not appear in Southeast Asia, and it is not the script later Indianized polities used. The scripts that reached Champa, Funan and Java were Brahmi-derived, transmitted through Pallava and Gupta worlds from the fourth century CE.

Kingship offers two models. The Achaemenid satrapy is an imperial, tribute-taking structure; the mahajanapada is a competitive, fortified, tax-and-army state. Neither is the devaraja of later Southeast Asia, but both establish that a ruler can universalize authority through a sacred order. The Buddha's teaching, by contrast, offered a trans-local community, the sangha, that could travel without an army.

Cult and law remain Indian-local. Vedic sacrifice, Buddhist monastic discipline and Jain asceticism flourished, but none has left an archaeological trace in Southeast Asia before the first millennium CE. The absence is the evidence: this is a century of Indian consolidation, not Indian expansion.

## Debates & confidence

The date of the Buddha is the largest uncertainty. The traditional 563–483 BCE chronology is still widely repeated, but the Bechert symposium and much subsequent scholarship favour a death around 400 BCE (Bechert 1991). The Kharosthi-Aramaic derivation is accepted in outline but undocumented in its intermediate stages (Salomon 1998). Taxila's status as an Achaemenid satrapal capital is an inference from size and location, not a documented fact (Marshall 1951). The Sungai Batu early date is a low-confidence outlier (Abd Halim, Mokhtar & Masnan 2023). Confidence is high for the Indian and Persian narrative, very low for any Southeast Asian Indianization this early.
