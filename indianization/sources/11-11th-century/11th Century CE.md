# 11th Century CE

The eleventh century is the imperial turn. Angkor becomes a bureaucratic state with a written oath of allegiance and its largest reservoir; Pagan converts to Theravada and imports a complete Pali canon; and Rajendra Chola's fleet sacks Srivijaya's ports in 1025 — the only Indian naval expedition ever mounted against Southeast Asia. Afterwards its Indic states are rival centres of orthodoxy, not borrowers.

## What happened

Suryavarman I won Angkor in a decade of civil war and ruled 1002–1050 as "King of the Just Laws." He assembled some four thousand provincial officials to swear allegiance in the palace, was the first Khmer ruler to wall his residence, and began the West Baray — 8 km by 2.1 km, the largest Khmer reservoir to survive (Higham 2014: 371). A Mahayana Buddhist tolerant of Shaivism, he sent a chariot to Rajendra Chola around 1012 and extended Khmer authority to Lopburi and the Menam basin (Hall 1975). His successors faced Cham attacks and internal revolt.

The hinge is maritime. Rajendra I raided Srivijayan ports in 1017 and again in 1025, reaching Palembang, capturing King Sangrama Vijayottunggavarman and removing the jewelled *vidyādhara* gate listed in the Tanjavur inscription (Kulke et al. 2009). The fleet apparently came through the Sunda Strait rather than the Malacca Strait, striking from the south while Srivijaya's navy watched the north. No occupation followed, but the *maṇḍala* never recovered: Chinese missions from "Sanfoqi" after 1028 probably refer to Malayu–Jambi, and the last epigraphic mention of Srivijaya is Chola (Miksic & Goh 2017).

In Burma, Anawrahta's conversion by the Mon monk Shin Arahan in 1056 turned a Kyaukse principality into a Theravada empire. His siege of Thaton ended on 17 May 1057; tradition says he took thirty sets of the Pali canon on thirty-two white elephants and deported the Mon court, whose craftsmen built Pagan's first great monuments (Htin Aung 1967: 33–37). Kyansittha (1084–1112) completed the Shwezigon and built the Ananda in 1105.

Champa lived under Vietnamese pressure: Ly Thai Tong killed Jaya Simhavarman II in 1044, and in 1069 Ly Thanh Tong captured Rudravarman III and annexed three northern districts. Harivarman IV (1074–1080), son of a Coconut-clan father and an Areca-clan mother, reunified the country and restored Po Nagar (Maspero 2002: 61). In Java, Airlangga reunified the Mataram heartland from 1019, founded Kahuripan, endowed a monastery for his Srivijayan queen in 1035, and in 1042 or 1045 split the realm between his sons.

## Indianization evidence

The Sdok Kak Thom inscription is the single most important document of Khmer religion: it traces a family of *purohita* across two centuries, records the *devarāja* ritual instituted by Jayavarman II, and lists the mantras and regalia involved (Coedès 1968: 98–101). Indianization appears here as a hereditary profession — priesthood transmitted within families, not imported per reign.

Pagan shows the same process with a different vector. Sanskrit declines after Anawrahta's conversion; Pali becomes the liturgical language and the Mon script is adapted for Burmese. The Ananda's plan is Indian — sanctum and circumambulatory passages recall Bengal and Odisha — but its fourfold Buddha programme is a local doctrinal statement, and its builder is remembered as *cakravartin* and bodhisattva (Schober 2002).

Champa's Harivarman IV is the clearest case of double descent: Sanskrit royal titulature on one side, Coconut and Areca clan mothers on the other. In Java, Airlangga's Calcutta Stone is Sanskrit *prasasti* in form but Old Javanese in political logic, and his partition invokes *rājadharma* while solving a local succession problem.

## Debates & confidence

The 1025 expedition is secure; its motive is not. Sastri (1935) proposed a trade war against Srivijayan obstruction of Chola–China shipping, Kulke a *digvijaya* for prestige, and Hall a diplomatic chain in which Suryavarman I drew Rajendra into his quarrel with Tambralinga. The Sunda Strait route is inferred, not stated.

Thaton is the largest historiographical fault line. Aung-Thwin (2005) argues the conquest is a post-Pagan legend with no contemporary evidence; most Burmese historians still accept the chronicle account. Ananda is now dated 1105, though older literature gives 1091, and the Baphuon has been radiocarbon-dated to Suryavarman I (Leroy et al. 2015).
