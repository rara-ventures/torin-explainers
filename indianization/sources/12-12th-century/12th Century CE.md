# 12th Century CE

The twelfth century builds the two poles of Khmer religion: Angkor Wat, the largest Vishnuite temple ever constructed, and the Bayon, the last Mahayana state temple at Angkor. Between them the Cham sack the Khmer capital and are conquered in return. Meanwhile Pagan and Polonnaruwa align their monastic lineages with the Mahāvihāra, producing the Sinhalese Theravada that will reach Sukhothai and Lan Na.

## What happened

Suryavarman II (r. 1113–1150) made Vishnu, not Shiva, the focus of the royal cult and commissioned a state temple on 162.6 hectares, moated by a 190 m channel and crowned by a quincunx rising 65 m — Angkor Wat (Freeman & Jacques 2006: 48–50). Its outer gallery carries the Mahabharata and Ramayana wars, the Churning of the Ocean, the king's procession and the 32 hells and 37 heavens; 1,860 *devatā* line its walls. Excavation shows the enclosure also housed 3,000–4,300 residents (Stark et al. 2015). Divākarapaṇḍita, who had already crowned two kings, presided over the consecration.

Suryavarman's foreign policy was aggressive and unsuccessful. Three campaigns against Đại Việt (1128, 1132, 1137) failed; in 1145 he sacked Vijaya and installed Harideva, a brother of his queen, only for Jaya Harivarman I to retake the capital and kill him. In 1177 Jaya Indravarman IV reversed the equation: a Cham fleet went up the Mekong and across the Tonlé Sap to sack Yaśodharapura and kill Tribhuvanādityavarman. Jayavarman VII drove the Cham out, was crowned in 1181, and by 1191 had made Champa a Khmer dependency (Coedès 1968: 169–170).

His building programme was a welfare state in stone: Ta Prohm for his mother in 1186 with 80,000 attendants and 615 dancers; Preah Khan for his father in 1191 with 97,840; 102 hospitals, 121 rest-houses, and Buddha images in 23 towns (Higham 2001: 125–129; Woodward & Douglas 1994). The Bayon is a three-tiered mandala of face-towers — 49 originally, 37 surviving, roughly 200 faces — around a 43 m tower that held a 3.6 m Buddha shielded by Mucalinda.

Elsewhere the century is a monastic realignment. Parakramabahu I (1153–1186) reunified Sri Lanka, purified the saṅgha under Mahāvihāra discipline, built the Parakrama Samudra and Gal Vihara, and fought a punitive war with Pagan in 1165. At Pagan, Alaungsithu raised the Thatbyinnyu (c. 1150, 66 m) and Narathu the Dhammayangyi; in 1180 the primate Shin Uttarajīva led a mission to Ceylon and realigned Burmese ordination with the Mahāvihāra. In Java, Kediri's Jayabhaya (1135–1159) took Janggala and commissioned the *Bharatayuddha*, finished on 6 November 1157.

## Indianization evidence

Angkor Wat is the most complete surviving statement of Sanskritic royal theology. Its five towers are Meru, its moat the ocean, its sanctuary the palace of a god who is also the king's posthumous identity — Paramaviṣṇuloka. The reliefs run counter-clockwise (*prasavya*), the direction of Brahminical funerary ritual, which is why most scholars read the temple as Suryavarman's mausoleum (Coedès 1968: 162).

The Cham wars show Indianization as a shared grammar rather than a foreign veneer: both sides claim *cakravartin* status, both inscribe in Sanskrit, both build *kalan* towers to the same Śaiva pantheon. The Bayon's naval relief translates a historical battle into Indic visual narrative.

The century's decisive transfer, though, is monastic. The Mahāvihāra ordination brought back to Pagan by Uttarajīva and his pupil Chapata created a lineage that could travel: within a century it would supply Sukhothai, Martaban and Angkor with monastic authority. The Kalyani inscription of 1472 preserves the reform's lineage history (Sirisena 1978). In Java, the *Bharatayuddha* turns the Mahabharata into dynastic allegory — Kurukshetra standing for Kediri's absorption of Janggala.

## Debates & confidence

The Bayon's faces are the century's most durable argument: Jayavarman VII himself, the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara, or Brahma's four faces. The count is unstable — 216 is traditional, roughly 200 is defensible, and towers fell or were added, so 49 became 37. The 1177 sack is accepted in most accounts but questioned by Vickery, who distrusts the Chinese sources behind it.

Angkor Wat's orientation has been read as funerary, as Vishnu's western direction, and as an astronomical instrument; Mannikka's readings have had a mixed reception. Construction is dated 1113–1150 in most literature, 1122–1150 in others. Chapata's return is 1181 in the Kalyani tradition, after Uttarajīva's death in 1191 in the Burmese chronicles.
