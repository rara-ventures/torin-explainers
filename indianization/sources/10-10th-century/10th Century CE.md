# 10th Century CE

The tenth century is when the Sanskritic state stops being an import and becomes an inheritance. Yasovarman I fixes the Khmer capital at Yasodharapura for centuries, while Srivijaya and Java fight a trade war that draws the Chola navy into Southeast Asian waters. Its most durable export is a monastic network: the Sumatran scholarship that trains Atiśa.

## What happened

Yasovarman I (r. 889–910) abandoned Hariharalaya, built a five-towered quincunx on the hill of Bakheng, and dug the Yasodharatataka — the East Baray — with dykes 7.5 km by 1.8 km (Higham 2001: 64–65). He endowed roughly a hundred *āśrama*, a capillary network carrying court religion into the provinces (Coedès 1968: 111–112). After the Koh Ker interlude of Jayavarman IV (928–944), Rajendravarman II returned the court to Angkor and commissioned the East Mebon and Pre Rup (961/962). His minister Yajnavaraha, tutor to the young Jayavarman V, consecrated Banteay Srei on 22 April 967 — the only major Angkor temple built by a courtier rather than a king (Freeman & Jacques 2006: 206). Jayavarman V began Ta Keo in 975; it was never finished.

Champa's century turns on a single object. A stele of 918 records Indravarman III ordering a golden Bhagavatī at Po Nagar; in 950 a Khmer army carried it off, and in 965 Jaya Indravarman I replaced it with the 1.2 m stone goddess still worshipped at Nha Trang (Coedès 1968: 124; Ngô 2006: 192). The Vietnamese sack of Indrapura in 982 then broke the northern principality's primacy, and political weight drifted south toward Vijaya.

In the archipelago the century is a war of position. Mpu Sindok moved the Mataram court from Central to East Java in 929 — later tradition blames Merapi, though Srivijayan pressure is equally plausible — founding the Ishana dynasty at Watugaluh. Under his patronage the *Kakawin Rāmāyaṇa* and *Sanghyang Kamahāyānikan* turned Sanskrit epic and tantric doctrine into Old Javanese court literature (Coedès 1968: 128–129). The Anjukladang inscription of 937 records the repulse of a Malayu raid; in 990 Dharmawangsa launched a fleet against Palembang and failed (Miksic & Goh 2017). A Srivijayan envoy stranded at the Song court in 999 was still begging protection from Java two years later.

Sri Lanka supplies the sharpest rupture. Rajaraja I annexed Rajarata in 993, sacked Anuradhapura and made Polonnaruwa a Chola provincial capital (Sirisena 1978). Pagan remained a modest Kyaukse principality; its king-list is chronicle tradition, not contemporary epigraphy.

## Indianization evidence

The mechanism runs at two speeds. In the Khmer and Cham courts Sanskrit remains the epigraphic language and the *varman* name-system universal, but inscription content shifts from genealogical boast to administrative detail: land boundaries, temple servants, lamp oil, the oaths of 4,000 officials. This is a bureaucracy using Indian legal forms rather than admiring them.

Architecturally the borrowing is structural, not ornamental. Bakheng's quincunx, moat and axial stairways reproduce the Meru-and-ocean cosmogram; Pre Rup and Ta Keo repeat the grammar in local materials and proportions. The *devarāja* cult is maintained by a hereditary Brahmin priesthood — Yasovarman's teacher Vāmaśiva stood in a lineage linked to Śaṅkara's circle (Coedès 1968: 111).

Champa offers the cleanest single-object test. The same goddess is Bhagavatī in Sanskrit and Yan Po Nagar in Cham; the same temple absorbs Śaiva, Buddhist and later Vietnamese devotion without rupture. Indianization here is a vocabulary, not a replacement. Java runs the other way: the *Kakawin Rāmāyaṇa* naturalises Indian epic into Old Javanese metre. Sumatra's contribution is institutional — Muaro Jambi's monastic complex, the Dharmakīrtiśrī school, and Atiśa's twelve years before 1025 (Chattopadhyaya 1981: 91).

## Debates & confidence

Two structural arguments remain open. First, whether the barays were irrigation, ritual water or both: Groslier's "hydraulic city" has been qualified by work showing the reservoirs could not guarantee a dry-season rice crop alone. Second, the Po Nagar sack is dated 950 by Coedès, 945–946 in some Cham chronologies.

Atiśa's Sumatra chronology is uncertain. Tibetan sources say twelve years and a return in 1025, implying arrival c. 1012–1013; the older 991–1011 window is not supported by the *Blue Annals* or Chattopadhyaya. Dharmawangsa's 990 campaign is accepted, but the Srivijayan-sponsored revolt that destroyed Watugaluh is reconstructed from later Javanese tradition. Confidence in the Khmer royal sequence is high.
