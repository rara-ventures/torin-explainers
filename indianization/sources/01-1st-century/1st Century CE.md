# 1st Century CE

The first century CE is conventionally the start of Phase 1 Contact: the Mekong Delta enters the Chinese record, Indian goods appear in quantity at Oc Eo, and the Kaundinya–Soma legend supplies Southeast Asia's first Indian-derived foundation myth. It is also the century of the mature Gandharan Buddha, the Kushan take-off and the peak of Indo-Roman shipping. Evidence for actual Indian institutions in Southeast Asia remains thin; the century is contact, not transplant.

## What happened

In the Mekong Delta a settlement system that would become Funan took shape. Oc Eo was a port with canals, pile-built structures, workshops and a hinterland reaching to Angkor Borei in Cambodia (Malleret 1959; Stark 2007). Its imports included Indian carnelian and agate beads, glass, seals and gold ornaments. The Chinese record is the earliest textual fix: the Hou Hanshu notes a Funan contact in 84 CE, and later histories preserve the legend of the Brahmin Kaundinya, who married the nāga princess Somā and ruled her kingdom (Pelliot 1903; Coedès 1968). Whether Kaundinya was a historical figure or a literary charter is unresolved. The legend makes Indian descent and Brahmanical ritual the source of royal legitimacy.

In Gandhara the century saw political reconfiguration and artistic crystallization. The Kushan confederation consolidated under Kujula Kadphises, and the Indo-Parthian Gondophares ruled c. 19–46 CE; the Takht-i-Bahi inscription is associated with him (Salomon 1998). Gandharan sculpture developed its synthesis of Hellenistic modelling and Buddhist iconography, and the Bimaran casket, a gold reliquary from eastern Afghanistan, usually dated to this century, carries one of the earliest Buddha images (Zwalf 1996).

The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, written in the first century CE, describes the routes from Egypt to the Red Sea, Gujarat, the Malabar coast and the Bay of Bengal, and names its goods and ports (Casson 1989). Arikamedu, Muziris and the Sri Lankan ports were part of the same circuit. Roman amphorae, glass and coins increase; Pliny the Elder complained about the drain of coin to India, and a Taprobane embassy reached Claudius around 50 CE (Pliny 1942). Its scale is debated; its regularity is not (Tomber 2008; Ray 1989).

Dong Son and Sa Huynh continued, and the Kra ports were reorganized after Khao Sam Kaeo's decline. In central Vietnam the Chinese record of Lâm Ấp begins in 192 CE, just after this century. In Kedah, Sungai Batu's brick structure is dated c. 110 CE, but contested. The routes, goods and legends are in place; the first Sanskrit inscription in Southeast Asia is still two or three centuries away.

## Indianization evidence

Script is the weakest link in Southeast Asia. Kharosthi and Brahmi were fully developed, and Gandhara was producing Buddhist manuscripts in Kharosthi by the first century CE (Salomon 1998). But no Southeast Asian inscription in an Indic script predates the third or fourth century CE. The Pallava script, the vehicle of the earliest Sanskrit inscriptions in the region, belongs to the fourth century.

The Kaundinya–Soma story presents Indian brahminical authority marrying local territorial authority; the resulting king rules through a combined genealogy. This is the classic Indianization formula, but it is preserved in Chinese and later Khmer texts, not in a contemporary inscription (Coedès 1968). The Funanese kings named in Chinese annals—Hun-t'ien, Hun-p'an-huang, Fan Shih-man—are known only through that filter.

Indian beads, seals and metalwork at Oc Eo imply Indian merchants, and Buddhist material becomes clearer in the second and third centuries. No temple, Indian deity image or monastic foundation is securely dated to the first century. Law is invisible: no Sanskrit legal text, dharmaśāstra or Indian-style court document survives this early.

## Debates & confidence

Funan's foundation date is a convention; the Chinese first record is 84 CE, but archaeology shows earlier settlement (Pelliot 1903; Stark 2007). Kaundinya's historicity is unknowable; the legend may be a local charter, not a memory of an Indian voyage (Coedès 1968). Oc Eo's chronology has been revised since Malleret; its earliest occupation may be second century BCE, not first century CE (Malleret 1959; Stark 2007). The timeline's "c. 68 CE Fan Shiman" is a misdating: the Liangshu's Fan Shih-man belongs to the third century, c. 205–225 CE. The Bimaran casket's date and Buddha identification are disputed. Confidence is high for the Roman trade and Gandharan sequence, medium for Oc Eo's chronology, low for the Kaundinya legend as history.
