# 9th Century CE

The ninth century is the classical synthesis. Borobudur reached completion, Prambanan answered it across the plain, a Javanese-descended king endowed a monastery at Nalanda, and the Khmer moved their capital to Yasodharapura. In Champa a Mahāyāna dynasty built the largest Buddhist foundation in the country's history at Đồng Dương. The century shows Indian institutions operating at imperial scale—and being reshaped by local landscapes and dynastic rivalries.

## What happened

In Java, Samaratungga's reign produced the charters that anchor Borobudur's chronology. The Karangtengah inscription (824) is bilingual—Sanskrit for doctrine, Old Javanese for the land grant—and records that his daughter Pramodhawardhani consecrated a *Jinalaya*, generally identified as Borobudur; a *Venuvana* received the ashes of King Indra. The Tri Tepusan inscription (842) endows tax-free land to maintain a *kamūlān* called Bhūmisambhāra, probably the monument's original name (Soekmono 1976; de Casparis 1956). Borobudur was finished around 825, on most estimates, though some place the final stage nearer 833.

Samaratungga married his daughter to the Sanjaya lord Rakai Pikatan. The alliance failed. The Shivagrha inscription, issued in 856 by Pikatan's successor, records the defeat of a rebel fortified at Ratu Boko and the dedication of the Śivagrha—Prambanan—whose 47-metre Śiva temple was inaugurated on 12 November 856. The complex is the largest Hindu temple site in Indonesia: 240 structures, a Rāmāyaṇa gallery, and a plan modelling Mount Meru. Whether it was a dynastic counter-monument to Borobudur or simply a change of royal patronage is still argued (Jordaan 1993).

The defeated prince is conventionally Balaputra, who withdrew to Sumatra. The Nalanda copper plate of Devapaladeva, dated to the king's thirty-ninth regnal year (c. 860), styles him king of Suvarṇadvīpa, grandson of a Śailendra king of Java, and records his endowment of five villages for a monastery at Nalanda (Shastri 1924). It is the strongest single document of a two-way Buddhist circuit linking Java, Sumatra, and Pala Bengal.

In Cambodia, Indravarman I (r. 877–889) built Preah Ko (879) for his ancestors and Bakong (881), the first fully developed Khmer temple-mountain: a five-tier sandstone pyramid representing Meru, ringed by eight brick towers. His son Yasovarman I (r. 889–910) moved the capital to Yasodharapura, centred on Phnom Bakheng, and dug the East Baray, a reservoir 7.5 by 1.8 kilometres. He also founded about a hundred *āśramas* across the kingdom.

In Champa, Indravarman II (r. 854–893) founded Indrapura near Đồng Dương and in 875 dedicated the Mahāyāna monastery of Laksmindralokeśvara, recorded in C.66. The foundation, with its royal cult of Avalokiteśvara and strong Vajrayāna elements, made Buddhism the court religion for a century, until the Vietnamese sack of 982. In 889 Yasovarman I invaded Champa and was repelled.

## Indianization evidence

The century's evidence is monumental and institutional rather than linguistic. Borobudur, Prambanan, Bakong, and Phnom Bakheng are all attempts to build Mount Meru in local stone, and all four are oriented, terraced, and sited by cosmology. The charters that fund them use Sanskrit for doctrine and Old Javanese or Old Khmer for land, witnesses, and taxes—a bilingual division of labour that is itself the signature of localization (Wolters 1999).

The Nalanda grant shows the network operating in reverse. A king of Suvarṇadvīpa endows an Indian monastery, and the Pala ruler grants the revenue of five villages; monks, not armies, carry the relationship. In Champa, the Đồng Dương foundation imports Pala-style Vajrayāna and Avalokiteśvara devotion but installs them in a Cham royal cult with a Cham posthumous name, Paramabuddhaloka. In Cambodia, the *devaraja* is fused with ancestor worship: Preah Ko's six towers house the deified parents and grandparents of the king.

## Debates & confidence

Balaputra's parentage is unresolved: he is variously Samaratungga's son, brother, or nephew, and his "exile" may be a later construction (de Casparis 1956). The Nalanda plate is genuine, but its date depends on Devapala's regnal chronology. Borobudur's completion is inferred from the 824 and 842 charters, not attested. The dual-dynasty model behind Prambanan—Buddhist Śailendra versus Śaiva Sanjaya—is the field's oldest quarrel; Jordaan (1993) and others read the 856 war as political rather than religious. Đồng Dương's "Buddhist island" is partly an art-historical construct, since Śaiva material continues alongside it. Yasovarman's hydraulic city is now read as flood control and cosmology rather than irrigation.
