# 2nd Century CE

The second century is the century of the entrepôt. Roman demand for pepper, aromatics and silk pulled Indian Ocean shipping through the Strait of Malacca into the South China Sea, and the Mekong delta port of Óc Eo became a canalised city of warehouses, workshops and resident artisans. Yet its most famous Indian document, the Võ Cạnh stele, may not belong to the century at all.

## What happened

The monsoon calendar dictated everything. Ships left the Red Sea or the Coromandel coast with the southwest monsoon and returned with the northeast, making a few transshipment points rich: the Kra Isthmus portages, the Kedah coast, the Mekong delta. The *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* already describes this routing, and Ptolemy's *Geography*, compiled around 150, fixes the names Europe would use for a millennium — the Golden Chersonese for the Malay Peninsula, Cattigara for the great port beyond it (Wheatley 1961; Hanus & Smagur 2020).

Óc Eo is the archaeological correlate. Malleret's excavations from 1942 uncovered a 450-hectare site cut by a longitudinal canal and four transverse canals, with pile-supported houses and a canal running 68 km north to Angkor Borei (Malleret 1962; Stark 2007). The imports are the point: Roman gold medallions of Antoninus Pius and possibly Marcus Aurelius, intaglios, glass, and Indian seals and beads. Some Roman gold was reworked locally into bracteates — imperial imagery converted into an amulet, not currency (Malleret 1962; Borell 2008). Funan also minted silver bearing a hamsa.

Politically the delta was a cluster of walled centres that Chinese writers lumped together as Funan. The *Hou Hanshu* records embassies to the Later Han from 84 CE, and the *Liang shu*, compiled in 636, preserves a king-list beginning with the Brahmin Hùntián and the nāga princess Somā — a myth attested in Sanskrit only in 658 (Pelliot 1903; Coedès 1968). In 192 a Cham leader the Vietnamese call Khu Liên rebelled against Han rule at Xianglin, south of the Hải Vân Pass, founding the polity the Chinese called Linyi: Lâm Ấp, the seed of Champa (Taylor 1983; Vickery 1998).

In India the centre of gravity was Amaravati, where the Sātavāhana stūpa received its limestone casing and narrative reliefs. The Ikṣvāku kings who made Sanskrit the epigraphic norm ruled only from the 3rd century, so no Sanskrit turn is yet visible in the delta (Pollock 2006). Gandhara exported both the Buddha image and monks: An Shigao reached Luoyang in 148, Lokakṣema translated there in 178–189.

## Indianization evidence

Against the vault's four-part test — script, law, kingship, state cult — the 2nd century meets at most one criterion, and that one is contested.

Script: Võ Cạnh C.40, cut in an early Pallava-Grantha hand, is the only candidate for 2nd-century Sanskrit in the region. If correctly dated it proves a scribe trained in a South Indian chancery worked for a local ruler. If it belongs to the 4th–5th century, as Zakharov (2010) argues from metre and lexicon, the century has no indigenous epigraphy.

Cult: Óc Eo has produced Amaravati-style Buddha bronzes and a mukhalinga, but conventional dating places most in the 3rd century (Malleret 1962; Ray 1989). The Roman material proves exchange, not conversion.

Kingship: Chinese notices describe a king with walled towns and taxes in gold, silver and pearls. They record no Sanskrit title, no -varman suffix, no linga cult. The Kaundinya–Somā charter myth is attested only from the 7th century.

Law: nothing. No dharmaśāstra text, Sanskrit legal formula or Indic judicial vocabulary appears in the 2nd-century record.

The verdict: this century built the plumbing — ports, lanes, merchant communities. Indianization proper is a 3rd- and 4th-century epigraphic event.

## Debates & confidence

**Võ Cạnh's date** is the live controversy. Coedès (1968) placed it in the 2nd–3rd century and read Śrī Māra as a Funanese king; Zakharov (2010), following Sircar, argues the verse metre and the term *bhṛtya* belong to Gupta-period epigraphy, pushing the stele to the 4th or 5th century. One date decides whether Southeast Asia's Sanskrit epigraphy begins in the 2nd or the 4th century.

**Cattigara** is unidentified. Óc Eo is the leading candidate but unproven (Hanus & Smagur 2020).

**"Funan" itself** may be a Chinese administrative fiction; Jacques (1979) and Hà Văn Tấn (1986) argue the name has no local epigraphic existence.

Confidence: high on trade goods and site chronology, low on political narrative and the Võ Cạnh date.
