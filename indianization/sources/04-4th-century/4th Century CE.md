# 4th Century CE

The 4th century is the century Champa starts speaking for itself. Bhadravarman I moves his capital to Simhapura, dedicates the Mỹ Sơn valley to a linga named after himself and Shiva, and invites Brahmins from India; within a generation a Cham-language inscription in Pallava letters shows the package localized, not merely imported. Upstream, Samudragupta's march to Kanchipuram binds Gupta and Pallava into the model that will travel.

## What happened

Samudragupta (c. 335–375) turned the Gupta court into the most self-conscious Sanskrit monarchy of the age. His Allahabad pillar inscription, composed by the minister Harishena, is a *prashasti* in the high court style: it lists the kings of Dakṣiṇāpatha he defeated and then reinstated, among them Vishnugopa of Kanchipuram (Pollock 2006). The point was not annexation but hierarchy — defeated rulers kept their thrones and owed homage. That model, a Sanskrit eulogy wrapping a local king in universal sovereignty, is exactly what Southeast Asian courts would copy.

In Champa, Bhadravarman I (r. 380–413) is the first king the inscriptions name. He took the regnal style Dharmamahārāja Śrī Bhadravarman, moved the capital from Kandapurpura to Simhapura — modern Trà Kiệu — and founded the sanctuary of Mỹ Sơn, dedicating the valley to a linga called Bhadreśvara, "the lord Bhadra", merging his own name with Shiva's. He invited learned Brahmins to settle in the kingdom, and C.72 records the god's four boundaries, a tithe on the land and a curse on anyone who encroaches (Finot 1902; Majumdar 1927).

Champa was also fighting. Chinese annals record attacks on Jin-held Jiaozhou in 347 under Fan Wen, and Bhadravarman himself campaigned in Rinan and Jiuzhen in 399 before a long war with the Chinese governor Du Xue; he vanished after a defeat around 413 (Vickery 1998).

The decisive document is C.174 from Đông Yên Châu, near Trà Kiệu: the oldest substantial text in Old Cham, an Austronesian language, written in Pallava script. It invokes a Nāga and ends with a curse threatening a thousand years of hell (Schweyer 2011). Sanskrit supplies the medium and the ritual grammar; Cham supplies the language and the local serpent deity. That combination — not the Sanskrit alone — is what Indianization looks like on the ground.

Funan meanwhile sent Chandana to the Jin court in 357 with tamed elephants, and Buddhist texts and monks continued to move through the delta toward Jiankang (Pelliot 1903). In the Mediterranean, Constantine's new capital and Theodosius's Christianity reoriented Rome eastward; in Sri Lanka the tooth relic became a palladium of kingship; in Gandhara the Kidarites displaced Kushan authority. None of this stopped the sea trade — it changed who financed it.

## Indianization evidence

Champa crosses the vault's threshold in this century, and does so on three criteria rather than two.

Script: C.72 is formal Sanskrit in a Pallava-derived hand, and C.174 adapts the same script to Old Cham. Adaptation to the vernacular is the strongest single marker in the whole dataset: it means scribes were training locals, not just importing specialists (Schweyer 2011).

Kingship: the *-varman* suffix, the title Dharmamahārāja, the identification of the king with a linga, the settlement of Brahmins and the fixing of temple boundaries and tithes together constitute an Indic royal theology, not a decorative veneer.

Cult: Mỹ Sơn is a Shaiva state sanctuary from its foundation. The cult is continuous, propertied and dynastic.

Law: partial. C.72's boundary-and-curse formula is dharma-legal in idiom — it protects a religious endowment with karmic sanction — but it is not a code, and no dharmaśāstra manuscript is attested.

The century therefore marks the transition from contact to adoption: the moment a Southeast Asian king represents himself, in his own country, in an Indian language and an Indian script, as a dharma-king.

## Debates & confidence

**C.72's date** is secure in the reign but not in its details. Bhadravarman's original shrine was timber; the brick A1 that survives is later, and the inscription's "invitation of Brahmins" may be a later temple tradition retrojected onto the founder.

**C.174's date** is argued: some epigraphists place it in the 4th century, earlier scholarship in the 5th or later — which would weaken the claim that Cham vernacular epigraphy begins under Bhadravarman.

**Samudragupta's southern campaign** may have been a raid, not a conquest; the inscription says the kings were released.

**The "Gupta golden age"** is a periodization some reject as court propaganda.

Confidence: high on the reign and inscriptions; medium on C.174; low on Bhadravarman's death and his kingdom's size.
