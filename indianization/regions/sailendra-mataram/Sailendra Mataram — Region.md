# Sailendra–Mataram (Java)

**c. 730–930 CE** · Central Java · Old Javanese + Sanskrit · Śaiva → Mahāyāna/Vajrayāna → Śaiva

Between roughly 730 and 930 CE the rice plains of south-central Java produced Southeast Asia's densest concentration of Indian-derived sacred architecture — Kalasan, Sewu, Borobudur, Prambanan — under two houses whose relationship is still argued over, the Śailendras and the Sanjayas. It is the best-documented case of Indianization as selective appropriation: Indian script, titulature, cosmology and monastic institutions were imported wholesale, then rebuilt around a Javanese landscape of volcanoes, terraced ancestor shrines and rice-rent economies.

## Why it matters

Mataram is the control case for every general theory of Indianization. Here a dated epigraphic sequence runs two centuries and ties to standing monuments, so claims about *how* Indian forms travelled can be tested.

Three results matter. First, the **vector was institutional, not military**. The Kalasan and Kelurak charters attribute temple foundations to a *guru* — at Kelurak, a teacher "from the land of Gauda" in Bengal — who obtained the king's consent and his land grant (Coedès 1968). Buddhism arrived as a network of masters with texts and consecration rights, funded by royal *sīma* endowments.

Second, the **script breaks the standard model**. Most of maritime Southeast Asia took the Pallava script of Coromandel; the Śailendra charters of 778 and 782 are in Prāṇāgarī, a northern Indian script matching the Bengal connection rather than the Tamil one. "Pallava script" is the wrong single label for Java.

Third, the flow **reversed**. Around 860 a Javanese-descended king in Sumatra endowed five villages at Nalanda in Bihar (Shastri 1924) — a two-way circuit, and the strongest single argument against the older "Hindu colony" reading. Borobudur itself is no copy: a Mahāyāna mandala in plan, but its stepped terraces continue the Javanese *punden berundak* ancestor-mound form, carved by local workshops from imported texts (Miksic 1990).

## Historical arc

**Sanjaya (r. c. 717–746).** The sequence opens with the Canggal inscription of 732, a Sanskrit *prasasti* in Pallava script from Gunung Wukir, Magelang. It records that Sanjaya, nephew of king Sanna, erected a Śivaliṅga and claimed rule over Java — "rich in grain and gold mines" — after disorder. The linga stands 10 km from where Borobudur would rise: the two religions share one landscape.

**Panangkaran (r. c. 746–784).** Patronage turned Buddhist within a generation. The Kalasan inscription (700 Śaka = 778 CE) records that the king's *guru*, "jewel of the Śailendra family", persuaded Mahārāja Panangkaran to build a Tārā temple and a monastery, endowing the village of Kalasa to the saṅgha; it is the first dated use of the dynastic name. The Kelurak inscription (782) records a Mañjuśrī sanctuary — Candi Sewu — for King Indra, equating Buddha, Dharma and Saṅgha with Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Maheśvara, a Vajrayāna move. A Śailendra banner appears at Ligor in 775.

**Samaratungga (r. c. 812–833).** The Karangtengah inscription (824) is bilingual — Sanskrit for doctrine, Old Javanese for the land grant — and records that Samaratungga's daughter Pramodhawardhani consecrated a *Jinalaya*, generally identified as Borobudur, while a *Venuvana* received the ashes of King Indra. Borobudur's five stages are placed c. 780–833 on stylistic grounds (Coedès 1968); Tri Tepusan (842) endows tax-free land to maintain it.

**Balaputra and Pikatan (r. c. 847–855).** Samaratungga married his daughter to the Sanjaya lord Rakai Pikatan; the alliance failed, and war broke out in the 850s. The Shivagrha inscription, issued in 856 by Pikatan's successor, records the defeat of a rebel fortified at Ratu Boko and the dedication of the Śivagrha — Prambanan — whose 47-metre Shiva temple was inaugurated that November: a Śaiva counter-monument to the Buddhist plain. The defeated prince is conventionally Balaputra, who withdrew to Sumatra. The Nalanda copper-plate of Devapaladeva, c. 860, styles him king of Suvarṇadvīpa, grandson of a Śailendra king of Java, and records his endowment of five villages at Nalanda (Shastri 1924).

**Mpu Sindok (r. 929–947).** In 929 the court moved to Tamwlang, then Watugaluh in the Brantas valley, founding the Isyana line. The causes are unresolved — Merapi eruption, epidemic, succession politics, or river access to the spice route. Central Java's temple programme stopped; Borobudur was left to the ash.

## Indian vs local

| Indian import | Javanese adaptation |
|---|---|
| Sanskrit *prasasti* in Prāṇāgarī | Bilingual charters: Sanskrit for doctrine, Old Javanese for land and witnesses |
| Śivaliṅga and temple as Mount Meru | Stepped terraces continuing the *punden berundak* ancestor-mound (Borobudur) |
| *Cakravartin* / universal ruler | *Rakai* lordship and *kadatwan* court, nested over local village heads |
| Tārā and Mañjuśrī cults from Bengal | Royal palladium images in gold and silver; deity assimilated to royal ancestor |
| Buddhist *vihāra* and saṅgha | *Sīma* tax-free estates funding temples, without a surviving ordination lineage |
| Rāmāyaṇa and Purāṇic epic | Prambanan reliefs with Javanese dress, architecture and demon-masks |

## Debates & confidence

**Borobudur's dates** are inferred, not attested: no foundation charter survives, so the c. 780–833 span rests on carving-style comparison and on identifying the 824 *Jinalaya* with the monument. Confidence: medium. **The Śailendra–Sanjaya relationship** is the field's oldest quarrel. The dual-dynasty model (Bosch 1952; de Casparis 1956) posits two competing houses, Buddhist and Śaiva; the single-dynasty model (Poerbatjaraka) makes Sanjaya's line Śailendra and reads the religious shifts as patronage changes within one family. Confidence: low either way — Sojomerto's *Dapunta Selendra* may not even contain the dynastic name. **The 856 battle** is recorded, but the identification of the rebel as Balaputra is an inference; Jordaan (1993) and others propose the Javanese lord Rakai Walaing instead, which would make it a civil war rather than a religious one.
