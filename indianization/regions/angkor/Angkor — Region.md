# Angkor (Cambodia)

**802–1431 CE** · Khmer · Sanskrit + Old Khmer → Pali · Shaiva → Mahayana → Theravada

Angkor was the capital region of Kambuja (Kambujadeśa), the Khmer mandala that dominated mainland Southeast Asia from the 9th to the 15th century. Its core — Yasodharapura, Angkor Thom, Angkor Wat — formed a dispersed urban landscape of roughly 1,000 km², the largest pre-industrial city by area (Evans et al. 2013). Indian kingship, Sanskrit and the temple-mountain were naturalised so thoroughly that the Khmer later abandoned Sanskrit and Hinduism for Pali and Theravada without abandoning their temples.

## Why it matters

Angkor is the clearest case of **Indianization as localization** (Wolters 1999), not colonization. Indian *devaraja*, *cakravartin*, Sanskrit and the temple-mountain were adopted, but fused with local *neak ta* ancestor spirits, naga ancestry and a hydraulic economy of barays and canals. No Indian army, governor or colony is attested; the agents were Brahmin specialists, monks and Khmer elites selecting from a repertoire.

Scale is the second point. Where Champa was a chain of trading river-mouths and Sailendra Mataram a rice-growing rival, Angkor made the imported vocabulary imperial: state temples that consumed tens of thousands of labourers, 102 hospitals and roads across the realm. LiDAR shows a managed landscape of mounds, ponds and canals, not a "lost city" (Evans et al. 2013).

Third, Angkor shows the **Hindu → Buddhist transition as retention, not replacement**. Vishnu reliefs survive inside a living Theravada monastery; the temple was never abandoned, only re-consecrated (Carter et al. 2019). Brahmin *bakou* families still officiate at court rites; *neak ta* shrines sit beside Buddhist *wats*.

## Historical arc

**802** — Jayavarman II is consecrated on Phnom Kulen (Mahendraparvata) by the Brahmin Hiraṇyadāma. The rite establishes the *kamrateṅ jagat ta rāja*, "the lord of the world who is king". The evidence is the Sdok Kak Thom inscription (K.235), carved in 1053 by a priestly family pressing its hereditary claim (Kulke 1978); the Java-origin story is widely doubted.

**889–900** — Yasovarman I moves the capital to Yasodharapura, centred on Phnom Bakheng, a hill terraced into a five-tier temple-mountain. He builds the East Baray (7.5 km). The capital becomes a cosmological diagram: Meru at the centre, ocean in the moat.

**1113–1150** — Suryavarman II builds Angkor Wat: 162.6 ha, dedicated to Vishnu, oriented west. Five towers are Meru's peaks, the 200 m moat the cosmic ocean; the bas-reliefs run the Churning of the Ocean of Milk, the epics and the king's procession.

**1177** — A Cham fleet comes up the Mekong and across Tonlé Sap, sacks Yasodharapura and kills Tribhuvanadityavarman. The shock is ideological as much as military: the protective god has failed.

**1181–1218** — Jayavarman VII defeats the Cham, is crowned, and rebuilds on a Mahayana programme. Angkor Thom (9 km²) is laid out around the Bayon, whose 216 faces are read as Avalokiteśvara. Ta Prohm (1186), Preah Khan (1191), 102 hospitals and roads follow.

**1296–97** — Zhou Daguan arrives at the court of Indravarman III. He describes shaven-headed Theravada monks and Pali texts, and reports that "in the recent war with the Siamese, the country was utterly devastated". Theravada is established at court, but Brahmanical rites remain.

**c. 1350–1431** — Theravada becomes dominant and Angkor Wat is gradually re-consecrated to the Buddha. Sanskrit epigraphy at Angkor ends by 1295 (K.488); the last dated Khmer inscription is 1327 (K.470). Ayutthaya attacks repeatedly (1352–57, 1393–94); the 1431 sack drives Ponhea Yat to Phnom Penh. But "collapse" misleads: Angkor Wat's enclosure is reoccupied in the 14th–15th centuries and used into the 18th century (Carter et al. 2019).

## Debates & confidence

**"Collapse."** 1431 is a chronicle convention, not an extinction. Angkor Wat's enclosure was reoccupied after a 13th–14th-century gap and used into the 18th century; Carter et al. (2019) read the decline as protracted, driven by drought, monsoons and maritime trade. Buckley et al. (2010) tie the megadroughts to the water network's failure.

**Devaraja.** Coedès read it as royal apotheosis; Kulke (1978) and Jacques (1985) argue the Sanskrit term denotes the god, while the Khmer *kamrateṅ jagat ta rāja* is a territorial guardian spirit.

**Hindu → Buddhist.** Not linear: Mahayana, then a violent Shaiva restoration under Jayavarman VIII (1243–95), then Theravada under Indravarman III.

**Hydraulic state.** Groslier's *cité hydraulique* (1979) is qualified by Acker's (1998) tests, which found the barays poorly placed to irrigate the main fields; most now read them as flood-control, transport and cosmology.
