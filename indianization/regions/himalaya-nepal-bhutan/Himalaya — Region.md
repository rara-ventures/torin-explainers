# Himalaya — Nepal & Bhutan

The Himalayan conservatory is the Kathmandu Valley, with the Bhutanese valleys attached through Tibet. From the Licchavi inscriptions of the fifth century to the Shah conquest of 1768–69, Nepal sustained a Sanskrit epigraphic and legal culture that lapsed in most of India, and a Newar Vajrayana that preserved the Sanskrit Buddhist canon after the Indian monasteries were destroyed. Its Indianization was a storage system: forms were conserved, recast locally, and re-exported north.

## Why it matters

Nepal is the vault's clearest case of Indianization as conservation rather than conversion. The Licchavi kings made Sanskrit the court language while Newar remained the vernacular, and left roughly 190 inscriptions — 53 in Gupta script, 137 in the later Kutila — the densest Sanskrit epigraphic corpus in the Himalayan zone (Vajracharya 1973). Manadeva's 464 Changu Narayan pillar uses the panegyric vocabulary of Gupta rule, reproducing its political grammar beyond the Himalaya within a generation (Riccardi 1989).

The second asset is Newar Buddhism: a non-monastic, caste-based Vajrayana in which married Vajracharya and Shakya householders perform ritual for lay patrons, keeping Sanskrit as its liturgical and scholastic language after Indian Buddhism lost its monasteries. The tradition claims to be the only living Buddhist Sanskrit tradition and holds most surviving Sanskrit Buddhist manuscripts (Gellner 1992). That archive is why Nepal matters to Tibet: after the twelfth-century collapse of Indian monasticism, Tibetan scholars travelled to Kathmandu to obtain and copy originals, and the flow of Newar artisans and texts northward continued for centuries (Davidson 2005). Nepal did not merely receive Indianization; it stored it and sold it on.

## Historical arc

**464 CE — Manadeva I at Changu Narayan.** Licchavi offshoots from Vaishali held the valley by 400. Manadeva's pillar is the earliest dated record: Sanskrit in Gupta Brahmi, a royal eulogy naming three predecessors, at a Vishnu temple (Riccardi 1989). It fixes Nepalese chronology.

**c. 500–643 CE — Shaiva state.** Pashupatinath became the focus of Licchavi patronage, with Pashupata ascetics, varna-jati norms and Dharmashastra learning. Amshuvarman (605–621) ruled as *maharajadhiraja* and worked the salt route to Tibet; Xuanzang, writing in 643, reports some 2,000 monks of both Mahayana and Hinayana (Petech 1984).

**694–705 CE — Sivadeva II.** The corpus is dominated by grants of fields, revenue and judicial authority. Sivadeva II endowed Buddhist institutions heavily, and the fourteenth-century *Gopalarajavamsavali* says he took monastic vows. The abdication is unverifiable, but the grants treat the sangha as a parallel estate with enforceable rights (Lewis 2000).

**879–1482 CE — Malla law and the Newar dyarchy.** The Nepal Samvat era begins in 879, ending Licchavi rule; three Malla courts shared the valley by 1400. Jayasthiti Malla (1382–1395) issued a caste code staffed by Brahmins from Mithila and Banaras, fitting Buddhist clergy into the varna order as Vajracharya and Shakya without suppression (Petech 1984; Slusser 1982). At the Malla peak a married Vajracharya priesthood served the same king as the Brahman *purohita*, and consecration required the Kumari's blessing (Gellner 1992).

**1504–1758 CE — archive and co-worship.** Tsangnyön Heruka was invited by Kathmandu's king in 1504 to restore Swayambhu. Its 1751–58 renovation carries a trilingual dedication in Nepal Bhasa, Tibetan and Devanagari, with Hindu and Tibetan donors: one monument revered as Swayambhunath by Hindus and as a dharmadhatu mandala by Buddhists.

**1768–1769 CE — Shah unification.** Prithvi Narayan Shah entered Kathmandu on 25 September 1768 during Indra Jatra and completed the valley conquest in 1769. The Shah state kept the Malla settlement, funding Brahmanical and Buddhist establishments alike; the king's annual Kumari tika remained a legitimating rite.

**Bhutan.** Kyichu Lhakhang is traditionally one of 108 border-taming temples built by Songtsen Gampo in 659, which Phuntsho treats as the start of Bhutanese history. Padmasambhava's visit is placed in the eighth century, Phajo Drugom Zhigpo brought Drukpa Kagyu around 1224, and Zhabdrung Ngawang Namgyal unified Bhutan in 1616 under the Tsa Yig. Every Indic element — script, mantra, monastic law — arrived through Tibet (Aris 1979; Phuntsho 2013).

## Indian vs local

| Indian import | Himalayan transformation |
|---|---|
| Gupta imperial titulature (*maharajadhiraja*, chakravartin rhetoric) | Licchavi kings ruled through caste councils and *samanta* landholders; legitimacy ran through Pashupatinath and Taleju, not a devaraja cult |
| Brahmanical varna and dharmaśāstra | The 1382 Malla code made Buddhist clergy hereditary castes; Vajracharya became a married "Buddhist Brahmin" |
| Buddhist monasticism | Newar Buddhism replaced celibate monasteries with householder clergy in *baha* courtyards — a caste church, not a sangha |
| Shakta/Tantric goddess worship | The Kumari: a Shakya girl becomes living Taleju and blesses the king at Indra Jatra; first recorded 1280 |
| Sanskrit Buddhist canon | Preserved in Newar manuscripts after the Indian monastic collapse, then copied and exported to Tibet |
| Mauryan/Gupta stupa cult | Swayambhu is both Hindu Swayambhunath and Vajrayana mandala; trilingual 1751 fabric |

## Debates & confidence

Licchavi chronology rests almost entirely on the 464 inscription; king lists before Manadeva float, and the early dates in the list (a Jayavarman placed at 185) are unusable. Epigraphic counts vary between roughly 190 and 200+; fragments are contested. Confidence is high on 464, medium on the fifth-century sequence. The "Newar dyarchy" is a misleading label: Vajracharya and Brahman priests served one sovereign, dividing ritual labour rather than sovereignty, and the fifteenth-century caste settlement should not be projected back onto the Licchavi period (Gellner 1992). Bhutan is weakest: its Buddhism is Tibetan-mediated, so "Indianization" may be "Tibetanization" at one remove, and Kyichu's 659 foundation is traditional, not excavated. Confidence: high on the Swayambhu trilingual inscription, medium elsewhere.
