# Champa (Vietnam Coast)

Champa was less a kingdom than a chain of Austronesian-speaking Cham polities along the central Vietnamese coast, from Linyi/Lâm Ấp (c. 192) to Panduranga (to 1832). It took Sanskrit, a South Indian–derived script, Śaiva kingship and brick temples from India, but on local terms: its earliest sentence is a curse on a local nāga, and its earliest polity is visible mainly through Chinese labels.

## At a glance

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Polity** | Cham polities: Linyi/Lâm Ấp (c. 192–629), Campā, Indrapura, Vijaya, Panduranga |
| **Core area** | Thu Bồn valley (Simhapura/Trà Kiệu, Mỹ Sơn); later Indrapura, Vijaya, Kauthara, Panduranga |
| **Script** | Old Cham in a Pallava-type South Indian script; Sanskrit for ritual |
| **State cult** | Śaiva, centred on Bhadreśvara at Mỹ Sơn; Mahayana interlude at Indrapura (875–c. 982); later Bani Islam |
| **Indianized?** | Yes — Indian script, Sanskrit titulature, Śaiva temple cult and Brahmanical origin myth, but no Indian rule |

## Why it matters

Champa isolates the variables. Unlike Angkor or Java it was never a single agrarian empire with one fixed capital; it was a set of river-mouth polities whose wealth came from the China–Indian Ocean trade. Indianization there reads as a repertoire chosen by local elites, not a package installed by a state. First, the epigraphic sequence is early and bilingual: the Võ Cạnh stele (C.40) is the oldest Sanskrit inscription in Southeast Asia, and the Đông Yên Châu stele (C.174, c. 350) is the oldest surviving text in any Austronesian language — a Cham nāga curse in Indian script. Second, its Mahayana Buddhism came from the Pala world and Java, not Amaravati, and Indravarman II's Đồng Dương monastery (875) is the largest Buddhist foundation in Champa. Third, its long war with Đại Việt is a control case — Indianized kingship offered no defence against a Sinitic state with its own mandala logic. Śaiva Ahiér and Bani Cham communities still practise their rites in Panduranga, making Champa one of the few Indianized polities with a living ritual system.

## Historical arc

**192** — Khu Liên rebels at Tượng Lâm; Chinese annals call the resulting polity Linyi (Lâm Ấp), though whether the two are the same remains disputed (Vickery 2005).

**2nd–4th c.** — At Trà Kiệu, Han coins and roof tiles precede Indic material (Glover 2011). Indian goods, Brahmi-derived script and Śaiva iconography arrive via Funan/Óc Eo and direct South Indian trade.

**c. 350–400** — C.174, the Old Cham nāga curse, is cut near Trà Kiệu; the Sanskrit C.40 stele of Śrī Māra is set up at Nha Trang (date contested). Bhadravarman I (r. c. 380–413) founds the Śaiva sanctuary of Bhadreśvara at Mỹ Sơn, dedicating the valley to it (C.72). Simhapura becomes the capital.

**605** — Sui general Liu Fang sacks the Cham capital and carries off the royal archives: Buddhist books and the gold tablets of eighteen kings (*Sui shu* 64; Maspero 1928).

**7th c.** — Sambhuvarman rebuilds Mỹ Sơn after a fire. Prakāśadharman/Vikrāntavarman I issues C.96 (18 February 658), the first inscription to name Campāpura and claim Kaundinya–Soma nāga ancestry.

**8th c.** — Power shifts south to Kauthara and Po Nagar. A raid (usually dated 774) steals the jewelled mukhaliṅga; Satyavarman restores it in 784 (C.38), and the goddess Bhagavatī begins to eclipse the liṅga.

**875** — Indravarman II founds Indrapura (Đồng Dương) and the Mahayana monastery of Laksmindralokeśvara (C.66, 13 May 875). Po Nagar receives a golden Bhagavatī in 918; the Khmer take it in 950 and a stone image replaces it in 965.

**982** — Lê Hoàn of Đại Cồ Việt sacks a northern Cham city and kills the king; Indrapura declines and the centre moves to Vijaya. Vickery (2005) argues the target lay in Quảng Bình, not at Indrapura.

**11th–12th c.** — Vijaya is the capital. Lý Thánh Tông captures Rudravarman III in 1069 and takes three northern provinces. In 1177 a Cham fleet sacks Angkor; Jayavarman VII retaliates, and Champa is under Khmer occupation c. 1190–1220.

**1471** — Lê Thánh Tông takes Vijaya and annexes the north. Panduranga survives as a tributary until Minh Mạng dissolves it in 1832.

## Indian vs local

| Indian import | Cham localization |
|---|---|
| Sanskrit titulature (*-varman*, *rājadharma*) | King's name fused into the god's: Bhadravarman → Bhadreśvara |
| Pallava-type script and Sanskrit eulogy | Old Cham used for C.174, a nāga curse at a local spring |
| Kaundinya–Soma origin myth | Recast in C.96 as a Cham dynastic charter, beside local nāga claims |
| Śaiva temple-mountain | Brick *kalan* towers housing the liṅga as lineage god |
| Mahayana Buddhism (Pala, Java) | Laksmindralokeśvara as dynastic protector beside Śiva |
| Islam (15th–17th c.) | Bani Islam keeps the Cham ritual calendar and absorbs Po Nagar |

## Debates & confidence

C.40's date is uncertain: palaeographers place it between the 2nd and 5th century, and Zakharov (2010) argues for a Gupta-era date, which would make Funan, not Champa, the first Sanskrit epigraphic polity. Linyi = Champa is also contested; Vickery (2005) and Schweyer (2012) treat "Champa" as a later cover-name for several polities; the 192 date is Chinese annalistic convention. The 982 target is disputed (Vickery 2005), as are the 1471 casualty figures. The political narrative is therefore medium-confidence at best. The claim that Gaudidvipa/Bengali monks founded Đồng Dương rests on inference from art and script; C.66 names no Indian monastery.
