# Philippines — The Indianized Periphery

The Philippines is the outer edge of the Indianized world. There is no Sanskrit temple-mountain, no *devarāja*, no *dharmaśāstra*, no Indic state cult. Yet a 2 kg gold goddess was cast in Mindanao, the country's oldest dated document is a 900 CE copperplate in Old Malay and Kawi script, and the pre-colonial Baybayin syllabary descends from Brahmic writing. The Philippines is the control case for what Indianization looks like when it arrives as **objects, titles and trade** rather than as a court package.

## At a glance

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Polities** | Barangay networks and river ports: Tondo, Maynila, Namayan, Butuan, Ma-i, Cebu, Sulu, Maguindanao |
| **Core area** | Pasig–Laguna delta (Tondo), Agusan/Butuan, Cebu, Sulu archipelago |
| **Script** | Kawi (900 CE); later Baybayin and other *suyat* scripts, Brahmic-derived |
| **Religion** | Indigenous *anito* cults with Hindu–Buddhist goldwork; Islam in Sulu from the 14th–15th c.; Catholicism after 1565 |
| **Indianized?** | Partly — script, Sanskrit titles (*rajah*, *śrī*), goldwork and loanwords; no Indic kingship, temple cult or dharmaśāstra |
| **Key date** | 900 CE — Laguna Copperplate Inscription, the earliest document in the Philippines |

## Why it matters

The Philippines tests the definition. If Indianization means a Sanskrit-derived script, Sanskrit epigraphy, Indic kingship and *dharmaśāstra*, then the archipelago has only the first, and only in fragments. That makes it the clearest **outer-ring** case in the vault — not a failed copy but a different mode of contact: maritime trade without a rice state, and objects without a state religion.

The two surviving objects carry the argument. The **Agusan image** — the so-called Golden Tara — is a 2 kg, 21-karat gold female deity, 178 mm high, found in 1917 on the Wawa River in Agusan del Sur and now in the Field Museum, Chicago. Its identity is disputed (Śaiva goddess, Buddhist Tārā, or the Tantric offering goddess Vajralāsyā), and its style points to a Javanese model from the Nganjuk region. It is evidence of a shared goldworking and Tantric visual world, not of an Indian colony.

The **Laguna Copperplate Inscription**, found in 1987 at Lumban, is dated to the Śaka year 822 — Monday, 21 April 900 — and is written in Old Malay in Kawi script with Sanskrit technical vocabulary. It records the acquittal of a debt of 1 *kati* and 8 *suwarṇa* of gold (about 865 g) owed by the family of Namwaran, in the presence of the chief of Tundun (Tondo). It is not a royal panegyric. It is a receipt. That is the point: Indian forms reached the Philippines through commerce and law, not through a court commissioning Sanskrit verse.

The script completed the transfer. Kawi, the Old Javanese script, was used in Luzon by 900. Baybayin and the other *suyat* scripts are Brahmic-derived, but the route is indirect — through Java and the Malay world, not straight from India. The Philippines therefore shows the **last mile** of the script vector: an Indian writing principle localized into syllabaries for Austronesian languages, with no Sanskrit literature attached.

## Historical arc

**c. 500 BCE–200 CE** — Austronesian mariners link island Southeast Asia to southern India and Sri Lanka. The Philippines sits on the eastern leg of that network. There is no evidence of Indian settlement, and no Indian object of this date has the density of Óc Eo or Kedah (Bellina 2014; Manguin 2016).

**9th–10th c.** — The Agusan image is cast, probably locally from a Javanese model (Bosch 1920; Francisco 1963). In 900 the Laguna Copperplate is issued at Tondo, naming Pailah, Puliran, Binuangan and Dewata. Old Malay is the trade lingua franca; Kawi is the chancery script.

**971–12th c.** — Ma-i is first documented in the *Song shi* in 971 and stays in Chinese geography into the 14th century; its location is unresolved between Mindoro and Bay in Laguna, where the Hokkien reading of the characters is similar (Cole 1912; Go Bon Juan 2005). Butuan sends missions to the Song court and keeps direct trade links recorded with Champa, Đại Việt, Pon-i (Brunei), Srivijaya, Majapahit, Kambuja and Persia. Butuan’s ivory privy seal, cut in the 9th–12th centuries with *Butban* in stylised Kawi, is the second Sanskrit-script object from the archipelago and the only one naming a Philippine polity itself; the *balangay* boat-burials at Libertad on the old Agusan river make Butuan a maritime culture rather than a legend. The Agusan–Surigao zone is linked to Javanese gold miners, an inference from style and later trade, not an inscription.

**13th–14th c.** — Majapahit trade ceramics and glass reach Philippine ports. The *Nagarakretagama* (1365) lists a dependency that some read as Sulu (*Solot*), but the identification is contested. Rajah and *śrī* titles spread through the Malay-speaking zone without the institutions that normally accompany them.

**14th–15th c.** — Islam arrives in Sulu through Johor, Brunei and Gujarati networks; the Sulu Sultanate is traditionally founded in 1457. Tondo, Maynila and Namayan remain Hindu–Buddhist or mixed until the Spanish period.

**1521–1571** — Magellan reaches Cebu in 1521; Legazpi takes Cebu in 1565. Maynila burns in 1570 — Spanish firing or a local scorched-earth withdrawal is still unresolved — and is declared the city of Manila on 24 June 1571. Before conquest, Tondo and Maynila ran a duopoly on the archipelago’s Chinese goods, and Maynila’s rulers used intermarriage with Brunei to strengthen noble claims, the standard technique of a thalassocratic periphery that could not be garrisoned. Catholicism replaces the *anito* cults, but Baybayin survives in land documents into the 17th century.

**1917 / 1987** — The Agusan image is found and sold to the Field Museum in 1922 for ₱4,000; the Laguna Copperplate is dredged from the Lumbang River and translated by Antoon Postma in 1991–92.

## Indian vs local

| Indian import | Philippine localization |
|---|---|
| Kawi script | Baybayin and other *suyat* syllabaries; no Sanskrit literature |
| *Rāja*, *śrī*, *dewata* | Titles layered onto *datu*/barangay authority; no territorial state |
| Hindu–Buddhist iconography | Gold deities with ambiguous attributes, absorbed into *anito* cosmology |
| Sanskrit technical vocabulary | Legal and astronomical terms in Old Malay, not a liturgical language |
| Temple-mountain / state cult | Absent; no monumental Hindu or Buddhist architecture |
| Dharmaśāstra | Absent; customary law and the *Code of Kalantiaw* — the latter a 20th-century forgery exposed by William Henry Scott |

## Debates & confidence

**The Golden Tara.** Identity is unresolved: H. Otley Beyer read a Śaiva goddess, Juan Francisco a Buddhist Tārā, and recent scholarship Vajralāsyā of the Diamond Realm maṇḍala (Capistrano-Baker 2011). The date floats between the 9th–10th and 13th–14th centuries, and the "Javanese miners in Agusan" link is Beyer's inference, not epigraphy. Confidence: low on identity, medium on a Javanese stylistic model, high that it is local work.

**The copperplate.** Postma's translation is accepted; his toponyms are not. He placed Pailah, Puliran and Binuangan in Bulacan; Jaime Tiongson and the Pila Historical Society place them around Laguna de Bay. What is secure is the date, the language, the debt mechanism and Tondo. Confidence: high on text and date, medium on geography.

**"Hindu kingdoms."** The claim that Butuan, Cebu or Tondo were Hindu kingdoms rests on later genealogies (Sri Lumay of Cebu), Chinese trade notices and titles, not on contemporary Sanskrit inscriptions. The *rajah* title is real; the state is not demonstrated. Confidence: low.

**“Barangay” as a category.** The word used to describe the whole pre-colonial political order rests on a very small textual base: nearly every early citation goes back to one Spanish report, Juan de Plascencia’s *Las costumbres de los indios Tagalos de Filipinas* (1589), and Damon Woods argues that “barangay” as a label for polities is a Spanish reconstruction rather than an indigenous institution. Most barangays were 30–100 households; Tondo, Maynila, Cebu, Butuan and Sulu are another order of thing entirely. Confidence: high that the evidence is thin, open on the correct indigenous term.

**The epics may be late.** The Maranao *Maharadia Lawana*, the Philippine Rāmāyaṇa, was recorded by Juan Francisco only in 1968 — and Francisco himself dated the story’s arrival to the **17th–19th centuries**, after Spanish contact, via Javanese and Malay channels. The *Darangen* (17 cycles, some 72,000 lines; UNESCO-recognised 2005/8) preserves pre-Islamic Maranao law and values but cannot be dated to the Indianization period. Literary “Indianization” in the Philippines is therefore a weak inference, not a strong one, and should not be filmed as a fact. Confidence: low.

**Caste and the fate of words.** The *timawa*/*tumao*/*uripon* hierarchy is a local class system, not *varṇa* — and the vocabulary itself records conquest: *timawa*, a warrior rank, was demoted under Spanish rule to “freeman” and then to “poor”, while *maharlika*, a Tagalog warrior class, was inflated in modern usage into “royalty”, which it never was. Spiritual leadership, by contrast, was overwhelmingly female — the *babaylan*, *katalonan* and *balian*, with effeminate men (*asog*, *bayok*) also serving — and no Indian analogue displaced it. The Wikipedia article's "caste system" label is wrong and should not be imported.

**Scale.** The Philippines is the strongest argument in the vault against the "Indian colony" model: script and goldwork travelled, institutions did not. Confidence: high.
