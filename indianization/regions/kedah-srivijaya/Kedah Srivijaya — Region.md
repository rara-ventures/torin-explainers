# Kedah–Srivijaya (Malay Peninsula & Sumatra)

> **Deprecated — retained for reference.** Split on 30 October 2026 into the Straits Archipelago cluster ([[kedah-bujang]], [[langkasuka-srivijaya]], [[temasek-melaka]], [[timor-sandalwood]]); canonical treatment is now [[Straits Archipelago — Region]]. The stale `supersedes: "[[Straits Archipelago]]"` field predates the split, so the frontmatter is inconsistent by design.

Kedah–Srivijaya names two ends of one commercial system: the Bujang Valley entrepôt — *Kataha* in Sanskrit, *Kadaram* to the Cholas, *Kalah* to Arab geographers — and the Sumatran Buddhist polity whose inscriptions call it Srivijaya. Seventh- to eleventh-century Straits traffic bound them together, and Srivijaya became the first Malay power to leave a substantial archive in its own language, written in an Indian script.

## Why it matters

Kedah–Srivijaya is the clearest case where Indianization can be read off the language itself. The 683–686 inscriptions are Old Malay legal and administrative texts in a Pallava-derived script: the imported technology is the writing system, while language, oath formulae and polity's name stay Malay — Wolters' localization (Wolters 1999). The vocabulary of rule is Indian; the grammar of legitimacy is not.

Buddhist institutions also functioned as commercial diplomacy. Balaputra's Nalanda endowment (860), the temple built for the Song emperor (1003) and the Chudamani Vihara at Nagapattinam (c. 1006) trace one monastic circuit linking Pala Bengal, Srivijaya and Chola country. Monks, not armies, carried the relationship.

Kedah's archaeology supplies the material base the Sumatran inscriptions lack: iron workshops, glass, imported ceramics and more than fifty candi (Murphy 2018). The Chola raid of 1025 is a control case — Indianized kingship gave no protection against a naval power interested in tolls, not dharma.

## Historical arc

**c. 2nd–6th century** — Kedah enters Tamil and Indian Ocean commerce as a tin and forest-goods source; *Kadaram* appears in the *Paṭṭiṉappālai*. The Bujang Valley becomes a settled port complex, with iron smelting at Sungai Batu, brick shrines and a stone bar bearing the *ye dharmā* formula in Vatteluttu/Pallava characters (Jacq-Hergoualc'h 2002).

**671** — Yijing, sailing to Nalanda, spends six months at Shilifoshi (Palembang) and reports more than a thousand monks studying Sanskrit grammar and Vinaya; he places *Ka-cha* (Kedah) thirty days' sail away (Yijing, trans. Takakusu 1896).

**683–686** — Three Old Malay inscriptions form the founding archive of the *kadatuan*: Kedukan Bukit (683) records Dapunta Hyang's *siddhayatra* with a force given as 20,000; Talang Tuo (684) endows the Śrīksetra park; Kota Kapur (686) curses treason and announces a campaign against Java (Griffiths 2011).

**775** — Ligor B at Vat Sema Muang, the Kawi face of the Ligor stele, records three bodhisattva sanctuaries and is attributed to a Sailendra ruler, drawing the peninsula's ports into a Java–Sumatra polity.

**c. 790–860** — Sailendra marriage links Java and Sumatra. Balaputra, shut out of Central Java or already ruling Sumatra, becomes mahārāja and in 860 obtains from Devapala a Nalanda monastery grant (Coedès 1968).

**10th century** — Javanese attacks (990) and Srivijayan embassies frame the period; in 1003 a Srivijayan king asks the Song court to name a temple built for the emperor's long life. Ibn Rustah, c. 903, calls the ruler the richest in the world.

**c. 1006** — Maravijayottungavarman builds the Chudamani Vihara at Nagapattinam under Rajaraja I: a Srivijayan foundation on Chola soil.

**1025** — Rajendra I's fleet attacks Kadaram and Palembang, captures Sangrama Vijayatunggavarman and carries off the jewelled gates. Virarajendra raids again in 1068; Kulottunga I claims overlordship of Kedah.

**12th–13th century** — Kedah's ports shift to Sungai Mas and Pengkalan Bujang; the Sumatran centre fragments into Jambi/Dharmasraya; Majapahit and the Thai states absorb the strait.

## Indian vs local

| Indian import | Malay/Straits adaptation |
|---|---|
| Pallava-derived script | Used for Old Malay, not Sanskrit: the 683–686 edicts are vernacular |
| Sanskrit cosmic kingship | *Dapunta Hyang* and *kadatuan*; power through oath and curse, not a temple-mountain |
| Buddhist monasticism | Endowment diplomacy: Nalanda funded, Chudamani Vihara built in Chola country |
| Nāga cosmology | Telaga Batu: seven nāga heads and a spout, water poured over the oath-stone |
| Śaiva temple | Low brick candi on square platforms; Candi Bukit Batu Pahat |
| Tamil merchant guilds | Tamil quarters at Kedah and Barus; charters negotiated locally |

## Debates & confidence

Confidence is high for the inscriptions' dates and wording, medium for the political narrative, low for the polity's extent. The capital is unresolved: Palembang is the Coedès–Manguin orthodoxy, but a 2013 University of Indonesia survey argued for Muara Jambi, and Chaiya has Thai proponents; no site has yielded a convincing urban core. Kulke (1993) asks whether *kadatuan* Śrīvijaya was an empire or a kraton, and most now prefer "mandala" or "network". Kedah's status oscillated between province and autonomous port, and Chola inscriptions claim conquests that archaeology reads as continuity. Sungai Batu's radiocarbon dates — 788–547 BCE against a 2nd–10th-century CE reading — remain contested (Khaw and Gooi 2021). The 20,000 troops of 683 and the older 1090 date for the Chudamani Vihara are both doubtful.
