# China — Gandhara to Chang'an Buddhism

China is the vault's largest relay: it imported Indian Buddhism wholesale in texts, images and monastic institutions while refusing the Indic package of script, dharmaśāstra and kingship. Transmission ran through Parthian, Yuezhi, Kuchan and Sogdian intermediaries and was reprocessed into a Chinese canon, monastic order and Buddha. China is therefore both filter and archive for Southeast Asia.

## At a glance

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Polity** | Han (202 BCE–220 CE) → Six Dynasties → Tang (618–907) → Song → Yuan; an imperial state |
| **Core area** | Luoyang and Chang'an; the Hexi corridor at Dunhuang; the ports of Guangzhou and Quanzhou |
| **Script** | Chinese characters; no Indic script adopted. Siddham confined largely to dhāraṇī |
| **Religion** | Mahayana Buddhism beside Confucian state cult and Daoism |
| **Indianized?** | No — relay. Fails script, dharmaśāstra and Indic kingship; Buddhist patronage never became an Indic state cult |

## Why it matters

China is the control case that makes Indianization measurable rather than a synonym for contact. If translated sutras, monastic law and Buddhist iconography were sufficient, China would be the most Indianized society in Asia. It is not: the emperor remained Son of Heaven, law stayed Confucian-legalist, administration stayed Chinese-literate, and no brahmin genealogy legitimated the throne.

The Central Asian filter matters because it is the same filter. Buddhism reached China through Parthia, Gandhara, Kucha and Sogdiana: An Shigao was Parthian, Lokakṣema Yuezhi, Kumārajīva Kuchan (Zürcher 1959). Their vocabulary reached Korea, Japan and Vietnam, and their recensions set what pilgrims went to India to check. Yijing, a Chinese monk, gives the fullest external description of Srivijaya's monastic curriculum (Takakusu 1896).

China is also the vault's archive. The Hou Hanshu, Gaoseng zhuan and the pilgrims' records preserve routes and institutions for polities that left few inscriptions. Quanzhou's Tamil carvings are the rare case where the Indian side survives in stone rather than Chinese text (Lee 2009).

## Historical arc

**138–126 BCE — the Western Regions opened.** Zhang Qian's mission returned with intelligence on Dayuan, Daxia, Anxi and Shendu (India); Shiji 123 is the first Chinese geographic notice of the subcontinent (Watson 1993).

**65 CE — first documented Buddhism.** Liu Ying, Prince of Chu, patronised Buddhist practices at his court, the earliest dated evidence in China (Hou Hanshu, 5th c.; Zürcher 1959). The White Horse Temple tradition of 68 CE is later hagiography.

**148–189 CE — the translation turn.** An Shigao, a Parthian, reached Luoyang in 148 and worked until about 180; Lokakṣema, a Yuezhi, translated Mahayana sutras including the Aṣṭasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā between 178 and 189 (Zürcher 1959; Nattier 2008).

**366 CE — Dunhuang.** The Mogao caves began as a meditation site and became a pilgrimage and manuscript depot. Cave 17 yielded tens of thousands of documents in Chinese, Tibetan, Sogdian and Sanskrit, including the Diamond Sutra of 868, the earliest dated printed book (Whitfield 2004).

**399–412 CE — Faxian and Kumārajīva.** Kumārajīva, brought to Chang'an in 402, retranslated the Lotus, Diamond and Madhyamaka corpus into classical prose; Faxian returned with Vinaya texts (Gaoseng zhuan, c. 530).

**629–645 CE — Xuanzang.** Xuanzang left illegally in 629, studied at Nalanda under Śīlabhadra, and returned with 657 Sanskrit texts. His Da Tang Xiyu ji (646) covers some 110 countries and remains primary for seventh-century India (Sen 2006).

**671–695 CE — Yijing and the sea route.** Yijing sailed in 671, spent six months at Bhoga (Palembang) and eleven years at Nalanda, then translated four years in Srivijaya before reaching Luoyang in 695 with some 400 texts. His Bhoga chapter is the key source for Srivijaya's Buddhism (Takakusu 1896).

**1087–1368 — the maritime turn.** Song China opened a maritime trade bureau at Quanzhou in 1087; Zhao Rugua's *Zhu Fan Zhi* (1225) catalogued the southern seas. Under the Yuan a Tamil Ainnurruvar guild built a Vishnu temple there in 1283, its granite fragments now in Kaiyuan Temple (Lee 2009).

## Indian vs local

| Indian import | Chinese transformation |
|---|---|
| Sanskrit and Gāndhārī sutras | Literary Chinese; early *geyi* "concept-matching" with Daoist terms, corrected by Xuanzang (Nattier 2008) |
| Open canonical corpus | Closed imperially sponsored Tripitaka, with Chinese apocrypha and *panjiao* rankings |
| Vinaya monastic law | Dharmaguptaka Vinaya became the ordination standard, but imperial law overrode it; filial piety entered monastic practice |
| Stupa relic mound | Multi-storey timber pagoda, axial to a courtyard temple |
| Gandharan Buddha image | Yungang and Longmen figures take Chinese court physiognomy and drapery |
| Chakravartin kingship | Emperor as Son of Heaven and bodhisattva-patron; no brahmin consecration or devarāja |

## Debates & confidence

**First transmission.** 65 CE (Liu Ying) is the earliest documented practice; the White Horse Temple story of 68 CE is legendary, and some push Buddhist presence to the first century BCE. Confidence: high on 65 CE, low on anything earlier.

**Translation attribution.** The Taishō canon assigns twelve texts to Lokakṣema, but Zürcher, Harrison and Nattier accept only a subset; the An Shigao corpus is similarly inflated by later attribution. Confidence: medium.

**"Chinese Buddhism" as Indianization.** Zürcher framed the process as a conquest of China; the vault calls it a relay because script, law and kingship did not transfer. The counter-case is real — Buddhism reshaped Chinese art and economy. The disagreement is definitional: does Indianization require the Pollock-style Sanskrit cosmopolis, or only measurable Indian institutions? Confidence: high on classification, medium on cultural weight.
