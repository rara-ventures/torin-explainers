# Straits Archipelago — Kedah, Langkasuka, Temasek, Timor

This is a composite folder, not a single polity. It gathers four things that share a route rather than a dynasty: the Bujang Valley entrepôts of Kedah, Langkasuka and its Srivijayan overlord, the Singapore–Melaka succession, and the sandalwood coast of Timor. The subfiles exist so that a 535 BCE charcoal date and a 1511 Portuguese conquest are not forced into one continuous narrative.

## Why it matters

The Straits are the control case for Indianization as a commercial technology rather than a conquest. No Indian state ever held the peninsula; what arrived were scribes, monks, Brahmins, guilds and a vocabulary of kingship, carried on ships and paid for with tin, iron, forest goods and sandalwood. It isolates what was adopted: writing and ritual, not administration.

It also preserves a sequence in which Indian ritual sovereignty outlives Indian religion. Parameswara, whose name means "supreme Śiva," founded Melaka around 1400; within a generation his line had converted to Islam, yet the court kept Sanskrit regnal titles, the *undang-undang* code and the *shahbandar* offices. Indianization here is a layer, not an episode.

The folder is also a lesson in source types: Sumatra gives inscriptions, Kedah archaeology, Langkasuka and Timor Chinese notices, Singapore ceramics, and Melaka a chronicle written a century later. Each produces a different chronology, and their disagreements are the region's real historiography.

## Historical arc

**c. 535 BCE / c. 110 CE** — Sungai Batu has yielded iron workshops, a jetty and ritual sites. Charcoal from furnace SB2H gave 788–537 BCE, but the brick structure dates to c. 110 CE and Bayesian modelling favours the 2nd–10th century (Khaw and Gooi 2021). Iron, not Indian text, is the earliest sign of organised production.

**c. 100–200 CE** — *Kataha*/*Kadaram* enters Tamil and Greek geography; the *Paṭṭiṉappālai* names Kadaram, while Ptolemy's *Takola* is usually put on the Kra Isthmus. Tamil merchants likely carried the first Brahmi-derived writing.

**c. 500–515 CE** — The *Liangshu* describes Lang-ya-xiu, a walled kingdom whose ruler Bhagadatta sent envoys in 515, 523, 531 and 568. Yarang near Pattani is the likeliest centre (Wheatley 1961). Its tale of a founder who fled to India and married an Indian princess is a charter myth.

**671–686 CE** — Yijing spends six months at Shilifoshi (Palembang), reporting a thousand monks studying Sanskrit grammar. Old Malay inscriptions follow: Kedukan Bukit (683), Talang Tuwo (684), Kota Kapur (686). The script is Pallava-derived; the language is Malay.

**775** — The Ligor stele at Vat Sema Muang places the peninsula's ports under a Sailendra banner (Coedès 1968).

**c. 800–1200 CE** — More than fifty brick candi, with a Śaiva cult at Site 16 and Buddhist tablets at Sungai Mas (Murphy 2018). Tamil teachers supplied models; local masons built low stepped platforms, not South Indian towers.

**1025 and 1068 CE** — Rajendra I's fleet attacks Kadaram and Palembang and captures Sangrama Vijayottunggavarman. The raids break Srivijayan toll power but not the port complex, which shifts to Sungai Mas.

**c. 1300–1400 CE** — Temasek is a walled settlement of perhaps 85 hectares at Fort Canning, with palace terraces, imported ceramics and Indian carnelian (Miksic 2013). Wang Dayuan (1349) describes Danmaxi with Malay and Chinese residents. Sacked in 1398.

**c. 1400–1511 CE** — Melaka is founded around 1400–1402 by Parameswara; conversion to Islam follows c. 1414 through Pasai. Under Muzaffar and Mansur Shah it becomes the region's entrepôt, with Gujarati, Tamil and Chinese quarters. Albuquerque takes it in 1511.

**1225–1550 CE** — Timor appears as Ti-wu in Zhao Rugua (1225), Ti-men in the *Song shi* (1345), and Ku-li Ti-men in Wang Dayuan (c. 1350) — from Sanskrit *giri*, "mountain" (Ptak 1983). No candi, no script: only a Sanskrit toponym and a sandalwood chain to China, Java and Macao.

## Indian vs local

| Indian import | Straits adaptation |
|---|---|
| Pallava-derived script; Sanskrit as ritual language | Old Malay used for law, oath and narrative (683); Sanskrit kept for formulae and titles |
| Śaiva and Mahayana temple architecture | Low brick candi on square platforms; a round base at Sungai Batu carries a square shrine |
| Brahminical royal consecration | *Dapunta Hyang* rules as *ratu*; legitimacy binds through oath and curse, not genealogy |
| Nāga and water cosmology | Oath stones with nāga spouts; water poured over the inscription to bind witnesses |
| Indian Ocean merchant guilds | *Shahbandar* offices, Orang Laut patrols, separate Tamil, Gujarati and Chinese quarters |
| Sanskrit legal vocabulary | *Undang-undang Melaka* survives conversion; Arabic and Sanskrit terms coexist in Malay law |

## Debates & confidence

Sungai Batu's date is the sharpest dispute: a single outlier charcoal date of 788–537 BCE is widely publicised, but the brick monument dates to c. 110 CE and the Bayesian model to the 2nd–10th century (Khaw and Gooi 2021). Srivijaya's capital remains unresolved between Palembang, Muara Jambi and Chaiya, and no urban core has been excavated. Melaka's founding is given as 1400, 1402 or 1403, and whether Parameswara converted or his successor Megat Iskandar Shah did is still argued. Zhao Rugua's Ti-wu is probably Timor, but not certainly (Ptak 1983). The Bujang Valley's earliest-monument claim also depends on which structure is dated.
