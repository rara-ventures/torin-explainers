# Pagan (Myanmar)

Pagan (Bagan) was the capital of the first Burmese empire, which turned a small Irrawaddy settlement into a Theravada capital that built over 10,000 monuments. Its Indianization ran less through Indian colonists than through a relay: Pyu Buddhist culture, Mon script and clergy, and from the late 11th century Sri Lanka's Mahāvihāra lineage. Pagan is the sharpest mainland test of whether "Indianization" names an Indian presence or a Southeast Asian appropriation.

## At a glance

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Polity** | Pagan (Bagan) kingdom; *Arimaddanapura*, "foe-crushing city" |
| **Core area** | Irrawaddy dry zone: Pagan, Kyaukse (*Ledwin*) plain, Mandalay–Myingyan; Lower Burma ports after 1057 |
| **Script** | Pyu → Mon → Burmese alphabet (traditional 1058); Pali liturgy, Mon/Burmese donations |
| **State cult/religion** | Theravada from c. 1056; Tantric (*Ari*), Mahayana, Shaiva, Vaishnava and *nat* cults coexist |
| **Indianized?** | Yes — script, Pali canon, titulature, law, iconography; no Indian rule |

## Why it matters

Pagan breaks the standard Indianization model in three places. First, the vector is indirect. The Pallava-derived script reached Burma through Pyu and Mon intermediaries, the Pali canon arrived, traditionally, from Mon Thaton in 1057, and the authoritative ordination lineage came from Lanka after 1180. Little at Pagan is first-generation import: it is Indian material at two or three removes, reworked at each step — the strongest case for Wolters' "localization" reading, in which Indian forms are raw material for Southeast Asian projects, not evidence of Indian expansion (Wolters 1999).

Second, the content of Indianization is disproportionately Buddhist. Pagan adopted Sanskrit regnal titles and Brahmanical deities, but imperial legitimacy rested on Pali learning — the Tipiṭaka, the *dhammarāja* ideal, monastic lineage — not on a Shaiva *devarāja*. Where Angkor built a god-king and a baray-fed temple-mountain, Pagan built a canon-and-monastery state financed by tax-free land endowments. That contrast explains why Pagan, not Angkor, became the model for Sukhothai and Lan Na.

Third, Pagan is contested: Aung-Thwin's *Mists of Rāmañña* (2005) calls the Mon-relay narrative a colonial construction, which would relocate Pagan's Indianization into Pyu and direct Indian channels.

## Historical arc

**c. 2nd century BCE–9th century CE — Pyu substrate.** Walled Pyu cities — Beikthano, Halin, Sri Ksetra — adopt Brahmic writing by the 3rd–6th centuries, take Pali/Sanskrit names, and title their kings *varman*. Sri Ksetra yields the richest first-millennium Theravada material in Myanmar, alongside Mahayana and Hindu iconography (Stargardt 1990). Nanzhao raids in the 750s–830s weaken the Pyu; Burman speakers settle the Irrawaddy confluence in the 830s–840s and absorb Pyu culture.

**849 — foundation.** *Hmannan* has Pyinbya fortify Pagan, but radiocarbon dates the walls to c. 980 and the earliest royal inscription is 956: 849 is traditional, not archaeological (Aung-Thwin 1985).

**1044–1077 — Anawrahta.** Anawrahta's accession begins verifiable Burmese history. He secures the Kyaukse irrigation plain, then turns south. In 1056 the Mon monk Shin Arahan converts him; in 1057 Pagan takes Thaton, whose king Manuha reputedly withheld the canon. The chronicles have the Tipiṭaka carried north on white elephants; strategically the conquest secured the Tenasserim coast against Khmer advance (Lieberman 2003). In 1071 he sent monks and scriptures to Lanka, where Chola invasion had wrecked the sangha: the flow reversed.

**1084–1112/13 — Kyanzittha.** Kyanzittha's reign is the synthesis: Mon scholars at court, most royal epigraphs in Mon, the Ananda temple (begun c. 1090, completed 1105) built after monks described the Nandamula cave, the Shwezigon with its precinct for the thirty-seven *nats*, and the Myazedi stele (1112/13), giving the same text in Pyu, Mon, Burmese and Pali (Luce 1969). His title, Śrī Tribhuvanāditya Dhammarāja, is Sanskrit form carrying Pali content.

**1174–1211 — Sithu II and the Sihala reform.** Shin Uttarajiva's mission to Lanka (1180) and Shin Chapata's return from ten years at the Mahāvihāra (traditional date 1181) install a Sinhalese ordination lineage at Pagan. The result is a schism between the Former Order (Shin Arahan's lineage) and the Latter Order (Sinhalese), which the crown backed.

**1210s–1287 — collapse.** Ever-larger tax-free monastic grants erode crown revenue; Mongol pressure from Yunnan runs 1277–1287, Narathihapate flees and is killed, and the dynasty ends at Myinsaing in 1297.

## Indian vs local

| Indian import | Burmese adaptation |
|---|---|
| Pali Tipiṭaka and Mahāvihāra ordination | Canon via Mon Thaton (1057), re-ordination from Lanka (1180s); village Buddhism keeps *nat* and *Ari* practice for centuries |
| Sanskrit regnal titulature | Sanskrit title becomes Burmese; the *dhammarāja* ideal fuses with Pyu and Burman ancestral kingship |
| Brahmanical deities | Viṣṇu as Kyanzittha's patron; Indra becomes Thagyamin, king of the *nats*, at the Shwezigon |
| Indian temple forms (rock-cut cave, Gupta–Pala stūpa) | Ananda's "Nandamula cave" rebuilt in brick with Mon vaulting; the stūpa becomes the gilded *zedi* |
| Brahmic script | Pyu → Mon → Burmese alphabet, with added tonal markers; Mon and Burmese, not Sanskrit, carry donations |
| Dharmaśāstra | *Dhammasattha* in Pali and Burmese, first attested in a 13th-century Pagan inscription; local custom and *yazathat* override (Huxley 1997) |

## Debates & confidence

**Thaton.** Aung-Thwin (2005) finds no contemporary evidence of the 1057 sack, denies a substantial Mon polity there, and reads Mon influence as post-Pagan legend; Lieberman (2003) and Mon epigraphy support a Mon cultural zone. Confidence: medium.

**The "Ari" purge.** The scandalous *Ari* of the chronicles — liquor, sacrifice, *ius primae noctis* — are known from 18th–19th-century texts and may be a retrospective label for forest monks. Anawrahta's purge was partial at most: Ari monks were back at court by the late Pagan period.

**Theravada vs Mahayana.** Pagan was syncretic: Tantric, Shaiva and Vaishnava elements were stronger among elites than later orthodoxy admits, and "heretical" meant unfaithful to one's own scriptures, not non-Buddhist (Lieberman 2003).

**Chronology.** 849 is chronicle-derived and archaeology points to c. 980 for the walls; the Kalyani date is disputed between 1472 and 1476–79. Confidence: high on architecture, medium on narrative, low on conversion.
