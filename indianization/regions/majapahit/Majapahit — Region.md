# Majapahit & Successors (Java–Malay)

Majapahit (*Måjåpahit*, Sanskrit *Wilwatikta*) was the last and largest Hindu–Buddhist state of Java, centred at Trowulan from 1293 to the early sixteenth century. Indian script, titulature, law and temple form had been resident in Java for seven centuries, so Majapahit shows the mature Javanese form of Indianization. Its court produced the *Nagarakretagama* (1365); its collapse opened Java to Islam.

## At a glance

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Polity** | Majapahit (Wilwatikta), successor to Singhasari; *mancanagara* provinces under *bhre* lords |
| **Core area** | Trowulan and the lower Brantas; eastern Java, Madura, Bali |
| **Script** | Old Javanese (Kawi), Pallava-derived; Sanskrit for charters and titles |
| **State cult** | Śaiva–Buddhist together (Siwa-Buddha), twin state priests |
| **Indianized?** | Yes — script, titulature, *dharmaśāstra*, temple form; no Indian rule, no varna society |

## Why it matters

Majapahit is the best-documented test of Wolters's argument that Southeast Asians localized Indian material rather than receiving it (Wolters 1999).

First, the chronology is deep: by 1293 Javanese courts had used Pallava-derived script, Sanskrit *prasasti*, Śaiva and Buddhist foundations and *sīma* land grants for seven centuries. Majapahit is thus a late case, not a reception case: Sanskrit survives mainly as a lexicon of legitimacy — *śrī mahārāja*, *-wardhana*, *dharmmadhyakṣa* — while the working language of law, poetry and administration is Old Javanese.

Second, the evidence is unusually dense: a dated inscription series (1294–1486), a court poem describing the capital and its ceremonial calendar, Chinese eyewitness reports from Zheng He's voyages (Ma Huan 1970), and the Trowulan complex of canals, brick enclosures and workshops. Claims can be checked against more than one archive.

Third, the same courtly technologies — Sanskritic titulature, sacred genealogy, chronicle form, the ruler as *cakravartin* — reappear almost intact in the Islamic sultanate of Demak. Indianization supplied a repertoire of ruling techniques that outlived the religion which carried them.

## Historical arc

**1222** — Ken Arok of Tumapel defeats Kediri at Genter and founds Singhasari. The story is known only from the *Pararaton*, compiled between 1481 and 1600, and is heavily mythologized (Sastrawan 2020a).

**1268–1292** — Kertanagara expands: the Pamalayu expedition to Sumatra (1275) and a campaign against Bali (1284). He refuses Kublai Khan's tribute demand and mutilates the envoy Meng Qi (1289). In 1292 his vassal Jayakatwang of Kediri revolts, takes Kutaraja and kills him. Kertanagara's son-in-law Raden Wijaya withdraws to the Tarik forest and founds a settlement named, from the bitter *maja* fruit, Majapahit.

**1293** — A Yuan fleet of 20,000–30,000 under Shi Bi, Gao Xing and Yighmish arrives off Tuban to punish Singhasari. Finding it destroyed, the commanders accept Wijaya's alliance against Kediri; Daha falls in April. Wijaya then turns on his allies and drives them back to their ships. In November he is consecrated as Kertarajasa Jayawardhana.

**1309–1350** — Jayanagara is murdered in 1328; his half-sister Tribhuwana Wijayatunggadewi rules, with Gayatri Rajapatni as eminence. In 1336 she appoints Gajah Mada *mahāpatih*; his Palapa oath — to take no *palapa* until the archipelago is subdued — is recorded only in the *Pararaton*.

**1350–1389** — The reign of Hayam Wuruk (Rajasanagara) is the peak. In 1357 the Sundanese royal party is massacred at Bubat after a dispute over a marriage alliance, an episode suppressed in the court poem but central to *Kidung Sunda*. In 1365 Prapanca completes the *Nagarakretagama*, describing the capital and 98 tributary names from Sumatra to New Guinea (Robson 1995).

**1389–1429** — Wikramawardhana fights the Regreg war (often called Paregreg, 1404–1406) against the eastern court of Bhre Wirabhumi in Blambangan. The west wins, but the war drains the treasury and loosens control over overseas vassals; some 170 Chinese envoys were killed (Ricklefs 2008).

**1429–1527** — Suhita (1429–1447) is the last strong ruler. From 1468 the court splits: Kertabhumi holds Trowulan, Singhawikramawardhana withdraws to Daha. In 1478 Ranawijaya takes Trowulan and kills Kertabhumi; in 1527 Demak destroys the court at Daha. Courtiers, priests and artisans flee to Bali.

## Indian vs local

| Indian import | Javanese adaptation |
|---|---|
| Sanskrit titulature: *śrī mahārāja*, *-wardhana* | Sanskrit regnal names; power held through *bhre* kin-lordship over provinces |
| *Dharmaśāstra* legal literature | *Kutaramanawa Dharmashastra*: Old Javanese code of fines and compensation |
| Śaiva and Buddhist establishments | A single Siwa-Buddha cult, with twin Śaiva and Buddhist state priests |
| Indian temple-mountain and *prāsāda* form | Brick *candi* as mortuary shrine for a deified king, over *punden berundak* |
| Sanskrit *kāvya* metre and poetics | Old Javanese *kakawin*: Sanskrit prosody, Javanese landscape and genealogy |
| *Agrahāra* / *brahmadeya* land grants | *Sīma* freehold estates, inscribed and witnessed by village notables |

## Debates & confidence

The founding date floats between 1292 (settlement) and 1293 (consecration), and even the November day is insecure; it all rests on later Javanese texts plus the *Yuan shi*. The 1478/1527 question is less a dispute than a category error: 1478 is the *sudarma wisuta*, Ranawijaya's coup at Trowulan, while 1527 is the Demak conquest of Daha. Both derive from Śaka chronograms, not annals. The extent of the "empire" is the live argument: the *Nagarakretagama*'s 98 names are a eulogistic inventory, and Sastrawan (2020b) argues that direct administration barely extended beyond core Java. The Palapa oath survives only in the *Pararaton*, is dated 1334 or 1336, and may be a later literary construction; even *amukti palapa* is disputed between "enjoy pleasure" and "take rest."
