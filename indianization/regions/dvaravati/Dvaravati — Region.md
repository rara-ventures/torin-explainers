# Dvaravati (Central Thailand)

Dvaravati names a cluster of Mon-speaking Buddhist moated city-states in the lower Chao Phraya plain, c. 6th–11th centuries CE. It produced the earliest substantial body of Buddhist art, Pali epigraphy and urbanism in what is now Thailand, and the clearest case for reading Indianization as selective localization, not colonization. The name is a scholarly construct, attested mainly in a coin legend, and the "kingdom" was probably never a single centralized state.

## At a glance

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Polity** | Dvaravati — Mon moated city-states, not a unified kingdom |
| **Core area** | Lower Chao Phraya plain: Nakhon Pathom, U Thong, Suphan Buri, Khu Bua, Si Thep; extensions to the Khorat plateau and Lamphun |
| **Script** | Old Mon, Pali and Sanskrit in a Pallava-derived script |
| **State cult/religion** | Theravada Buddhism dominant; Mahayana and Shaiva/Vaishnava Hinduism co-present |
| **Indianized?** | Yes — script, Buddhism, iconography, titulature; no evidence of Indian rule |

## Why it matters

Dvaravati matters for three reasons. First, it is the hinge between the Indian Ocean Buddhist world and the later Tai states: its Mon script, Pali liturgy and Theravada monastic institutions are the substrate for Sukhothai, Ayutthaya and Lan Na. Second, it complicates the vector story. The older model routed Theravada through Sri Lanka; Dvaravati's art points instead to a strong Amaravati/Andhra connection, with Lankan influence arriving later and being over-read (Brown 1996; Revire 2014). Third, it is where "Indianization" is most visibly under construction: Cœdès treated Dvaravati as an Indianized state; later scholarship recast it as localization, with Indian forms rebuilt around local cults and merit economies.

The archaeology supports that: Indian script, Buddha types and the dharma-wheel were adopted, but settlement, pottery, stucco and urban plan remain Southeast Asian. Buddhism spread as a lay merit economy — thousands of stamped clay tablets and inscribed verses — not a royal conversion from above.

## Historical arc

**c. 4th–5th century — proto-Dvaravati.** Moated settlements appear while Iron Age occupation continues at Chansen and U Thong. Imported glass, beads and a Roman coin of Victorinus (269–71) reportedly from U Thong show these communities were tied into maritime exchange before any Indian-style monument (Murphy 2016; Landes 1982).

**c. 5th–7th century — the Buddhist horizon.** Dharmachakra pillars and *ye dhamma* inscriptions at Nakhon Pathom mark the earliest monumental Buddhism in Thailand, dated from the 5th to the 7th century (Dupont 1959; Brown 1996). Two Old Mon votive fragments from Wat Phra Rang, Nakhon Pathom (late 6th century), are the earliest written Mon in the country (Skilling 2003–04).

**Mid-7th century — external testimony.** Xuanzang places "To-lo-po-ti" between Isanapura (Chenla) and Sri Ksetra (Pyu); Yijing, a generation later, reports that the inhabitants of these countries revere the Three Jewels. These brief notices are the main near-contemporary descriptions of the polity (Cœdès 1968).

**7th–9th century — florescence.** Nakhon Pathom becomes a leading centre, its stupa now encased in Phra Pathom Chedi; Khu Bua yields narrative stucco of the Buddha's life and Jataka tales; Si Thep adds a twin moated city, over 112 monastery sites and a Mahayana cave monastery at Khao Thamorrat. Silver coins reading *śrī dvāravatī svarapuṇya* circulate in Pallava script. Theravada, Mahayana and Hinduism coexist (Boeles 1964; UNESCO 2023).

**8th–11th century — diffusion and pressure.** Mon culture extends north to Hariphunchai (Lamphun, founded c. 750) and east onto the Khorat plateau, marked by *sema* stones. From the 10th century Khmer power presses on Lavo (Lopburi), which falls in 1002; Suryavarman II campaigns in the 12th century. Dvaravati centres are subsumed rather than destroyed.

**13th century — afterlife.** Hariphunchai falls to Mangrai in 1292, extinguishing the last Mon polity in the north. Mon script, Pali learning and Theravada institutions pass to Sukhothai, Lan Na and Ayutthaya, the base layer of Tai Buddhist kingship.

## Indian vs local

| Indian import | Mon/Dvaravati adaptation |
|---|---|
| Dharmachakra from Amaravati/Andhra | Set on tall pillars at monastery and settlement entrances, becoming a civic emblem; spokes reworked in local stone |
| Pali *ye dhamma* stanza as dharma relic | Stamped on clay votive tablets and buried in stupas and images for mass merit-making (Skilling 2003–04) |
| Pallava-derived script | Old Mon for donations and land claims; Pali and Sanskrit for ritual; Mon script later fed Lan Na and Thai writing |
| Gupta–Amaravati Buddha iconography | Broad face, full lips, both hands in *vitarka mudra*; Khu Bua stucco adds local dress, musicians and boats |
| Indian stupa (terraced base and *anda*) | Laterite and brick stepped-pyramid chedis (Wat Chamadevi, Khao Klang Nok), sited by local cosmology |
| Sanskrit titulature (*śrī dvāravatī*) | Used on silver coinage as a merit formula, not in a royal genealogy; kingship stays epigraphically invisible |

## Debates & confidence

Three things remain open. **Chronology:** the 6th–11th-century frame is pushed back by "proto-Dvaravati" sequences from the 4th–5th century (Murphy 2016), and the Nakhon Pathom wheels are dated anywhere from the 5th to the 8th century. **Political form:** Mudar's locational analysis of moated sites found no clear settlement hierarchy, arguing against a single kingdom and for competing centres; "Dvaravati" may be an art-historical label rather than an emic polity name, since it is attested mainly on coins. **Religion:** Theravada is usually called the state cult, but Revire (2014) shows only that laypeople and monasteries were Buddhist by the 7th–8th century, not that kings were; Mahayana and Hindu material is substantial. Confidence is high on material culture, medium on chronology, low on dynastic narrative.
