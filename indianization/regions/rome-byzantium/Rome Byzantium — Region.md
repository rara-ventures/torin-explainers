# Rome & Byzantium — Erythraean Sea to Oc Eo

Rome is the vault's consumer terminus: the Mediterranean market that bought Indian pepper, nard and gems with gold but adopted no Indic script, law, kingship or cult. Its Southeast Asian relevance is indirect: Roman coin types and gold bracteates reached Oc Eo through Indian and Indianized intermediaries, a relay rather than a Roman presence.

## At a glance

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Polity** | Roman Empire (30 BCE–476 CE), then Byzantine; in the Indian Ocean a diaspora of Alexandrian merchants and Red Sea ports |
| **Core area** | Mediterranean; gateways at Alexandria, Coptos, Myos Hormos, Berenike, Clysma |
| **Script** | Greek and Latin; no Indic script adopted, though Tamil-Brahmi sherds occur at Berenike and Pattanam |
| **Religion** | Imperial cult, then Christianity; sixth-century Nestorian communities in Malabar and Sri Lanka |
| **Indianized?** | No — relay/consumer; adopts none of the four markers, and trade alone is not Indianization |

## Why it matters

Rome is the thesis's control case. If pepper and gold made a society Indianized, Rome would be the largest Indianized polity on earth: it ran a state-supported monsoon fleet, kept an Alexandria pepper depot, and sent bullion east in quantities Pliny thought catastrophic. Instead it kept Latin law, Greek paideia and its own gods. It consumed India without becoming India.

That separates commodity traffic from the transmission of script, dharma, kingship and cult, the vault's bright line ([[Indianization Sources]]). It also explains why Roman objects appear so far east. The gold bracteates of Antoninus Pius and Marcus Aurelius at Oc Eo are not evidence of Roman ships in the Gulf of Thailand; they show Roman prestige travelling inside the Indianized Funan network, as bullion, heirloom or local imitation, deposited as elite ornament (Malleret 1962; Borell 2008). Oc Eo records a relay at two removes: Rome, India, Funan.

Roman issues also fix termini post quem in Southeast Asian layers, measuring local demand for gold.

## Historical arc

**30 BCE.** Augustus annexes Egypt; Strabo reports 120 ships a year from Myos Hormos for India against "a very few" under the Ptolemies (Strabo 2.5.12); the Hippalus monsoon now sets the schedule.

**c. 40–70 CE.** The *Periplus Maris Erythraei*, by a Greek merchant of Roman Egypt, runs from Myos Hormos to Muziris, where the first import listed is "a great quantity of coin"; exports are pepper, malabathrum, pearls, ivory, nard and gems (Periplus 56; Casson 1989). The text knows the Ganges, Chryse and Thinae but thins past the Bay of Bengal: Chryse is "the last part of the inhabited world toward the east"; silk reaches India overland via Bactria or down the Ganges (Periplus 63–64).

**c. 150 CE.** The Muziris papyrus (P.Vindob. G 40822) preserves a loan for a voyage to Muziris and the valuation of three-quarters of the *Hermapollon*'s return cargo: 1,154 talents 2,852 drachmas of silver, including 60 boxes of Gangetic nard at 4,500 drachmas each and 85 talents of ivory (Rathbone 2000; De Romanis 2020). The only account of a single voyage, it shows credit, insurance and tax.

**77 CE.** Pliny puts the annual drain to India, the Seres and Arabia at HS 100 million, "what our luxuries and women cost us" (Pliny, *NH* 6.101). The figure is rhetorical; Tamil Nadu gold hoards confirm the direction.

**166 CE.** The *Hou Hanshu* records envoys of "Andun" (Marcus Aurelius Antoninus) arriving through Rinan with ivory, rhinoceros horn and tortoise shell (Hou Hanshu 88.12) — Southeast Asian goods, so probably merchants, not envoys.

**c. 138–180 CE, deposited later.** Two gold medallions, of Antoninus Pius and probably Marcus Aurelius, reach Oc Eo with Mediterranean-style jewellery and glass (Higham 2014: 279; Young 2001: 29); deposition is 2nd–3rd century and the route Indian, not Roman.

**c. 300–550 CE.** After the third-century crisis and the solidus reform, trade ran through Aksum, Himyar and Sri Lanka (Turner 1989). Cosmas Indicopleustes calls Taprobane the central mart where ships from India, Persia and Ethiopia meet, receiving silk, aloes, cloves and sandalwood from Tzinista (Cosmas, *Top.* XI; McCrindle 1897). His Sopatrus story has a Ceylonese king prefer the Roman nomisma to the Persian miliarision: "pieces of this kind are picked for export to the island."

**552–562 CE.** Procopius reports monks smuggling silkworm eggs from Serinda, breaking the Sasanian monopoly; the 562 treaty regulates silk at Callinicum and Nisibis (Procopius, *Wars* VIII.17; Menander fr. 6.1).

## Indian vs local

| Indian import | Roman/Byzantine use |
|---|---|
| Pepper, malabathrum, nard | Cooking, medicine, perfume; nard in unguents, pepper in Apicius |
| Ivory | Consular diptychs, furniture inlay, pyxides, Roman iconography |
| Cotton cloth, silk yarn | Finished, re-dyed and rewoven in Mediterranean workshops |
| Beryl, sapphire, diamond, pearl | Cut and set in Roman jewellery and church treasure |
| Tortoise shell | Veneer and inlay for boxes and couches |
| Buddhist teaching, via Alexandria | Clement names the Buddha among barbarian sages; no cult follows |

## Debates & confidence

**Oc Eo's Roman material.** Malleret's finds include two gold medallions and jewellery "from or inspired by" the Mediterranean (Higham 2014). Borell (2008) questioned the dates and identification of several; some pieces are local imitations. Direct Roman presence is not demonstrated. Confidence: high that Roman-type gold reached Oc Eo; medium on date and route; low on Romans there.

**Muziris.** Pattanam has yielded a wharf, Roman amphorae and terra sigillata, but the identification is contested; Kodungallur remains a rival (De Romanis 2020). Confidence: medium-high for a Roman-period port; medium for the name.

**Scale of trade.** Pliny's HS 100 million is a moralist's figure; the papyrus values one cargo. Whether most ran direct or via Sri Lankan and Arab intermediaries is unresolved. Confidence: high on the network, low on volumes.
