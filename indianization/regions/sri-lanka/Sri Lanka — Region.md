# Sri Lanka — Theravada Conservatory

Sri Lanka is the Indianization case that runs backwards. Settled and scripted from northern India in the mid-first millennium BCE, it took Buddhism as a Mauryan mission, then spent fifteen centuries hardening that inheritance into a canon, a monastic rule and a royal ideology — and exported all three after Indian Buddhism had collapsed. It is a conservatory, not a colony: Indian forms survived because they were localized, endowed and institutionalized.

## At a glance

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Polity** | Anuradhapura (c. 377 BCE–1017 CE) → Polonnaruwa (1017–1232); Chola occupation 1017–1070 |
| **Core area** | Dry zone (*Rajarata*): Anuradhapura, Polonnaruwa, the Mahatittha port; tank-cascade irrigation |
| **Script** | Early Brahmi → Sinhala; Pali and Sanskrit written in Sinhala letterforms |
| **State cult/religion** | Theravada (Mahavihara); Abhayagiri and Jetavana carried Mahayana and Vajrayana; Hindu *devale* cults |
| **Indianized?** | Yes — **conservatory**: Indian script, canon and kingship preserved and re-exported; no lasting Indian rule |

## Why it matters

Sri Lanka is the Theravada factory: it made Theravada a Southeast Asian religion. Between the 11th and 15th centuries the Mahavihara ordination lineage, the Pali Tipitaka and its commentaries were carried from the island to Pagan, Martaban, Sukhothai, Lan Na and Pegu; every Theravada polity in the region traces its *upasampada* to a Lankan re-ordination (Cūḷavaṃsa; Gombrich 2006). The mechanism was scarcity, not conquest: as Buddhist institutions in India shrank, the island held the only intact, royally endowed, textually fixed version of the tradition.

The canon itself is a Lankan artifact. The Tipitaka was preserved orally for three centuries, then written down at Aluvihare in the 1st century BCE; the commentarial tradition was translated from Sinhala into Pali by Buddhaghosa in the 5th century CE. What Burma and Thailand received as the word of the Buddha was therefore already edited, closed and glossed in Sri Lanka.

It is the strongest test of that framing. Indianization here was neither a veneer over an untouched culture nor a colonial implant: a wholesale import became the local culture, then was re-exported as orthodoxy. The vector reverses after c. 1100, and later mainland history works out that reversal.

## Historical arc

**c. 250 BCE — the mission.** The *Mahavamsa* places Mahinda, son of Ashoka, at Mihintale in the reign of Devanampiya Tissa, converting the king and founding the Mahavihara on the donated Mahamegha park (Mahāvaṃsa 1912; de Silva 2005). The traditional date is 246 BCE. Sanghamitta follows with a Bodhi sapling and the bhikkhuni order. The 3rd-century horizon is secure: the earliest inscriptions are in Brahmi and a Magadhi-like Prakrit.

**c. 200 BCE–300 CE — the hydraulic capital.** Anuradhapura becomes a planned city of tanks and relic mounds. Dutthagamani (r. c. 161–137 BCE) builds the Ruwanwelisaya. Under Mahasena (273–301) the Mahavihara is sacked and the Jetavana built, a third fraternity beside Mahavihara and Abhayagiri (Bandaranayake 1974).

**89 BCE — the canon written.** Famine and South Indian war threaten the reciters; monks at Aluvihare in Matale commit the Tipitaka to ola leaves. The *Dipavamsa* dates it to Vattagamani's reign (29–17 BCE), a discrepancy unresolved (Norman 1983).

**c. 310–410 CE — cosmopolis.** The Tooth Relic arrives from Kalinga and is enshrined at the Abhayagiri. Faxian, resident c. 410–411, counts 5,000 monks at the Abhayagiri, 3,000 at the Mahavihara and 2,000 at the Cetiyapabbata, and carries off a Sanskrit Mahīśāsaka Vinaya — the island was a clearing-house for several schools, not a Theravada monoculture (Hirakawa 1993). Buddhaghosa then re-Pali-izes the commentaries at the Mahavihara.

**993–1070 — conquest and reversal.** Rajaraja I and Rajendra I take Anuradhapura; the capital moves to Polonnaruwa under Chola rule, and Shaiva temples and sharper caste practice arrive with the occupiers. Vijayabahu I expels the Cholas by 1070 and, finding the sangha decimated, imports monks from Ramanna (Burma) to restore ordination — the first reversal of the flow (Cūḷavaṃsa).

**1153–1186 — the purification.** Parakramabahu I reunifies the island, rebuilds the irrigation network around the Parakrama Samudra, and convenes a council c. 1165 under Mahathera Kassapa. The Abhayagiri and Jetavana fraternities are abolished and their Mahayana books banned; Lankan Mahayana never recovers (Gombrich 2006). The unified Mahavihara church that emerged is the body Pagan and Sukhothai later imported.

## Indian vs local

| Indian import | Lankan transformation |
|---|---|
| Early Brahmi script and Magadhi-type Prakrit | Evolves into Elu and Sinhala with Grantha and Kadamba letterforms; Pali written in Sinhala script, so the canon's physical form is Lankan |
| Pali Tipitaka and Mahavihara ordination | Fixed in writing at Aluvihare; Sinhala commentaries re-translated into Pali by Buddhaghosa — the canon is as much Lankan as Indian |
| Stupa and vihara monasticism | Brick dagobas on square terraces, plus the *sandakada pahana* moonstone and guardstone idiom unknown in India (Bandaranayake 1974) |
| Relic veneration | The Tooth Relic becomes the palladium of kingship; Parakramabahu I retrieves it in 1157 to secure accession |
| Brahmanical deities | Absorbed as *devale* cults beneath the Buddha; Vishnu protects the dharma and the island, not a royal ancestor |

## Debates & confidence

**The Mahinda mission.** 246 BCE is back-calculated from a contested parinirvana date, and Ashoka's edicts name a mission to Tambapanni but never Mahinda or Sanghamitta. The 3rd-century transmission is well supported; the persons and year are chronicle construction (de Silva 2005). Confidence: high on the horizon, low on the date.

**Mahavamsa partisanship.** The chronicle is a Mahavihara document that omits or disparages the Abhayagiri, and its "heresy" language marks rival fraternities, not non-Buddhists. Geiger's trust has given way to reading it as royal and monastic apologetic (von Hinüber 1996). Confidence: high on events, medium on motive.

**The nikaya conflicts.** The fraternities were royal factions as much as doctrinal ones; Parakramabahu's purification was also centralization, and extinguished Lankan Mahayana. Confidence: medium.
