# Sukhothai–Ayutthaya (Siam)

Sukhothai (1238–1438) and Ayutthaya (1351–1767) turned the Chao Phraya basin from a Khmer mandala zone into a Siamese Buddhist polity. Their Indianization was second-hand: a Brahminical court package inherited through Khmer Lavo, and a Sri Lankan Theravada lineage arriving via Mon Martaban and Nakhon Si Thammarat. No Indian power ruled; script, law and monastic order were rebuilt around Tai kingship.

| **State cult/religion** | Theravada (Lankavamsa) with Brahminical court ritual |
| **Indianized?** | Yes — script, Dharmasastra law, Brahmin coronation, Pali canon; no Indian rule |

## Why it matters

Siam is the clearest case of Indianization by relay rather than contact. The Khmer package reached the Tai through Lavo; the Buddhist package through Martaban and the peninsula port of Nakhon Si Thammarat. Each leg reworked the material, making the region the strongest support for Wolters' "localization" model and the strongest test of Mabbett's scepticism about Indian agency (Wolters 1999; Mabbett 1977).

It is also where "Indianization" and "Theravada" visibly diverge. Ayutthaya's law and coronation are Brahminical in form, its legitimacy Buddhist in content; the two sit in one court without merging (Tambiah 1976; Wales 1931). That arrangement outlasted the Indian Ocean network that supplied it, and survived into the Bangkok period.

Finally, the region is a historiographical fault line. The Sukhothai "golden age" was built by Prince Damrong in the 1910s–20s on a single stele, found by Mongkut in 1833 and now the most contested document in Thai history (Chamberlain 1991).

## Historical arc

**1238 — foundation.** Pho Khun Bang Klang Hao (Si Inthrathit) takes Sukhothai from its Lavo-Khmer governor and founds the Phra Ruang dynasty. Khmer monuments already stand there — Ta Pha Daeng, Wat Phra Phai Luang, Wat Si Sawai — so the Tai inherit a Hindu-Buddhist landscape (Cœdès 1968).

**1279–1298 — Ramkhamhaeng.** Campaigns reach Nakhon Si Thammarat, Martaban and Luang Prabang; embassies go to Yuan China from 1282. The stele describes a paternal ruler who hangs a bell for petitioners, protects the Tipitaka and invents a script in 1283; its text is dated 1292. The Lankan lineage link comes later, from the *Jinakalamali*, not the stele (Griswold & Prasert 1971).

**1298–1361 — contraction and reform.** Martaban detaches in 1319 and Lan Na takes Tak in 1321. Then Sumana Thera returns from Martaban, where Wareru's Mon kingdom had installed Sinhalese ordination, and founds the Lankavamsa at Wat Pa Mamuang. Li Thai (r. 1347–1368) builds a *Devalayamahaksetra*, a Brahmin shrine, in the same monastery, casts Shiva and Vishnu bronzes, and is ordained in 1361 — the first Siamese king to enter the sangha temporarily (Baker & Phongpaichit 2017).

**1351 — Ayutthaya.** Ramathibodi I (U Thong) founds the capital at the Lopburi–Chao Phraya confluence, names it after Ayodhya, and draws Lavo and Suphan Buri into his coalition. He is credited with the Thammasat, a Dharmasastra recast in Pali-Buddhist terms; caste was not adopted (Baker & Phongpaichit 2016).

**1378–1438 — absorption.** Sukhothai becomes a tributary, then is annexed in 1438. Its nobles and monks move south and reshape the victor; Borommatrailokkanat's reforms (1448–1488) produce the palace law and the *cakravartin* ideal (Baker & Phongpaichit 2017).

**1569–1584 — Burmese vassalage.** Bayinnaung takes Ayutthaya in 1569 and installs Maha Thammarachathirat; Naresuan reasserts independence in 1584; the city becomes one of Asia's great entrepôts.

**1767 — fall.** The Konbaung besiege the city for fourteen months; it falls in April 1767, ending 417 years. The successor capitals are Thonburi and Bangkok.

## Indian vs local

| Indian import | Tai/Siamese adaptation |
|---|---|
| Thammasat (Dharmasastra, Manu) | Recut as Pali-Buddhist *dhammasattha*: Manu kept as ancestral authority, but merit, royal edict and Thai custom govern; no caste |
| Brahmin court rites | Brahmins officiate inside a Buddhist court; the king is lustrated but legitimated by relics and Pali texts |
| Brahmi script via Old Khmer | *Lai Sue Thai* (1283) re-cuts Khmer letterforms for Tai phonology and drops the above/below vowel marks |
| Devaraja / linga cult | Linga cult not adopted. Ayutthaya's king is a Rama/Vishnu avatar and *cakravartin*, but also a *dhammaraja*; the relic outranks the god-king |
| Pali Tipitaka and Mahavihara ordination | Canon and lineage arrive via Nakhon Si Thammarat and Martaban, not directly from Lanka |

## Debates & confidence

**The stele.** Vickery's 1987 paper, published as "A Piltdown Skull of Southeast Asian History?", attacked script, vocabulary and content; Piriya Krairiksh argued Mongkut forged it. A 1990 SEM-EDS analysis dated it with four other Sukhothai inscriptions; most specialists accept it as partly authentic. Confidence: low on narrative detail, high on the script's Old Khmer derivation (Chamberlain 1991).

**Sukhothai vs Ayutthaya.** National historiography made Sukhothai the first Thai kingdom and a golden age opposed to "Khmer-influenced" Ayutthaya. Baker and Phongpaichit treat Ayutthaya as the state that formed Siam, and Sukhothai as a small mandala promoted retroactively.

**Indianization, Theravada, China.** Cœdès' frame fits the script and law but not the period after 1300, when the dominant filter is Lankan Theravada and the Indian element is largely Khmer-mediated. China supplied trade and tribute models, not cosmology. Confidence: high on direction, low on dating.
