# Persia — Sasanian Gate (Ctesiphon & Sogdian Network)

Persia is the vault's clearest relay: an empire that handled Indian goods, books and commercial vocabulary without adopting an Indic script, dharma, kingship or cult. Its gates were Ctesiphon, where Sanskrit learning became Pahlavi, and the Sogdian network from Samarkand to Dunhuang. What reached Baghdad and the Mediterranean was usually a Persian recension.

## At a glance

| Field | Value |

| **Religion** | Zoroastrianism as state cult; Manichaeism, Nestorian Christianity, Judaism; Buddhism in Sogdian zones |
| **Indianized?** | No — relay/filter. Adopts fewer than two of script, dharma, Indic kingship, Buddhist/Śaiva state cult |

## Why it matters

Persia separates Indian influence from Indianization. The Achaemenids ruled Hindush and their Aramaic chancellery supplied the model for Kharosthi in Gandhara (Salomon 1998), yet Persia administered Indians rather than becoming Indic. The Sasanians taxed pepper and indigo, welcomed Indian physicians and commissioned translations, while keeping Middle Persian as the court language and Zoroastrian law as law.

The textual chain shows the filter. The Pañcatantra became Pahlavi in the sixth century, then Arabic under Ibn al-Muqaffa around 750; the Arabic fed Syriac, Greek, Hebrew and Latin versions (Riedel 2010). The Sanskrit and Pahlavi originals are lost, so Europe met India through a Persian voice. Aramaic gave Kharosthi its skeleton; Sanskrit gave Sogdian its words for caravan, price, measure and letter (Sims-Williams 1985). Persia took techniques and terms, not allegiance.

If trade alone produced Indianization, Sasanian Iraq would look like Funan. It does not, and that negative case sets the threshold for the core zone.

## Historical arc

**c. 520–486 BCE** — Darius I lists Hindush (Hiⁿdūš) at Naqsh-e Rostam; Behistun sets the trilingual imperial style. Aramaic is the chancellery script, and from it Kharosthi is adapted to an Indian language in Achaemenid Gandhara (Salomon 1998). Herodotus puts the Indian satrapy in the twentieth tax district, paying gold dust (Herodotus 3.91–94).

**247 BCE–224 CE** — Parthia rules from Merv and Hecatompylos and taxes overland traffic between India, Central Asia and Syria. Chinese sources remember its Buddhist translators, yet Parthia stayed Zoroastrian: a relay carrying a religion without adopting it.

**224–276 CE** — Ardashir I founds the Sasanian dynasty; Shapur I (r. 242–272) claims Sogdia and the Kushan lands at the Ka'ba-ye Zartosht. After taking Antioch in 256 and 260, he settles deportees at Gundeshapur, "Better than Antioch of Shapur," a royal residence and metropolitan see (Shahbazi and Richter-Bernburg 2002). Mani, who had travelled to India, dedicates his Middle Persian *Shabuhragan* to Shapur; Manichaean teaching makes the Buddha an apostle, a prophet in an Iranian cosmos, not a monk.

**312–13 CE** — Sogdian merchants at Dunhuang write the Ancient Letters, the oldest substantial Sogdian texts. They report a Samarkand-to-Luoyang diaspora, mention "100 freemen from Samarkand," and use Sanskrit loanwords for caravan (*sārtha*), price (*mūlya*), letter (*lekhya*) (Sims-Williams 1985; Henning 1948).

**531–579 CE** — Khosrow I centralises the empire, patronises Gundeshapur and sends Borzuya to India. Borzuya returns with the Pañcatantra; the Pahlavi *Kalila wa Dimna* is dated to the 540s–570s, its preface his spiritual autobiography (Khaleghi-Motlagh 1989; de Blois 1990).

**651–900 CE** — Yazdegerd III falls to the Arabs, but the Sogdian network survives: Samarkand converts slowly, Sogdian remains the lingua franca into the eighth century, and a *Kalila wa Dimna* scene is painted at Panjikent c. 740. Ibn al-Muqaffa's Arabic *Kalila* (c. 750) becomes a mirror for princes; in the 770s the Sindhind reaches al-Mansur's court, and the Abbasid translation movement absorbs Pahlavi, Sanskrit and Greek into a new canon (Gutas 1998).

## Indian vs local

| Indian import | Persian transformation |
|---|---|
| Pañcatantra, a Sanskrit *nīti* text | *Kalila wa Dimna*: Pahlavi c. 540–570 with Borzuya's autobiography inserted; Ibn al-Muqaffa's Arabic adds the trial of Dimna |
| The Buddha as teacher | Mani's *Shabuhragan* places the Buddha among the apostles; Sogdian and Manichaean texts carry a Buddha who is a prophet in an Iranian cosmos |
| Sanskrit commercial vocabulary | Sogdian adopts *sārtha* "caravan", *prastha* (a measure), *mūlya* "price", *lekhya* "letter", keeping an Aramaic-derived script |
| Indian astronomy (*siddhānta*) | The Sindhind arrives at Baghdad in the 770s and becomes an Arabic *zij*; Indian cyclic time survives, the Sanskrit apparatus does not |
| Indian medicine | Attributed to Gundeshapur and Indian physicians at court, transmitted through Syriac Christians; the "academy" is largely an Abbasid retrojection |

## Debates & confidence

**Borzuya's date.** The voyage is placed between the 540s and 570s; no contemporary record fixes it. De Blois (1990) treats the preface as literary construction; Khaleghi-Motlagh (1989) accepts Borzuya's journey but suggests Bozorgmehr made the Pahlavi translation. Confidence: high on the translation's existence, low on date and biography.

**The Sogdian letters.** Henning's 312–13 date, based on the sack of Luoyang, is standard; Harmatta argued for the 190s and Fujieda for a sixth-century paper date. Sims-Williams (1985) calls Henning's dating generally accepted but not proven. Confidence: medium-high.

**Extent of Indian influence.** The Indian core of the *Kalila* is clear; the scale of Persian and Arabic addition is not. Mani's Buddha is a prophet in an Iranian system, not evidence of an Indian cult. Gundeshapur's medical school rests mainly on later Arabic writers (Shahbazi and Richter-Bernburg 2002). Confidence: high on the relay, medium on transmission, low on institutions.
