# Mainland SE Asia — Composite Region

This folder is a composite, not a polity. It covers the mainland states that took Indian script, kingship and cult — Funan/Chenla, Dvaravati, Angkor, Sukhothai/Ayutthaya and Champa — alongside Dai Viet, which took Buddhism through China. Its purpose is comparative: to hold Indianized and Sinicized trajectories in one frame without merging Champa with its conqueror.

| **Indianized?** | Mixed — all but Dai Viet meet the ≥2 test; Dai Viet is a Sinic relay |

## Why it matters

Mainland Southeast Asia is the best laboratory for the Indianization question: it supplies both treatment and control. The treatment is the Funan/Chenla → Angkor and Dvaravati → Sukhothai/Ayutthaya sequence, where Sanskrit, Pallava-derived script, Brahmanical kingship and Buddhist monasticism were adopted without Indian rule. The control is Dai Viet, which inherited Chinese characters, Confucian bureaucracy and Chinese-filtered Thiền, and still destroyed Vijaya in 1471. Reading them together prevents two errors: treating "Indianization" as a single package, and treating it as a synonym for civilization.

Indianization was also layered rather than replaced. Angkor's Theravada turn kept Brahmin officiants; Ayutthaya combined Brahmin coronation with Pali Buddhism; Champa's Śaiva cult outlived its polity in Panduranga. Script is the most durable trace: Old Khmer, Old Mon, Old Cham and Thai descend from Pallava-type Brahmi, while Vietnamese retained Chinese characters into the 20th century. The contrast is less a clash of civilizations than two filters, Indian and Chinese, acting on societies that selected what they wanted (Wolters 1982; Pollock 2006).

## Historical arc

**c. 1st–6th century — Funan.** Envoys Kang Tai and Zhu Ying (mid-3rd c.) report Mekong-delta ports centred on Óc Eo in the India–China trade. The Kaundinya–Soma myth, Sanskrit seals and Vishnu/Shiva images form the earliest dense Indic assemblage in mainland Southeast Asia (Cœdès 1968).

**mid-5th century — Vat Phou.** The K.365 stele of Devanika at Lingaparvata (southern Laos) records a king from afar founding a sacred tīrtha and a Bhadreśvara foundation at Shrestapura. It maps Indian cosmology onto a local lingam-shaped mountain — the clearest case of a landscape redescribed in Sanskrit.

**c. 550–802 — Chenla to Angkor.** Chinese sources call Funan's successor Chenla; Isanapura and Shrestapura were probably rival principalities. In 605 a Sui army sacked Linyi and removed its archives. In 802 Jayavarman II consecrated the devaraja on Phnom Kulen, later Khmer tradition's founding act.

**c. 6th–11th century — Dvaravati.** Mon-speaking moated cities — U Thong, Nakhon Pathom, Khu Bua, Si Thep — yielded the earliest substantial Buddhist art and Pali epigraphy in Thailand. Dharmachakra pillars and ye dhamma tablets indicate a lay merit economy, not a royal conversion. Xuanzang places a Buddhist "To-lo-po-ti" between Chenla and Sri Ksetra.

**802–1431 — Angkor.** Suryavarman II built Angkor Wat (1113–50); Jayavarman VII answered the Cham sack of 1177 with Angkor Thom and the Bayon (1181–1218). Zhou Daguan found Theravada monks and Pali texts there in 1296–97. Ayutthaya sacked the capital in 1431, by which time the court had largely converted.

**1238–1438 — Sukhothai and Ayutthaya.** Sukhothai was founded in 1238. Ramkhamhaeng's stele claims a Lankan-derived Theravada and a Thai script (c. 1292); the 1361 Sihala ordination from Martaban strengthened the forest lineage. Ayutthaya (1351) combined Brahmin coronation with Pali Buddhism and the Thammasat, absorbing Sukhothai in 1438.

**192–1471 — Champa and Dai Viet.** Linyi emerged in 192; the Vo Canh stele and Mỹ Sơn sanctuaries follow by the 4th century. Đồng Dương (875) was a Mahayana capital; Lê Hoàn's 982 campaign ended it as a royal centre. Dai Viet, independent from 939, took Chinese characters, Confucian bureaucracy and Chinese-filtered Thiền at Thăng Long (1010). Lê Thánh Tông destroyed Vijaya in 1471; Panduranga survived until 1832.

## Indian vs local

| Indian import | Mainland adaptation |
|---|---|
| Sanskrit and Pallava-derived script | Old Khmer, Old Mon, Old Cham and Thai for administration; Sanskrit kept for ritual |
| Devaraja and cakravartin | God-king fused with local nak ta, naga and ancestor cults; legitimacy tied to territory |
| Temple-mountain (Meru) | Khmer baray and brick prasat; Mon/Thai chedi; Cham brick kalan for the lineage linga |
| Pali canon and Theravada monasticism | Lay merit economy of votive tablets; forest-monk lineages patronised by courts |
| Dharmashastra and Manusmriti | Ayutthaya's Thammasat and local customary law; Brahmin jurists beside Buddhist monks |

## Debates & confidence

The category is contested. Cœdès (1968) described a diffusion of Indian civilization; Mabbett (1977) questioned Indian agency; Wolters (1982) and Pollock (2006) reframed the process as localization and vernacularization. Confidence is high that Indic script, kingship and cult were adopted, low on who carried them — Brahmins, traders, monks or kings. Dates are weaker: Vo Canh ranges from the 2nd to the 4th century; Linyi's identity with Champa is disputed (Vickery 2005); "Dvaravati" is mainly a coin legend; the Ramkhamhaeng stele is widely treated as a later composition. The 1471 figure of 50,000 captives comes from Vietnamese annals, unverified.
