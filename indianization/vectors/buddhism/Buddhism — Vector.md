# Vector: Buddhism — Theravada / Mahayana / Vajrayana

Buddhism entered Southeast Asia as institutions, texts and rituals carried by monks and merchants, and became its most consequential Indian import. Its arc runs from Vo Canh and Dvaravati to the Mahayana courts of Srivijaya, Java and Champa, then to the Sinhalese Theravada reformation after the eleventh century.

## At a glance

## Why it matters

Buddhism gives the densest evidence for how Indian forms crossed the Bay of Bengal. Brahmanism left temples and inscriptions; Buddhism left those plus a canon, ordination lineages, pilgrimage itineraries and dated charters naming donors and teachers. No other vector offers so continuous a record.

It also breaks the older Indianization model. The vector was rarely an Indian initiative: Southeast Asian rulers endowed monasteries, sent monks to Nalanda and Lanka, and chose their texts. A Sumatran king endowed a Nalanda monastery in 860; a Sumatran scholar, Dharmakirti, taught Atisha (Sen 2003). Influence ran outward as well as inward.

Buddhism also explains state formation. Where Angkor rested on a Shaiva *devaraja*, Pagan and Sukhothai rested on the Tipitaka, monastic land grants and the *dhammaraja* ideal (Wolters 1999). The eleventh-century shift to Sri Lankan Theravada reoriented the mainland away from India, so "Indianization" fits the later period badly.

## How it traveled

**Theravada: Amaravati to Dvaravati, Lanka to Pagan to Sukhothai.** The earliest Pali-based Buddhism in the mainland belongs to Mon Dvaravati (c. 6th–11th centuries): *dharmacakra* stones, Pali inscriptions and monasteries. Brown (1996) reads the wheels of the law as Buddhist appropriations of Indian models, closest to Amaravati; Chinese pilgrims knew the region as To-lo-po-ti. This was not yet "Theravada" in a sectarian sense (Skilling 1997). The decisive turn was Lankan: the Mahavihara lineage reached Pagan, where Shin Arahan converted Anawrahta (traditional 1056) and Chapata's return (1181) installed Sinhalese ordination. From Pagan and Nakhon Si Thammarat it spread to Sukhothai.

**Mahayana: Nalanda to Srivijaya to the Sailendras.** Srivijaya, at Palembang, was the hinge. Yijing, who stayed in 671, found over a thousand monks studying "as in Madhyadesa" and told Chinese pilgrims to train there first. Talang Tuwo (684) records Jayanasa's garden and a prayer for a vajra-body. Dharmakirti of Suvarnadvipa taught there; Atisha studied with him c. 1011–1023. The Sailendras drew on the same network: Kalasan (778) records a Tara temple, Kelurak (782) a Manjusri, and Borobudur rose c. 780–840. Balaputra's Nalanda endowment (860) shows traffic both ways.

**Vajrayana: Pala Bengal to Java and Champa.** Nalanda and Vikramasila supplied texts, initiations and iconography; Acri (2016) describes one esoteric network across maritime Asia, not national traditions. Java produced tantric bronzes and mandala plans; in Champa, Indravarman II founded Indrapura (Dong Duong) in 875 around the Laksmindralokesvara, a royal Avalokitesvara cult.

**The Chinese filter: Dai Viet.** Buddhism reached the Red River delta through Chinese commanderies, not India: Luy Lau was an administrative and Buddhist center, and Vinītaruci, an Indian monk trained in China, arrived in 580 to teach what became Vietnamese Thien (Taylor 1983). Dai Viet took the Chinese canon and Chan lineages.

## Case studies

**Borobudur.** Borobudur (c. 780–840) has nine platforms, 2,672 relief panels and 504 Buddhas. Its plan stages a climb from Kamadhatu to Arupadhatu; the galleries narrate Gandavyuha and Jataka material. The Vajradhatu mandala reading (Wayman, in Gómez & Woodward 1981) is influential but interpretive; the terraced core descends from Javanese *punden berundak* platforms.

**Dong Duong.** Indrapura/Dong Duong (875) was Indravarman II's capital. Its state temple, the Laksmindralokesvara, enshrined Avalokitesvara as protector of the Bhrgu dynasty, and its workshop named a sculptural style. Sanskrit inscriptions and tantric deities sit alongside Shaiva worship at My Son: Buddhism was added to the Cham repertoire and lost favour after the tenth century.

**Pagan.** Pagan (1056–1287) shows the Theravada endgame. Anawrahta's conversion by the Mon monk Shin Arahan (1056) and the transfer of the Tipitaka from Thaton tied the state to Pali learning and Mon clergy; Kyanzittha's Ananda temple (c. 1090–1105) monumentalized it. The Sinhalese reordination of 1181 split the sangha but aligned Pagan with the Mahavihara. Pagan imported little directly from India (Cœdès 1968).

## Debates & confidence

**Labels.** "Theravada", "Mahayana" and "Vajrayana" are retrospective and partly polemical. Skilling (1997) shows that Pali-based Buddhism in early Southeast Asia was not a self-conscious Theravada sect, and that Mahayana, tantric and Pali practices coexisted. Confidence: high that the labels mislead, low on sectarian identity.

**Buddhization vs Indianization.** "Buddhization" names the later mass conversion of the mainland, but obscures that Buddhist change was itself Indianization — and that Southeast Asians drove it. Mabbett (1977) doubted Indianization as a causal model; Wolters (1999) reframed it as localization. Confidence: medium.

**Dates.** The 1056 conversion is chronicle-derived and Borobudur's window stylistic; the Vajradhatu reading is low-confidence. Epigraphic dates (684, 778, 782, 860, 875) are secure.
