# Vector: Sanskrit & Pallava-Grantha Script

The Pallava script—Late Southern Brāhmī—carried Sanskrit and then vernacular languages across Southeast Asia from the fourth century CE, branching into Cham, Kawi, Khmer, Mon, Thai, and Burmese. It traveled with priests, monks, scribes, and merchants, not armies, and was rebuilt at each destination to fit local phonology.

## At a glance

| Field | Value |

| Mechanism | Script transfer; Sanskrit abugida reused for panegyric, law, and vernacular literature |
| Carriers | Brahmins, Buddhist monks, Tamil merchant guilds, local scribes |
| Donor regions | Kanchi and the Tamil coast; northern India for the Pre-Nāgarī stream |
| Period | c. 4th–14th century CE; descendants still in use |
| Daughter scripts | Cham, Kawi, Khmer, Old Mon/Mon–Burmese, Thai, Lao, Burmese, Balinese, Javanese, Batak, Lontara, Baybayin |

## Why it matters

Writing is the clearest marker of Indianization because it is durable, datable, and expensive. A Sanskrit loanword can pass through a trade pidgin; a Sanskrit praśasti needs a patron, poet, scribe, and audience that accepts its authority. The earliest inscriptions—Võ Cạnh in Nha Trang, the Kutai yūpas in Borneo—show local rulers choosing an Indian monumental grammar, not Indian settlement (Coedès 1968; Griffiths 2014).

This underpins Sheldon Pollock's "Sanskrit cosmopolis": between roughly 300 and 1300 CE Sanskrit worked as a transregional language of power without an empire. By the fifth century "power in India now had a Sanskrit voice," and Southeast Asian courts joined by commissioning Sanskrit verse in southern Indian script (Pollock 1996; Pollock 2006). The script carried an epigraphic habit—dated grants, genealogies, curses—that outlasted spoken Sanskrit and shaped how states recorded themselves. It also gives the vault a hard test: trade alone does not produce a Sanskrit stele.

## How it traveled

The script developed from Tamil-Brāhmī in the Pallava heartland around Kanchi and is attested from the fourth century. It wrote both Old Tamil and Sanskrit, hence its full Sanskrit syllabary. Arlo Griffiths calls the dynastic label misleading and prefers "Late Southern Brāhmī"; the script moved east in multiple waves from southern India (Griffiths 2014; de Casparis 1975).

Carriers varied. Bhadravarman I of Champa (r. 380–413) invited Brahmins and left Sanskrit charters at Mỹ Sơn; C.72 records boundaries, a one-tenth tax, and a karma curse. Buddhist networks reached Srivijaya, where Yijing found over a thousand monks studying Sanskrit in 671. Tamil guilds reached Kedah and the Straits. Yet southern Indian languages did not travel in quantity, suggesting Southeast Asians were the main users of Sanskrit and Pali texts (Griffiths 2014). A separate stream appears at Kalasan (778), where Sanskrit is written in Pre-Nāgarī, a northern script, not Pallava.

Reception was adaptation. Cham emerged around 350 and wrote Old Cham, an Austronesian language, within a generation. Khmer is attested by 611 and later split into âksâr mul for Sanskrit and Pali and âksâr chriĕng for Khmer. Kawi added a schwa vowel absent from Sanskrit; Thai, derived from Old Khmer, added tone marks; Mon–Burmese was rebuilt around Pali orthography and lacks ś and ṣ; Cham dropped the virāma. Each daughter script is a local solution to a local phonology.

Sanskrit vocabulary followed but did not displace local languages. Old Malay carries siddhayātra, kośa, lakṣa, and cāra inside Austronesian grammar; Khmer, Cham, Javanese, and Thai absorbed large Sanskrit and Pali lexica in royal and religious registers. The pattern is diglossia: Sanskrit for panegyric and scripture, vernacular for administration and eventually literature. On the mainland, Pali displaced Sanskrit after the Theravāda turn, so Thai and Burmese now carry a Pali canon.

## Case studies

**Võ Cạnh C.40.** A 2.5-metre stele found in 1885, naming King Śrī Māra and conventionally dated to the 2nd–3rd century—the oldest Sanskrit inscription in Southeast Asia. Coedès linked its King Śrī Māra to Funan's Fan Shih-man; Zakharov, citing Vasantatilaka metre and bhrtya, argues for the 4th–5th (Coedès 1968; Zakharov 2010).

**Kedukan Bukit (683).** A Palembang stone of 1 May 683 records Dapunta Hiang's siddhayātra with 20,000 troops and 1,312 foot soldiers. The language is Old Malay, the script Pallava: Indian medium, Srivijayan state formation, Austronesian message (de Casparis 1975).

**Ramkhamhaeng (1283/1292).** The Sukhothai stele claims Ramkhamhaeng invented a Thai script in 1283; the script derives from Old Khmer, ultimately Pallava. Vickery called it "a Piltdown skull of Southeast Asian history" in 1987; electron microscopy supported its antiquity, but the debate remains open (Vickery 1991; Terwiel 2007).

## Debates & confidence

Three disputes matter. The Võ Cạnh date rests on palaeography, which gives a broad 2nd–5th-century range; a Gupta date would move the earliest Sanskrit anchor later (Zakharov 2010). Script does not equal language, settlement, or agency: the record is formal and religious, and carriers may have been Southeast Asians (Griffiths 2014). Finally, Daud Ali finds "major analytical problems" in linking language practice and ideology to state structures, calling the relation "highly complex and shifting" (Ali 2011); indigenist scholarship stresses selective local adoption (Wolters 1999). Confidence is high for script descent, medium for undated inscriptions, low for scribal ethnicity.
