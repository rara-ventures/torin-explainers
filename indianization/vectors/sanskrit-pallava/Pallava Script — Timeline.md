# Timeline — Vector: Sanskrit & Pallava Script

| Year | Event | Indianization Link | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2nd–3rd c. (contested: 4th–5th) | Võ Cạnh C.40 at Nha Trang: a 2.5 m three-faced stele in Sanskrit verse, Vasantatilaka metre, southern Brāhmī script, naming King Śrī Māra. | Oldest Sanskrit in Southeast Asia; script and metre imported, message entirely local. | Coedès 1968; Zakharov 2010 | medium |
| c. 350 | Đồng Yên Châu C.174: Old Cham, an Austronesian language, written in a Pallava-derived script, with a nāga curse formula. | Vernacularization within a generation — the abugida rebuilt for Cham phonology. | Coedès 1968; Griffiths 2014 | high |
| 4th c. (last half) | Kutai yūpas at Muara Kaman, East Borneo: seven stone posts in Sanskrit and Pallava script, "a gift to the Brahmin priests"; Mūlavarman performs the aśvamedha. | Pallava script at the far end of the sea route, carrying a Vedic ritual claim. | Vogel 1918; de Casparis 1975 | high |
| 380–413 | Bhadravarman I's C.72 at Mỹ Sơn: Sanskrit in Pallava script, recording boundaries, a one-tenth tax and a karma curse on later kings. | The full epigraphic habit — grant, boundary, curse — arrives in a single document. | Finot 1904; Coedès 1968 | high |
| 5th c. (some read 358) | Tarumanagara, West Java: the Tugu and Ciaruteun inscriptions of Pūrṇavarman, Sanskrit in Pallava script, with footprints and royal genealogy. | West Java joins the Sanskrit cosmopolis; the date rests on palaeography, not a recorded era year. | de Casparis 1975; Coedès 1968 | medium |
| 611 | Old Khmer first attested in the Angkor Borei region; the Khmer script derives from Pallava and later splits into *âksâr mul* for Sanskrit/Pali and *âksâr chriĕng* for Khmer. | A Mon–Khmer vernacular takes the Pallava abugida while Sanskrit keeps the register of panegyric. | Old Khmer corpus; de Casparis 1975 | high |
| 683 (1 May) | Kedukan Bukit, Palembang: Old Malay in Pallava script, recording Dapunta Hiang's *siddhayātra* with 20,000 troops and 1,312 foot soldiers. | Indian medium, Austronesian message: the script travels without the language. | de Casparis 1975; Coedès 1930 | high |
| 732 | Canggal inscription, Gunung Wukir: Sanskrit in the Pallava alphabet, an edict of Sañjaya of Mataram. | Pallava script is still the default in Central Java a century before the northern switch. | de Casparis 1975 | high |
| 778 | Kalasan inscription: Sanskrit in Prāṇāgarī (northern Indian script), the first Indonesian inscription naming the Śailendra dynasty; a Tārā temple is endowed. | A second stream — Gauda/Bengal rather than Kanchi — enters the same epigraphic market. | Kalasan inscription 778; de Casparis 1975 | high |
| 782 | Kelurak inscription: Sanskrit in Prāṇāgarī, recording a Manjuśrī sanctuary built for King Indra (Saṅgrāmadhanañjaya). | The northern script consolidates; the Sanskrit cosmopolis is now multi-sourced. | Kelurak inscription 782; de Casparis 1975 | high |
| 875 | Indravarman II founds Indrapura (Đồng Dương); its steles carry Sanskrit and Old Cham, and the Mahāyāna Lakṣmīndralokeśvara monastery promotes an Avalokiteśvara royal cult. | Diglossia made visible: two languages, two scripts, one monument. | Coedès 1968; Maspero 2002 | high |
| 914 (4 Feb) | Belanjong pillar, Sanur: Sanskrit in Kawi script on one face, Old Balinese in Prāṇāgarī on the other, dated to Śaka 835 by King Śrī Keśarī Warmadewa. | Bali writes both languages; script choice marks register rather than ethnicity. | Stutterheim 1934; Damais 1947 | high |
| 1035 (possibly 984) | Burmese script is attested in use, derived from Old Mon, itself a Pallava descendant. | A third-generation daughter script on the mainland, carrying Pali rather than Sanskrit. | Aung-Thwin 1985; de Casparis 1975 | medium |
| 1053 (8 Feb) | K.235, Sdok Kok Thom: 340 lines in Sanskrit and Old Khmer on one stele, recording nine generations of a priestly family. | The mature bilingual charter: Sanskrit for the cosmic claim, Khmer for the land. | K.235; Bhattacharya 2009 | high |
| 1292 (contested: 1283) | Ram Khamhaeng stele, Sukhothai: the earliest Thai script, derived from Old Khmer and ultimately from Pallava; Vickery called it "a Piltdown skull of Southeast Asian history." | The last daughter script in the chain, and the most contested. | Vickery 1991; Terwiel 2007 | medium |
