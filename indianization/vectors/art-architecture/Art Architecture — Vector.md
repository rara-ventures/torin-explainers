# Vector: Art & Architecture — Temple as Cosmos

Indic architecture in Southeast Asia is not a copy of Indian forms but a series of local solutions to a shared brief: Meru at the centre, the ocean in the moat, the king at the summit. From the 4th to the 15th century, stupa, temple-mountain, mandala and iconography travelled with monks, Brahmins and courts, and were rebuilt in local materials.

## Why it matters

Architecture is the largest surviving archive of Indianization. Epigraphy is sparse and often celebratory; standing monuments preserve the encounter at scale, across a thousand years. They show transmission as translation: Indian cosmology and iconography were adopted, but plans, materials and labour regimes were re-engineered for local landscapes and politics.

The comparison is diagnostic. A Cham brick *kalan*, a Javanese *candi* and a Khmer temple-mountain all invoke Meru, yet share no construction method. That divergence argues against the old "Hindu colony" model and for localization (Mabbett 1977; Wolters 1999). Foundations like Kalasan (778), Prambanan (856) and Bakong (881) anchor stylistic sequences that inscriptions alone cannot. Buildings are primary evidence for networks that left no archive.

Temples did political work. The temple-mountain placed the ruler at the cosmic centre; the mandala plan made territory a hierarchy of centres. UNESCO listing has made these monuments national symbols, so dating and attribution matter (UNESCO 1991).

## How it traveled

**Stupa.** The relic mound moved from Andhra (Amaravati, Nagarjunakonda) through Pyu Sri Ksetra to Bagan, where the *anda* became the bell-shaped Burmese pagoda. Dvaravati Mon cities took the form up the Chao Phraya; Sri Lanka supplied a rival Theravada model after 1000. The stupa travelled as ritual technology.

**Temple-mountain.** Bakong (881) is the earliest Khmer five-tier pyramid, its moats the cosmic oceans, its summit a *linga* of Shiva (UNESCO 1992). Phnom Bakheng, Pre Rup and Baphuon repeat the type; Angkor Wat turns it into a quincunx. Borobudur reaches the same idea differently: ten terraces from *kamadhatu* through *rupadhatu* to *arupadhatu* (UNESCO 1991).

**Mandala plan.** Concentric enclosures and axial gates make architecture a power diagram. At Borobudur the pilgrim passes 1,460 panels before emerging onto three circular platforms with 72 stupas; at Angkor Thom four cardinal gateways reproduce the mandala as urban form. Wolters's "mandala" is political, but the temple plan is its built analogue (Wolters 1999).

**Iconography.** Deities arrived with attributes intact and bodies altered. Phnom Da's sixth-century Vishnu triad is post-Gupta in iconography, local in physiognomy; Cham *mukhalinga* and Po Nagar's Bhagavati absorb local goddess cults; Javanese Tārā and Mañjuśrī serve as royal palladia (Guy 2014). The icon is Indian; the face usually is not.

**Materials.** Andesite at Borobudur, fired brick in Champa and early Chenla, laterite and sandstone at Angkor, stucco over brick in Dvaravati. Khmer masons corbelled their galleries rather than using a true arch; Javanese builders fitted dressed andesite without mortar. These are separate solutions, not copies.

**Artisan mobility.** No inscription records an Indian mason's guild. The movers are religious specialists: the Kelurak charter (782) records a *guru* from Gauda, and monastic networks linked Nalanda, Java and Tibet (Coedès 1968; Copplestone 2024). Sculptors were probably local, working from imported texts. Style therefore travelled faster than technique.

## Case studies

**Borobudur.** Built c. 780–840, Borobudur is a stepped mandala: five square terraces, three circular platforms, 72 openwork stupas, 504 Buddha statues and 2,672 relief panels (UNESCO 1991). Its divisions map Buddhist cosmology, but the mound continues *punden berundak*. No foundation charter survives; the date rests on style and on the 824 *Jinalaya* (Miksic 1990).

**Angkor Wat.** Suryavarman II's temple covers 162.6 ha, faces west and was dedicated to Vishnu before converting to Theravada (UNESCO 1992). Five towers form a quincunx around Meru, the moat is the cosmic ocean, and the galleries carry narrative relief. Its corbelled vaults and *nāga* balustrades are local (Jessup 2004).

**Prambanan and Mỹ Sơn.** Prambanan's 47 m Shiva temple (856) is the Shaiva counter-monument: 240 structures, a Trimurti of *prasada* towers, and Ramayana reliefs in Javanese dress (UNESCO 1991). Mỹ Sơn, occupied 4th–13th century, uses fired brick with sandstone pillars and bas-reliefs, its *kalan* towers reproducing Meru in Champa (UNESCO 1999).

## Debates & confidence

The quarrel is Indian prototype versus local innovation. The "Hindu colony" model gave way to Mabbett's eclectic reading of uneven transmission (Mabbett 1977), and scholarship now treats local architects as primary, with monastic networks carrying ideas, not masons (Copplestone 2024). "Indianization" survives as shorthand, not a mechanism.

Dating is the second fault line. Borobudur has no foundation inscription; Angkor Wat has no foundation stela; the Bakong–Borobudur sequence is inferred from style; the Võ Cạnh stele (C.40) is placed anywhere from the 2nd to the 5th century. Confidence is high for inscribed foundations, medium for stylistic sequences, low for individual artisans.
