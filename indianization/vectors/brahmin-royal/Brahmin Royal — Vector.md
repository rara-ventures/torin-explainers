# Vector: Brahmins, Devaraja & Cakravartin

Brahmin specialists supplied the ritual technology of Southeast Asian kingship: consecration, genealogy, Sanskrit titulature, daily service of a royal god. What travelled was not a caste system but a repertoire — priestly household, liturgy, pedigree — hired, housed and made hereditary by local courts.

## Why it matters

This is the test case for agency. Coedès (1968) imagined expansion — merchants, priests and warriors carrying civilisation outward. The Brahmins in the record do something narrower: named individuals invited by kings, paid in land and labour, absorbed into local kinship. They sold legitimacy, not sovereignty — a genealogy linking a river-mouth lord to a solar dynasty, a Sanskrit name in *-varman*, a consecration fusing king with god, a liturgy centring the cosmos on the palace.

Portable and cheap, it could be bought by any polity with a surplus: a Cham sanctuary valley, a Javanese rice state, a Theravada kingdom on the Chao Phraya. It outlived its host religions, crowning Buddhist kings at Pagan and Ayutthaya. Champa, Angkor, Java and Bali show one repertoire localizing four ways — the god absorbing the king at Mỹ Sơn, the king absorbing the god at Angkor Wat, the priesthood becoming a descent caste in Bali. That variation is the strongest evidence for vernacularization rather than transmission.

## How it traveled

**The specialist, not the caste.** The unit of transmission was a household. K.235 (1052) describes the Brahmin Hiraṇyadāma, "foremost of the twice-born," arriving from Janapada and teaching four Tantric texts to Jayavarman II's chaplain Śivakaivalya. The stele bars anyone outside Śivakaivalya's matriline from serving the royal god: "let none but these be its officiants." Ritual expertise became property; the stele is a land charter as much as a chronicle.

**Consecration.** The rite installed a *liṅga* and identified the king with Śiva. The Old Khmer object is *kamrateṅ jagat ta rāja*, "the lord of the world who is king"; *devarāja* is a later Sanskrit gloss. The same passage says the ritual made the sovereign *cakravartin* — universal monarch whose wheel rolls to the horizon. That was the key political export: a claim to overlordship requiring no territorial administration, what a mandala needed.

**Titulature and genealogy.** The *-varman* suffix, the posthumous god-name (*Parameśvara*, *Paramaviṣṇuloka*), the Sanskrit *praśasti* — claims in a transregional idiom (Pollock 1996; Gonda 1952). Genealogies were softer and more useful: Cham kings adopted Kaundinya–Soma, Khmer houses traced the sun, Bali's Brahmana later claimed Javanese descent.

**Varṇa.** Rarely a functioning order. In Java and Bali it became *wangsa* titles; in Khmer country the *brāhmaṇa* were a court corporation holding land, slaves and tax exemptions, not a caste. What was imported was the two-part ideology — king and priest, *kṣatriya* and *brāhmaṇa* — with the *purohita* as the king's ritual double. *Rājadharma* texts were known; customary law governed.

**Cross-confessional.** Buddhist kings kept the apparatus. The Śailendras built Tārā and Mañjuśrī sanctuaries through Gauda masters while Sanjaya's line installed Śaiva *liṅgas*; Jayavarman VII's Mahayana state used Brahmin consecration; Ayutthaya's Theravada court kept Brahmin astrologers and coronation rites (Baker and Phongpaichit 2017). Legitimation was a service, not a confession.

## Case studies

**Champa: Bhadreśvara at Mỹ Sơn.** Bhadravarman I (r. c. 380–413) endowed the valley to a Śiva named Bhadreśvara — the god built from the king's name plus *īśvara*. It remained the dynastic palladium for nine centuries. The 774 theft of the *mukhaliṅga*, restored by Satyavarman in 784, coincided with Bhagavatī's rise beside the *liṅga*. Local claims were wrapped in Sanskrit: Đông Yên Châu is a nāga curse in Old Cham, and C.96 turns Kaundinya–Soma into a title deed.

**Angkor: the 802 consecration.** The model rests on a retrospective text. K.235, cut 250 years later by the family that benefited, describes Jayavarman II's rite on Mahendraparvata, the imported Brahmin, the four texts, the hereditary monopoly. Under Suryavarman II the same logic produced Angkor Wat, where the king became Viṣṇu after death. After the Theravada turn the *baku* survived, performing rites Buddhism did not.

**Java: Sailendra and Sanjaya.** Two houses, one ritual market. Canggal (732) records Sanjaya's Śaiva *liṅga*; Kalasan (778) a Tārā temple built at a *guru*'s urging; Kelurak (782) a Mañjuśrī sanctuary whose master came from Gauda. Masters of either confession were contractors, hired by whichever house held the plain.

## Debates & confidence

**What *devarāja* meant.** Aymonier's "god-king" implied the monarch's divinity. Mabbett (1969) argued *devarāja* means "the god who is king" — the *liṅga*, not the man. Kulke (1978) read it as a state cult of the royal *liṅga*. The Khmer term is *kamrateṅ jagat ta rāja*, and the sole narrative, K.235, was written by interested heirs. Confidence: medium on the cult, low on its theology.

**Brahmin versus Buddhist legitimation.** Sanderson (2003) documents Śaiva Siddhānta lineages carrying Indian texts into Khmer country, but Buddhist kings funded Brahmin ritual freely. Jayavarman VII's Mahayana programme thinned Brahmin visibility, and the Theravada turn ended royal Śaivism but kept its ceremonials.

**The category itself.** Mabbett (1977) doubted "Indianization" as an explanation; localization (Wolters 1999) and Pollock's cosmopolis reframe it as elite adoption of a transregional idiom. No Indian state, no mission: hired expertise.
