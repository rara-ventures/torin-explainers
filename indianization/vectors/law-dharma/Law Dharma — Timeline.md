# Timeline — Vector: Dharmaśāstra, Manu & Local Law

| Year | Event | Indianization Link | Source | Confidence |
|------|-------|--------------------|--------|------------|
| c. 200 BCE–200 CE | **Mānava Dharmaśāstra** composed and redacted; Olivelle argues for the 2nd–3rd c. CE on numismatic grounds | The portable *vyavahāra* core — debt, contract, marriage, inheritance — that later travelled | Olivelle 2005 | medium |
| 4th–5th c. | Cham endowment charters, beginning with Bhadravarman's Mỹ Sơn stele, record land and *liṅga* grants with curses on violators | Sanskrit donative formula: the king grants, the god holds, the trespasser is cursed | C.72; Lingat 1949 | medium |
| 658 | Mỹ Sơn Sanskrit inscription (**C.96**) turns the Kaundinya–Somā foundation myth into a dynastic deed | Brahmin–nāgī alliance as a charter of legitimacy, repeated in Khmer law | C.96; Coedès 1968 | high |
| 7th c. | Chenla **praśasti** and boundary grants use Sanskrit legal formulae: witnesses, boundaries, imprecations | Indian documentary habit without an Indian code | Coedès 1968 | medium |
| 9th–10th c. | Khmer inscriptions record land suits, sales, inheritance and boundary trespass; the king is final judge | *Rājadharma* in practice: royal adjudication, Khmer reasoning | Ricklefs 1967 | high |
| 10th c. | **Tuol Rolom Tim** stele read by Cœdès as a legal text or judgment | Khmer dispute procedure committed to stone | Cœdès 1954 | medium |
| 974 | Inscription on Jayavarman V's education implies a Dharmasūtra–Arthaśāstra curriculum for princes | *Nīti* and *artha* as royal training, not a courtroom code | Ricklefs 1967 | medium |
| 12th c. | **Dhammavilāsa Dhammathat** compiled at Pagan; earliest Burmese legal treatise | Pali recension of Manu's eighteen heads, with Pagan custom beneath | Huxley 2005 | high |
| 13th c. | "Dhammathat" first appears in a Bagan inscription; the Mon/Pali legal genre is attested | Vernacularization: Sanskrit *dharmaśāstra* becomes Pali *dhammathat* | Huxley 1997 | high |
| c. 1290/91 | **Wareru Dhammathat** compiled in Mon at Martaban: eighteen chapters, under 5% borrowed from Manu | Manu as authority, Mon customary law as substance | Huxley 2005 | high |
| 1351 | Ramathibodi I founds Ayutthaya and is credited with the **Thammasat** | Dharmaśāstra recast in Pali to legitimate a new dynasty | Baker & Phongpaichit 2016 | medium |
| 14th–15th c. | **Kuṭāra Mānawa** and the Old Javanese **Agama** in Majapahit Java | Manu-derived procedure applied to Javanese custom; Java → Bali later | Hoadley & Hooker 1981 | medium |
| 15th–19th c. | **Kuṭāra Mānawa** recopied and applied in Bali alongside village *awig-awig* | Javanese Manu tradition survives in Balinese courts and villages | Hoadley & Hooker 1981 | medium |
| 1805 | Rama I compiles the **Three Seals Code**; the Thammasat heads it and remains working law | Indian-derived authority survives at the centre of Siamese law | Baker & Phongpaichit 2016 | high |
| 1832 | Minh Mạng annexes Panduranga and abolishes Cham customary law | End of the Cham legal tradition; Vietnamese code replaces local custom | Coedès 1968 | medium |
