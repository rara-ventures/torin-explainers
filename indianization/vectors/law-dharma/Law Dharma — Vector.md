# Vector: Dharmaśāstra, Manu & Local Law

Indian law reached Southeast Asia as a repertoire of authority, not a code. Dharmaśāstra supplied the vocabulary — *dharma*, *daṇḍa*, *vyavahāra* — and its lawgivers; courts supplied the rules. Mon *dhammathat*, Siamese *Thammasat* and Balinese *Kutara Manawa* all cite Manu and decide by custom.

## Why it matters

The dharmaśāstra is prescriptive literature, not legislation. The smṛtis describe an ideal order of *varṇa*, *āśrama* and penance; only the *vyavahāra* sections — debt, contract, marriage, inheritance, assault, theft — were portable to societies without caste (Olivelle 2005). What travelled was that core and its claim: law is inherited from Manu, and the king exists to protect it.

*Niti* and *artha* supplied the other half. *Rājadharma* made judging a royal duty; the *Arthaśāstra* described courts, fines and princely education. Khmer inscriptions show the king judging land disputes; Thai and Javanese texts place him under a written law he did not invent.

Law is diagnostic: Indianization here requires dharmaśāstra, and law is where import and application diverge most. A polity that copied charters but kept customary rules is not a failed India but the normal case; China and Gandhara, which took Buddhism but not dharmaśāstra, mark the negative end of the test.

## How it traveled

**The corpus.** Manu's *Mānava Dharmaśāstra* was fixed around the second or third century CE (Olivelle 2005) and travelled with the smṛti tradition and *niti* compendia. The *Arthaśāstra* left no Southeast Asian manuscript; Ricklefs (1967) inferred its norms behind a 974 inscription on a prince's education. *Niti* is better attested, in the Old Javanese *Tantri Kāmāndaka* and Majapahit *Nitisastra*.

**Carriers.** Transmission was a by-product: Brahmins served as consecrators, astrologers and jurists; monasteries trained scribes; merchants needed contracts. The clearest carrier is a monk — Shin Buddhaghosa translated the Mon *Wareru Dhammathat* into Burmese and Pali in the sixteenth century.

**Vernacularization.** The Sanskrit code became a Pali genre. *Dhammasattha* is attested in a Burmese inscription by the thirteenth century, and from it came Mon, Burmese, Tai and Lao recensions. Java produced the Old Javanese *Agama* (Hoadley and Hooker 1981) and the *Kutara Manawa*. Manu became a *rishi* who saw the law on the world's wall and recited it to Mahā Sammata, the first king of Buddhist myth.

**What was borrowed.** Huxley's count is the sharpest measure: in early Burmese dhammathats, material taken directly from the *Manusmriti* is four or five per cent (Huxley 2005). The rest is Pagan-era custom. The reason is structural: ninety per cent of Manu concerns caste, pollution, ritual and penance, which were meaningless without *varṇa*. What was borrowed is the *vyavahāra*: eighteen heads of litigation, evidence, compensation.

**Inscriptions and courts.** Law appears in epigraphy as procedure (Hooker 1978): boundaries recorded, witnesses named, grants protected by curses. Tenth-century Khmer inscriptions show land suits, temple estates that "may be neither taken nor given by the kings or their favorites," and a boundary violator sentenced to have his chest pierced (Ricklefs 1967). Cœdès (1954) read Tuol Rolom Tim as a legal text. None cites Manu by chapter; each enacts a legal habit.

**Vinaya.** Buddhist monastic law supplied a second legal culture: 227 rules, confession and expulsion, monastic adjudication. Sanghas governed their own property; dhammathat authors wrote under their authority. The question is how Buddhist Theravada Buddhist law was: Indian form, local content, Buddhist sanction.

## Case studies

**Champa.** Cham inscriptions record endowments and imprecations (Lingat 1949); the Kaundinya–Soma myth became a deed. No Cham dharmaśāstra survives. The "Sakkarai" cited as Cham law is the *Sakkarai dak rai patao*, a 5,227-page chronicle of Panduranga's kings. Minh Mang abolished Cham customary law in 1832. Confidence: high on idiom, low on any code.

**Angkor.** The tenth century is best documented. Ricklefs (1967) found a working land law: sales, inheritance, boundary suits, charters, trespass penalties. The king is final judge; the reasoning is Khmer. A 974 inscription on Jayavarman V's education suggests the Dharmasutra–Arthashastra curriculum; no code is cited. Confidence: high on procedure, medium on texts.

**Ayutthaya.** Ramathibodi I (r. 1351–1369) is credited with the *Thammasat*, a dharmaśāstra recast in Pali terms. It heads the Three Seals Code of 1805 and supplied its authority, while the Palace Law governed kingship (Baker and Phongpaichit 2016). Caste was not adopted; merit, royal edict and Thai custom decided cases. Its function was constitutional, not forensic.

## Debates & confidence

Did Indian law apply, or was it invoked? Textualists read the codes as working law; Huxley's arithmetic cuts the other way, and the sceptical position is that Buddhist law was custom, not the dhammathat. Ayutthaya supports a middle view: the *Thammasat* legitimated the code, while the Palace Law and royal edicts governed. That is legitimation without litigation, and the common Southeast Asian pattern. Confidence is high that dharmaśāstra vocabulary, Manu's name and the eighteen heads circulated; medium that courts reasoned from them; low that any Sanskrit code was enforced verbatim outside monastic and commercial contexts, where texts were read closely.
