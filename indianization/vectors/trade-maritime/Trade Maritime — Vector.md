# Vector: Monsoon Trade & Port-Polities

The monsoon trade system linked the Tamil coast, the Bay of Bengal and the South China Sea through taxing ports from the first century CE to 1511. It supplied the wealth that made Indianization possible without causing it: Sanskrit steles mark local elites converting commercial capital into ritual sovereignty.

## Why it matters

Trade is the precondition of Indianization in maritime Southeast Asia, not its cause. The monsoon carried the people who carried the culture—merchants, monks, Brahmins, scribes—and the wealth that paid them. No port, no monastery; no surplus, no panegyric.

But the same routes reached societies that did not Indianize. Roman Egypt bought pepper via Muziris and Arikamedu but kept Roman law; Sasanian and Arab merchants moved the same goods through the Gulf without adopting Sanskrit kingship; China imported camphor and cloves while keeping its bureaucracy.

Adoption was a political choice. Where a ruler needed a transregional language of legitimacy, Indian script, genealogy and cult were available. Where transient foreigners ran exchange, Indian forms stayed superficial: a Sanskrit toponym in Timor, Tamil inscriptions in a trading quarter. The vector is strongest where trade met state formation—Oc Eo, Kedah, Palembang—weakest where trade passed through (Wolters 1999).

Trade created the corridor; Brahmins, monks and courts built the institutions inside it.

## How it traveled

Two monsoons governed the route: southwest winds took ships from the Red Sea to Malabar and across the Bay of Bengal; northeast winds brought them back. The *Periplus*, a Greek handbook of c. 40–70 CE, describes the Red Sea–India leg, names Muziris and Barygaza, and credits Hippalus with the open-ocean crossing; east of the Bay its knowledge fades; Kattigara belongs to Ptolemy (Casson 1989).

The first major terminus was the Mekong delta. Oc Eo collected Indian beads, seals and gold foil, Roman medallions of Antoninus Pius, and Chinese mirrors; Malleret's 1942 excavations made it Funan's type-site. Whether it is Kattigara is argued; that goods and icons arrived together is not (Manguin 2004).

West of the delta, the Malay Peninsula offered a shortcut. Kedah's Bujang Valley—*Kataha* in Sanskrit, *Kalah* to Arabs, *Kadaram* to Tamils—sits at the strait's northwest mouth. Iron furnaces, a jetty and a brick monument mark an early port; fifty-odd candi and a *ye dharma* stone mark Indian ritual from the fifth century (Jacq-Hergoualc'h 2002; Murphy 2018).

By the seventh century the Straits had a hegemon. Srivijaya, centred at Palembang, controlled the approaches. Yijing spent six months there in 671 and found a thousand monks studying Sanskrit; the Old Malay inscriptions of 683–686 record oath, curse and campaign in Pallava-derived script. Srivijaya taxed a network, not an empire (Wolters 1967; Kulke 1993).

Fragility showed in 1025, when Rajendra I's Chola fleet sailed to Sumatra, sacked Palembang and attacked Kadaram. The raid was plunder, not conquest, but it broke Srivijayan toll power and shifted initiative to the Chola coast (Coedès 1968).

Melaka inherited the choke point after 1400. Its rulers converted to Islam around 1414 but kept the commercial machinery: four shahbandars, the *Undang-Undang Laut Melaka*, and a port collecting Gujarati textiles, Sumatran pepper, Chinese ceramics and Moluccan spices. Albuquerque took the city in 1511 and captured a business, not a culture (Heng 2009).

## Case studies

**Oc Eo.** Malleret's 1942 excavations uncovered a 450-hectare site with Indian seals, beads, gold foil, a wooden Buddha and Roman medallions of Antoninus Pius. It was an entrepôt, not a colony: imports and icons appear together, but there is no known Indian administration, cemetery or archive. Its identification with Kattigara is probable, not proven (Manguin 2004).

**Kedah and Sungai Batu.** The Bujang Valley preserves the material base Sumatran inscriptions lack: iron furnaces, a jetty, a brick monument dated c. 110 CE, and over fifty low brick candi. Charcoal from furnace SB2H gave 788–537 BCE, but Bayesian modelling favours the 2nd–10th centuries CE. Kedah remained the peninsula's main Bay of Bengal port and Chola target in 1025 (Jacq-Hergoualc'h 2002; Murphy 2018).

**Melaka.** Founded c. 1400, Melaka turned the choke point into a managed entrepôt. Four shahbandars handled Gujarati, Tamil, Chinese and Javanese traders; the *Undang-Undang Laut Melaka* codified port law; warehouses held Perak tin, Sumatran pepper, Indian textiles and Chinese ceramics. Islam arrived c. 1414, yet the court kept Sanskrit titles and much of its port administration (Heng 2009).

## Debates & confidence

**Trade versus Indianization.** The older Coedès model made Indian cultural expansion the engine of Indianized states; Wolters reframed adoption as localization, with local elites reshaping Indian forms. Most accept trade as necessary, not sufficient: it explains exposure, not the choice to build a Sanskrit state. Confidence high on precondition, low on cause.

**Srivijaya's control.** Palembang is conventional, but Jambi and Chaiya have partisans, and Kulke asked whether *kadatuan* Srivijaya was an empire or a kraton. Naval-monopoly claims rest on Chinese and Arabic notices, not administration. Confidence low.

**Port hierarchies.** Entrepôt models separate primary from feeder ports, but evidence is ceramics and foreign quarters, not treaties. The 1025 raid redistributed traffic rather than ending it. Confidence medium.
