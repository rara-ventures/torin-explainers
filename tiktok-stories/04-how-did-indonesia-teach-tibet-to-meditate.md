Yijing left Guangzhou in 671 on a Persian ship. He was headed to Nalanda to learn Sanskrit. His teacher told him: 'First, stop in Srivijaya for two years.'

At Palembang — Fo-shi — he found 1,000 monks studying exactly as in Central India. One of the four great scholars of his day, Śākyakīrti, lived there. Yijing stayed 6 months learning grammar and Old Malay.

He spent 11 years at Nalanda, collected 400 texts — half a million verses — then sailed *back* to Srivijaya for four *more* years to translate, because Srivijaya had the library and the peace.

He sent his book — *Record of Buddhist Practices* — home in 692 with the note: 'If a Chinese priest wants to go to India, first stay in Fo-shi.'

A generation later, another network. The Pāla king Devapala's Nalanda plate (860) says a Sailendra prince from Java — Balaputra, son of Samaragrawira, lord of Sailendra on Java — was exiled to Sumatra after losing a Javanese civil war. To legitimize his new Srivijaya line, he asked Devapala for five villages at Nalanda to fund a monastery.

The donor in Sumatra built for the Sangha in Bihar. That monastery stood.

And a century later, Atisha — the Bengali monk who reformed Tibetan Buddhism — spent 12 years *in Srivijaya* under Dharmakirti before he ever reached Lhasa.

Tibet learned emptiness via Sumatra's port.
