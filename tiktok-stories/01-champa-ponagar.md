Po Nagar in Nha Trang is not Vietnamese. It's Cham.

The Cham built an entire kingdom along Vietnam's coast — Champa — and they prayed in Sanskrit. In 781, a Javanese fleet of "black, gaunt, horrible men" sacked this hill and shattered its jewelled Mukhalinga. King Satyavarman chased them to sea, then crowned a new linga named after himself.

Thirty years later, his successor stopped praying to Shiva. He prayed to her — Bhagavati, the Lady of Kauthara.

Inside the 23-meter tower sits a 1.2-meter black granite goddess, 10 arms, holding dagger, bow, discus, conch. She is Durga as Mahishasuramardini, but to the Cham she is Yan Po Nagar — Mother of the Kingdom.

In 950, the Khmers stole her golden statue. In 965, King Jaya Indravarman carved the stone one you can still touch today.

Families of slaves — Cham, Khmer, Malay, Siamese, Chinese — were gifted to her temple as living property. Her lands were *devadravya*, untouchable, even when kings fell.

Champa died in 1471 when Dai Viet cannons took Vijaya and beheaded its king. But Po Nagar never closed. Today, Cham Ahiér Hindu pilgrims and Kinh Vietnamese Buddhists burn incense at the same tower.

The linga is gone. The goddess stayed — because in Champa, land was female.
