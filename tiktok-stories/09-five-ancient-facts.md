## Script

Here are five things everyone repeats about Asian history. All five are younger than your grandparents.

**One. The Srivijaya Empire.** A maritime empire that supposedly ruled the straits for six centuries. Nobody knew it existed until a Frenchman named George Cœdès wrote a paper in 1918. There's no Srivijayan chronicle. There's no excavated capital — the argument is still between Palembang, Jambi and Chaiya, and nobody has found a convincing urban core at any of them. What exists is a handful of Old Malay stones, some Chinese notices, and one copper plate from Nalanda. Srivijaya is partly an argument.

**Two. The Ramkhamhaeng inscription.** Called the first Thai text, dated 1292, the document behind the idea that Thailand had a happy little kingdom where the king hung a bell for petitioners. In 1987 the historian Michael Vickery published a paper attacking its script, vocabulary and content; a Thai artist, Piriya Krairiksh, argued it was forged in the reign of King Mongkut. A 1990 scientific analysis pushed back. Most specialists now say partly authentic. Meaning: a national founding document is still under litigation.

**Three. The Code of Kalantiaw.** A pre-Spanish Philippine law code with brutal, specific punishments, taught in schools for decades. In 1968 the historian William Henry Scott showed it had been written by a student, José Marco, in 1913. It is a forgery. It still circulates.

**Four. "Barangay."** The word for the pre-colonial Philippine political unit — used in textbooks, and still the name of the smallest government unit today. Almost every early citation traces back to a single Spanish report from 1589, by Juan de Plascencia, written to explain Tagalog society to the crown. Some scholars now argue that the "barangay" as a labelled system of government is partly a Spanish reconstruction. The coloniser named the thing it destroyed.

**Five. The Laws of Manu.** The book people imagine India exported across Asia. Over fifty manuscripts survive and they don't agree with each other. The version treated as authoritative since the 1700s isn't the oldest — it's the one British philologists chose after William Jones translated it in 1776, because they needed a single "Hindu law" to administer. The ancient law code is an editor's decision.

Now the important part. This is not a "everything is fake" video. All five things still tell you something real — about trade, about kingship, about law. What changed is what they're evidence *for*.

Srivijaya is evidence that the straits were connected. The Ramkhamhaeng stone is evidence that someone in the 19th century needed Thailand's origin to look a certain way. Kalantiaw is evidence that Filipinos wanted a past that wasn't Spanish. The barangay is evidence that the Spanish needed a grid to put people in. Manu is evidence that the British needed a book to rule by.

So the question isn't "is it true?" The question is: **who wrote it down, when, and what did they want?**

That's how you read history. That's how I'd read the next one you see.
