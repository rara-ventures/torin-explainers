## Script

Thai massage has a founder, and the founder is a doctor from India.

His name is **Jīvaka**. In the Buddhist texts he is the personal physician of the **Buddha** and of the Indian king **Bimbisāra**. He lived in what is now Rajgir, in the **5th century BCE**. Thai people regard him as the creator of their massage, and the massage is sold to tourists as more than 2,500 years old. ⚠verify

Now look at where the rules actually came from.

**1832.** King **Rama III** of Bangkok starts enlarging a temple called **Wat Pho**. He doesn't just build a temple. He turns it into a public school. Scholars, poets, monks and doctors are set to work cutting medicine into stone around the cloisters.

Sixty inscribed tablets. Thirty for the front of the body, thirty for the back, marking the pressure points. Then, in **1836**, eighty statues of hermits in stretching poses are finished, each with a text about the illness it treats. ⚠verify

So the rulebook for the massage is carved in Bangkok, in the 1830s.

Is that a fraud? No. The Indian doctor is real as a character in the texts, and Indian medical ideas do sit under Thai medicine. But the tour guide never tells you what is on the walls.

The Jīvaka texts are not neat. Historians have argued about them for decades. **C. Pierce Salguero** says medical legends cannot be treated as proof of what doctors actually did. And some of the healing methods in the Jīvaka texts look more Chinese than Indian to scholars. The Indian doctor was being rewritten in every country that borrowed him.

Thailand has older traces too. In **1687**, a French envoy, **Simon de la Loubère**, watched a Siamese masseur climb onto a sick man and press down with his feet. ⚠verify Ayutthaya had a Department of Massage Physicians, ranked like the government offices. ⚠verify And in **1758**, the royal chronicle records King **Borommakot** struck by a sudden attack of "wind" as his masseurs helped him up. They laid him back down and massaged him. ⚠verify

Now the body map. Thai massage works along **sen**, lines that run through the body. The UNESCO listing describes four body elements: earth, water, wind and fire. The standard Ayurveda list has five. Thai humours keep wind and bile, but the third one is **semha**, phlegm, where Ayurveda has kapha. ⚠verify

So what did India actually send? A doctor's name. A story about a healer who never stopped learning. And a reason for people to trust it.

What Thailand built on that was a body map, a school, and a stone rulebook. Made in its own capital, by its own king, with a team of scholars to make it stick.

India gave the massage a founder. Thailand wrote the rules. The stone version is now on a UNESCO list, and the founder's name still sells the tickets.

## Accuracy flags

1. **Jīvaka's date.** Wikipedia says "5th century BCE" and cites a debate over historicity. The 2,500-year figure is the Thai framing, not a dated fact. ⚠verify
2. **"2,500 years old" as sold claim.** The Thai massage article says "over 2,500 years ago". Check the phrasing before any on-screen "2,500-year" caption. ⚠verify
3. **Sukhothai massage inscription.** The Thai massage article says the oldest evidence is a stone inscription at Wat Pa Mamuang under Ramkhamhaeng and Maha Thammaracha I. Only Wikipedia is fetched for this. ⚠verify
4. **Three Seals date conflict.** The Thai massage article says the Three Seals Law recorded a Department of Massage Physicians "during the reign of King Borommatrailokkanat (1448–1488)". The vault dates the Three Seals Code to 1805. Either the law was compiled later from older material, or the attribution is loose. ⚠verify
5. **Stone material.** The Thai massage article says the inscriptions are on "marble panels". The Wat Pho article says the pictorial encyclopedia is on "granite slabs". The script says "stone" to avoid choosing. ⚠verify
6. **Number of authors.** The Thai massage article says "35 poets". The Wat Pho article says the inscriptions were written by "about 50 people". The script says "scholars" and avoids the number. ⚠verify
7. **Statue count and date.** Eighty hermit statues, completed 13 November 1836, come from the Thai massage article only. ⚠verify
8. **Sixty tablets, thirty front and back.** From the Wat Pho article. ⚠verify
9. **"Thailand's first university".** This is a popular description the Wat Pho article repeats with "considered". Not a formal designation. ⚠verify
10. **Salguero's view.** The line that medical legends can't be evidence of practice is quoted on Wikipedia from Salguero 2009. Check the primary before quoting. The script paraphrases. ⚠verify
11. **Simon de la Loubère 1687–88.** The massage description comes from the Thai massage article's citation of his account. Check the primary text (*Du Royaume de Siam*) before quoting. ⚠verify
12. **1758 royal chronicle episode.** Taken from the Thai massage article. The chronicle itself is not checked. ⚠verify
13. **Herbal compress ingredients.** The Thai herbal compress list includes camphor and borneol. The Barus camphor in [[08-camphor-and-the-wrong-door]] may be a different source tree. ⚠verify
14. **"Ten sen lines".** The Thai massage article gives the Thai name *เส้นสิบ* (sen sip) without translating it. "Ten" is a gloss. ⚠verify
15. **Semha for kapha.** Thai acupressure lists "Tridosha ... Vata, Pitta, and Semha". The naming and Pali origin of *semha* are not checked. ⚠verify
16. **Ayurveda claim is contested.** Thai massage is described as "likely the product of a 19th-century synthesis". This sentence is unreferenced on Wikipedia. ⚠verify
17. **Chinese travel stories.** The Jīvaka article says "stories exist about Jīvaka's purported travels to Thailand" without a source. Not used in the script. ⚠verify
18. **Wat Pho training numbers.** The "200,000 therapists in 145 countries" figure appears in the Wat Pho article. Unsourced on the page. Not used in the script. ⚠verify
