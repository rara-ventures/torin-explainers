## 1. A Hindu king paid for a Buddhist monastery built by an Indonesian king

**1006 CE.** The Srivijayan mahārājā Maravijayottungavarman builds the **Chūḍāmaṇi-vihāra** at Nagapattinam, on the Chola coast. The Chola king **Rājarāja I** funds it and guarantees its upkeep in a copper-plate charter. Three kings, two religions, one building, 6,000 km apart from Palembang.

Then the hook on top of the hook: the charter was **issued by a Hindu king for a Buddhist monastery built by a Sumatran king, and the Song emperor's name appears on the Chinese plate of the same set.**

**Why it travels:** it kills the "India exported Hinduism" version of the story with one object. Indianization here is a *financial instrument*.
**Anchor:** Leiden copper plates of Rajaraja I; Chudamani Vihara. **Confidence: high.**

## 2. Those plates are going home — and so should the gold goddess

The Leiden plates were in the University of Leiden collection **until 2026, when they were restituted to India** (Leiden University news, September 2026). The Agusan "Golden Tara" left the Philippines in 1922 for ₱4,000 and is still in Chicago.

**Why it travels:** repatriation is a live argument, not history. Pair them in one video: *"Two Southeast Asian objects left India's orbit in different ways. One is coming back."*
**Anchor:** Chudamani Vihara page; Agusan image / Field Museum A109928. **Confidence: high on the restitution, medium on the campaign's prospects.**

## 3. The year the fleet burned Sumatra is the year the monk who taught Tibet left it

**1025.** Rajendra Chola's invasion of Srivijaya. The *same year*, the Bengali scholar **Atiśa (982–1054)** begins his return voyage from Sumatra to India — and then to Tibet in 1042, where his Kadampa lineage reshapes Tibetan Buddhism.

The vault has these as two separate facts. The article joins them: the **intellectual hub of Vajrayana Asia was being bombed while its most important student was sailing out of it.**

**Why it travels:** coincidence reads as destiny, and the reveal is real. Existing story 04 (Tibet via Indonesia) gets a much better ending.
**Anchor:** Chola invasion of Srivijaya, citing Chattopadhyaya. **Confidence: medium — the year is a reconstruction, not an inscription.**

## 4. The raid's biggest winner was not India. It was Java.

After 1025 the captured mahārājā's daughter escapes the sack of Palembang, reaches the court of **Airlangga** in East Java, becomes his queen as Dharmaprasādottuṅgadevī — and in **1035 Airlangga builds a Buddhist monastery called Śrīvijayāśrama, named after her homeland.** The raid broke Srivijaya's toll monopoly; Java filled it, then built a peace settlement out of a refugee princess.

**Why it travels:** it inverts the expected outcome. "Empire attacks port; port loses; a third country wins" is a clean three-beat script.
**Anchor:** Chola invasion of Srivijaya, citing Munoz 2006:163. **Confidence: medium.**

## 5. A Tamil trading company dug a lake in Thailand and cut the contract into stone

**Takuapa, peninsular Thailand, c. 9th century.** A Tamil inscription records that **Avani-nāraṇam** — an artificial lake — was dug by a man named Nāṅgūr-Udaiyan, and placed under the protection of the **Māṇiggirāmam** merchant guild living in the military camp. Because *Avani-narayana* is a title of the Pallava king Nandivarman III (r. 826–849), the date hangs off a royal surname.

**Why it travels:** "a corporation's contract, carved in stone, in another country, 1,100 years ago" is a business-history hook the channel already proves works (insurance, trade depth, war-risk).
**Anchor:** Tamil inscriptions in the Malay world; Sastri 1949; Christie 1998. **Confidence: medium on date, high on the inscription.**

## 6. The oldest trade-rules sign in Aceh is about interest rates

**Neusu, Banda Aceh, c. 12th century.** A Tamil stone, front illegible except the word *maṇḍapam*, back reading like commercial law: decisions binding on "our people" (*makkal*), a waiver on collecting interest in kind, a reference to the royal emblem (*ilacchinal*, Sanskrit *lañcana*), and a curse-shaped enforcement clause. Provisional translation, in the Aceh Museum.

**Why it travels:** it is boring in the most compelling way. Indianization as compliance documentation.
**Anchor:** Neusu inscription; Christie 1998. **Confidence: low-medium — the reading is explicitly provisional.**

## 7. The "Monkey and the Turtle" is an Indian fable

The frame story of the *Pañcatantra* — the Indian *nīti* collection that also became the Persian *Kalīla wa Dimna* — arrives in Philippine folklore as *The Monkey and the Turtle*, taught to Filipino children, usually presented as purely local.

**Why it travels:** "the story you were told as a kid is 2,000 years old and from India" is the single most shareable format in this field.
**Anchor:** Hinduism in the Philippines; cf. *Pañcatantra* → *Kalīla wa Dimna* already in [[Persia — Region]]. **Confidence: medium on the specific tale's genealogy, high on the *nīti* circulation.**

## 8. *Bathala*, *tala*, *laho*: the Sanskrit hiding inside Tagalog

Juan R. Francisco counted **336 Sanskrit loanwords in Tagalog**, about **150** of them sources of major terms. Laura Junker's reading is the interesting one: the loans cluster in **governance and mythology** — the vocabulary of the *Maginoo* noble class — which means the words arrived as **status equipment**, not as conversation.

| Tagalog | From |
|---|---|
| *budhi* (conscience) | *bodhi* |
| *dukhâ* (poverty/suffering) | *duḥkha* |
| *lahò* (eclipse) | *Rāhu* |
| *talà* (star) | *tārā* |
| *mukhâ* (face) | *mukha* |
| *gurô* (teacher) | *guru* |
| *Bathalà* (supreme god) | *bhaṭṭara* |

**Why it travels:** participatory. Every Filipino viewer audits the list in the comments.
**Anchor:** Indian influences in early Philippine polities (Jocano 2001:138–9; Francisco; Junker 1990). **Confidence: high on the count existing, medium on individual etymologies — see §Accuracy.**

## 9. One stone lists 13 ports. Two of them appear in a Pali scripture and a Greek map.

The **Tanjore inscription of 1030** names the ports Rajendra Chola claimed to have raided: Sriwijaya (Palembang), Pannai, Malaiyur, Mayirudingan, Ilangasokam (Langkasuka), Mapappalam, Mevilimbangam (Kamalanka), Valaippanduru, Talaittakkolam, Madamalingam (Tambralinga), Ilamuridesam (Lamuri), Manakkavaram, Kadaram.

Two of those names go back much further than 1025. **Talaittakkolam** is Ptolemy's *Takkola* on the Kra Isthmus — and it also appears in the Pali *Milindapañha*. **Mapappalam** is placed by the Sri Lankan *Mahāvaṃsa* on the coast of Pegu. So a single eleventh-century victory list preserves toponyms from a **second-century Greek geography** and a **Buddhist scripture**.

**Why it travels:** "one stone, three civilisations, 800 years" is the deep-time version of the map video the channel already wins with.
**Anchor:** Tanjore inscription; Kulke et al. 2009; Arokiaswamy 2000. **Confidence: high on the list, medium on each identification.**

## 10. The raid that ended an empire left no occupation. Only a receipt.

The Chola fleet did not sail through the Malacca Strait where Srivijaya was waiting. It resupplied at **Barus** on Sumatra's west coast, ran south, and came at Palembang **through the Sunda Strait — from behind.** Srivijaya's navy sat at Kedah, watching the wrong door. Fast monsoon-timed raids meant the *maṇḍala* could not organise a defence or ask for help. And then the Cholas left: no garrisons, no administration. The last epigraphic mention of "Srivijaya" anywhere is that Chola Tanjore inscription, 1030–31. No Srivijayan envoy reaches China between 1028 and 1077.

**Why it travels:** it answers the question the whole series asks — *why didn't India hold Southeast Asia?* — with a specific military answer rather than a soft "cultural influence" one.
**Anchor:** Chola invasion of Srivijaya, citing Munoz 2006 and Miksic & Goh 2017. **Confidence: high on the route and absence of occupation, medium on the "collapse in 1025" thesis.**

### 11. The empire that was invented in 1918

Srivijaya is not recorded in any Srivijayan chronicle. There is no excavated urban core. **George Cœdès formally postulated its existence in 1918**, from Chinese notices, a handful of Old Malay stones and the Nalanda plate. Everything since is argument about whether his reconstruction is one state or a label for a network.

**Hook:** *"This 'empire' was invented by one Frenchman in 1918. Most of what you read about it is still his guess."*
**Anchor:** Srivijaya; Cœdès 1918. **Confidence: high on the historiography.**

### 12. Srivijaya may never have been a sea power at all

The newer reading reverses the textbook label "thalassocracy": fleets were **logistical support projecting land power** from river-mouth centres, and the naval strategy began as coercing ships to call at toll ports before degrading into raiding (Heng 2013). The polity may have disintegrated after 1025, with *Sanfoqi* in Chinese sources meaning Jambi, not Srivijaya.

**Hook:** *"The sea empire that probably was not a sea empire."*
**Anchor:** Heng 2013, *JSEAS* 44/3. **Confidence: medium, contested.**

### 13. The Cholas had no navy

"The Chola navy" was assembled from **merchant shipping** — there was no standing naval force and no purpose-built warship. The 1025 campaign worked because it was fast, monsoon-timed and came from the Sunda Strait, not because India commanded the sea.

**Hook:** *"The most famous Indian naval invasion in history was conducted by a merchant fleet."*
**Anchor:** Chola Navy; Karve/Sastri. **Confidence: high.**

### 14. A guild with four names, a bull on its flag, and a rulebook

The **Five Hundred of Ayyavole**: *Ayyavole* in Kannada, *Ayyavolu* in Telugu, *Āryarupe* in Sanskrit, *Ainurruvar* in Tamil. They guarded the *Vira-Baṇajā-Dharma* — the law of noble merchants — and flew a **bull** on their flag. The **Manigramam** received **72 royal privileges** from a Chera king in the Viraraghava copper plate (c. 1225), and its leadership passed by **corporate succession inside a merchant oligarchy**, not from father to son.

**Hook:** *"Medieval India had corporations with charters, flags and elected CEOs — and they wrote the contract in stone."*
**Anchor:** Manigramam; Five Hundred Lords of Ayyavole; Ainnurruvar; Subrahmanyam 2001. **Confidence: high on the inscriptions, medium on the "CEO" framing.**

### 15. Camphor: why a Tamil stone sits on a Sumatran beach

Barus on Sumatra's west coast sold camphor and benzoin from the inland forests to China, India and the Middle East; the **Lobu Tua inscription (1088)** records the Ayyavole 500 meeting there. Across the island, the port of **Panai/Pannai** supplied the eastern camphor route, is named in the Tanjore raid list, is called *Pane* in Armenian and Persian travel literature — and survives today as **16 Vajrayana temples at Padang Lawas**, especially Bahal.

**Hook:** *"A smell. That is the reason there is a Tamil receipt in Sumatra."*
**Anchor:** Pannai; Barus; Lobu Tua. **Confidence: medium-high.**

### 16. A monastery named after a king's father

The Chūḍāmaṇi-vihāra takes its name from **Cūḍāmaṇi**, the father of the builder Maravijayottunggavarman (r. c. 1008–1025, "Se-li-ma-la-pi" in the Chinese chronicles). So the object of "endowment diplomacy" was a son's memorial to his father, built on a Hindu king's money, certified under a Chinese emperor's title.

**Anchor:** Maravijayottunggavarman; Chudamani Vihara. **Confidence: medium.**

### 17. There was never one Book of Manu

Over **fifty manuscripts** of the *Manusmṛti* survive and they contradict each other. The recension treated as authoritative since the eighteenth century is not the oldest — it is the one **William Jones translated in 1776** and British administration then used to invent "Hindu law". So when Ayutthaya or Pagan invokes Manu, there is no single fixed original to have copied.

**Hook:** *"India exported a law book. Except there was never one law book."*
**Anchor:** Manusmṛti; Olivelle 2005; Kane 1968. **Confidence: high — and it strengthens the localisation thesis.**

### 18. Three hundred Ramayanas — and in two of them the wrong brother wins

By some counts there are **~300 versions** of the Rāmāyaṇa across Asia. In the Malay *Hikayat Seri Rama* and the Lao *Phra Lak Phra Lam*, **Lakṣmaṇ outranks Rāma**: Rāma is written as righteous but weak, pushed into the background while the younger brother acts. The Cambodian *Reamker* makes the heroes mortal rather than divine and promotes Hanumān and the mermaid **Suvannamaccha**.

**Hook:** *"Same epic, six countries. In two of them the little brother steals the ending."*
**Anchor:** Versions of the Ramayana; Hikayat Seri Rama; Reamker. **Confidence: high on the literary record.**

### 19. Java wrote its Ramayana 850 years before Thailand did

The **Kakawin Rāmāyaṇa** was composed in Central Java between **856 and 930** — the longest of all Old Javanese *kakawin*, and still treated by Javanese as the peak of the form. The Thai *Ramakien* as a court text is eighteenth-century. The epic travelled south through Java, not north from Ayutthaya.

**Anchor:** Robson 1995; Zoetmulder 1983. **Confidence: high.**

### 20. Cambodia's national epic is 16th-century — its first trace is a 7th-century stone

*Reamker* means "Glory of Rāma". The surviving poem is 16th-century, the Angkor Wat relief scenes 12th-century, and the earliest allusion is read in the **Veal Kantel inscription (K.359)** of the 7th century. A national epic with a thousand-year gap between its mentions.

**Anchor:** K.359; Bergundal 1971. **Confidence: medium on K.359, high on the rest.**

### 21. Shadow theatre crosses a border nobody drew

Southern Thai **nang talung** is the parent of Malaysian **wayang gedek**, traditionally performed in **Kedah** — while Kelantan's *wayang Siam* is also Thai-derived. The puppets, the music and the Rama repertoire moved across a border that only exists since 1909.

**Hook:** *"Malaysia's oldest theatre came from Thailand. The border is younger than the plays."*
**Anchor:** Nang talung; Kodaman 1968. **Confidence: medium — folk-history, not epigraphy.**

### 22. "Barangay" comes from one Spanish report in 1589

The word that names the entire pre-colonial Philippine political order rests mainly on a single document, Juan de Plascencia's *Las costumbres de los indios Tagalos de Filipinas* (1589). Some scholars now argue the "barangay" as a polities-label is a **Spanish reconstruction** of a society they were trying to describe. Most units were 30–100 households; a handful of river ports were something else entirely.

**Hook:** *"The word for the Philippines' first governments was invented by the people who destroyed them."*
**Anchor:** Precolonial barangay; Woods; Scott 1994. **Confidence: high on the thinness of the source base.**

### 23. Butuan had a stamp, and a graveyard of boats

The **Butuan Ivory Seal** (9th–12th c.) cuts the name *Butban* in stylised Kawi — a polity naming itself, in an Indian-derived script, on ivory. The **balangay** boat-burials on the old Agusan river make the same point physically: this was a boat culture with trade recorded to China, Champa, Đại Việt, Brunei, Srivijaya, Majapahit, Kambuja and Persia.

**Anchor:** Butuan Ivory Seal; Butuan (historical polity). **Confidence: medium-high.**

### 24. Ma-i is a reading problem, not a place

Chinese sources name **Ma-i from 971**. For most of the 20th century it was Mindoro — Fay-Cooper Cole proved the old name for Mindoro was *Mait* in 1912. In 2005 Go Bon Juan argued it is **Bay, Laguna**, because 麻逸 reads *mâ-i̍t* in literary Hokkien but *bâ-* in the vernacular. A whole "kingdom" may hinge on how a character was pronounced by whichever dialect the scribe heard.

**Hook:** *"We cannot find this kingdom because we cannot agree how to pronounce its name."*
**Anchor:** Ma-i; Song shi; Go Bon Juan 2005. **Confidence: high that it is unresolved.**

### 25. The Philippine Ramayana might be younger than Shakespeare

Juan Francisco, who recorded the Maranao *Maharadia Lawana* in 1968, dated the story's arrival to the **17th–19th centuries** — after Spanish contact, via Javanese and Malay channels. The *Darangen* (17 cycles, ~72,000 lines, UNESCO 2005/8) preserves pre-Islamic Maranao law but cannot be dated to the Indianization period either.

**Hook:** *"The 'Indian epic of the Philippines' may have arrived after the Spanish."*
**Anchor:** Maharadia Lawana; Francisco 1989. **Confidence: high that the dating is disputed; this is the honest answer.**

### 26. Meru reached China

The cosmic mountain is not a Southeast Asian quirk. Angkor Wat is Meru; the **Burmese pyatthat** roof's finial bud is Meru; and the Chinese pagoda sits on a **Sumeru throne** (xūmízuò). Meru even appears in Taoist scripture after Buddhism arrived in China. One imaginary mountain, four architectural traditions, 6,000 km.

**Anchor:** Mount Meru. **Confidence: high.**

### 27. A word for "poor" that used to mean "warrior"

*Timawa* named the Visayan feudal warrior class, above commoners and below the royal *tumao*. Under Spanish rule it was demoted to "freeman", then to commoner, and in modern Visayan it is simply an adjective for **impoverished**. Meanwhile *maharlika*, a Tagalog warrior class, was inflated into "royalty" in modern usage. Class vocabulary is the most durable record of conquest.

**Hook:** *"This word for 'poor' used to mean 'noble warrior'. The Spanish did that."*
**Anchor:** Timawa; Maharlika. **Confidence: high.**

### 28. The priests were women, and India did not change that

Pre-colonial spiritual specialists — *babaylan*, *balian*, *katalonan* — were **predominantly women**, with effeminate men (*asog*, *bayok*) also serving as mediums in *pag-anito* and *pagdiwata* rites. Where Indian ritual vocabulary entered Philippine religion it attached to *diwata* and *anito*, not to a Brahmin or Buddhist priesthood. The strongest available argument that Indianization stopped at the level of prestige language.

**Anchor:** Filipino shamans; Anito; Scott 1994. **Confidence: high.**

### 29. God-king: the diffusion order, and its grades

Wikipedia's account has *deva-rāja* travelling **Java → Malay kingdoms → Khmer → Thai monarchies**, carried by Brahmins deployed to courts; the related *cakravartin* claim came in three grades — universal monarch, ruler of one continent, and a merely local king — which is what made it purchasable by any polity with a surplus.

**Caveat:** the vault's own reading (Kulke 1978; Jacques 1985) is that *devarāja* is a Sanskrit gloss on the Khmer *kamrateṅ jagat ta rāja*, so the direction of the idea is contested. Film the **grades**, not the diffusion order.
**Anchor:** Devaraja; Chakravarti. **Confidence: medium on the order, high on the three grades.**
