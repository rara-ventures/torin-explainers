## Script

The Rāmāyaṇa is the most travelled story in Asian history. Depending on how you count, there are around three hundred versions. Six of them count as national literature. Read them in order and you notice something nobody mentions: the countries that borrowed it kept rewriting who gets to be the hero.

**Java, around 900.** The *Kakawin Rāmāyaṇa* is composed in Central Java in Old Javanese, on Sanskrit metres. Robson dates it between 856 and 930. It is the longest of all the Old Javanese *kakawin*, and Javanese writers treated it as the peak of their own art form. Pin that date: ninth century.

**Cambodia.** The earliest allusion there is read in a seventh-century inscription from Vyādhapura, K.359. Then the bas-reliefs at Angkor Wat in the twelfth century. But the surviving poem — the *Reamker*, "Glory of Rāma" — is sixteenth-century. And in the Khmer telling, Preah Ream is nominally an incarnation of Vishnu, yet he and everyone around him behave like mortals. The added episodes put Hanumān and a mermaid, Suvannamaccha, at the centre. It is the Rāmāyaṇa re-doctrined for a Buddhist court.

**Malay world.** The *Hikayat Seri Rama* keeps the plot and changes the politics. Rāma is righteous but passive; storytellers push him into the background and hand the decisive action to the younger brother, Laksmana, who is admired precisely because he reacts. Invented characters get promoted to leading roles.

**Laos.** The version is called *Phra Lak Phra Lam*. Read the title. Lak before Lam. The younger brother's name comes first.

**Thailand.** The *Ramakien*, compiled as a court text in the late eighteenth century under Rama I, who took his regnal name from this hero, and still performed as masked *khon* dance. Thai monarchs are numbered Rama I to Rama X. Here the epic became the theory of kingship.

**Philippines.** The Maranao *Maharadia Lawana* puts Rāvaṇa at the centre, a monkey-king granted immortality — the antagonist is the protagonist. But Juan Francisco, who recorded it in 1968, dated its arrival to the seventeenth through nineteenth centuries, through Javanese and Malay channels. This "Indian" epic may be younger than Spanish Manila.

Now look at the timeline, because it runs backwards from the usual map. Java has it in the ninth century. Cambodia's poem is sixteenth. Thailand's court version is eighteenth. The story did not flow out of India into a waiting region. It moved *around* Southeast Asia, court to court, and each court edited it to fit its own politics. Java wasn't a late recipient; it was one of the earliest.

That is the real definition of Indianization in this region. Not copying — rewriting. Same epic, three hundred times, no two endings agree, and in two countries the little brother wins.
