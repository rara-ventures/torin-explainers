In 802, a Khmer prince named Jayavarman II climbed Phnom Kulen, let a Brahmin named Hiranyadama perform the *aindra mahābhiṣeka*, and declared himself *kamrateṅ jagat ta rāja* — god-king. The report says he 'came from Java' — probably not Java, but the Malay world.

That ritual moved. Wherever the sacred linga went, the capital went — Mahendraparvata → Hariharalaya → and finally, around 900, Yasovarman I's Yasodharapura: Angkor. He dug the East Baray — 7.5 by 1.8 kilometers of water — as a mandala moat and built Phnom Bakheng as a five-tier Meru.

In 1113, Suryavarman II decided to outdo him. He built Angkor Wat — 162.6 hectares, outer wall 3.6 kilometers, moat 200 meters wide, central quincunx to 65 meters. The entire complex *is* the universe: outer wall = Cakravāla mountains, moat = cosmic ocean, central towers = Meru's five peaks. It's Vishnu's house, so it faces *west*, toward death.

His architect Divākarapaṇḍita encoded 108, 27 — lunar numbers — into the measurements. On equinox, the sun rises behind the central tower as seen from the western causeway.

A century later, after Cham boats sacked Angkor on the Tonle Sap, Jayavarman VII built Bayon — same plan, but Mahayana Buddhist, 216 faces, no two alike, possibly Hevajra not Avalokiteshvara.

In 1431, Ayutthaya sacked it. The court left for Phnom Penh. The hydraulic city — with its barays silted — was abandoned. But monks never stopped chanting there. Wat became *wat* — monastery. The only Angkor temple never lost.

Meru became Pali.
