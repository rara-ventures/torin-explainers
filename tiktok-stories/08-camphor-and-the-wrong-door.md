## Script

Everyone says India never conquered Southeast Asia. That's true. There's one exception, and it explains more than the rule.

1025. Rajendra Chola, the greatest king in southern India, sends a fleet across the Bay of Bengal and burns Srivijaya. Fourteen ports are named on a stone at Tanjore five years later: Palembang, Pannai, Malayur, Langkasuka, Takkola, Tambralinga, Lamuri, the Nicobars, Kadaram — Kedah. The king of Srivijaya is captured. A jewelled war-gate, the Vidyadhara-torana, is carried home to Tamil Nadu as proof.

Now the part that makes it a story instead of a list.

Srivijaya controlled two choke points: the Malacca Strait and the Sunda Strait. Its navy sat at Kedah, at the northwest mouth of Malacca, because that's where the ships came from. That's where everyone came from. Indian ships crossed the Bay and turned down the west side of the peninsula and waited at the door.

So Rajendra didn't use the door.

His fleet went to **Barus**, on Sumatra's *west* coast, out in the Indian Ocean, and resupplied. Then it ran south along that coast, went around the bottom of Sumatra, and came into the Sunda Strait — and hit Palembang **from behind**. The navy guarding the wrong mouth of the strait never saw it coming. Srivijaya's mandala had no time to organise a defence or ask anyone for help.

Why Barus? Camphor and benzoin. Resin from the trees in Sumatra's northern forests, worth a fortune in China, India and the Middle East for incense, medicine and perfume. Barus was the port that sold it, and the port was already full of Tamil merchants. There's a stone there from 1088 — the Ayyavole 500, one of the great merchant guilds, meeting at "Varocu." The guilds mapped the coast decades before the fleet did. The invasion ran on commercial logistics.

And here's the second thing nobody says. The Cholas **had no standing navy**. No purpose-built warships. The expedition was assembled from trading vessels. It was a merchant fleet carrying an army, timed to the monsoon, and it was never designed to hold anything.

Which is why they didn't stay. There are no Chola garrisons in Sumatra. No Chola administration. No Chola tax system. The last time the word "Srivijaya" appears in any inscription anywhere is on that Chola stone at Tanjore, around 1030. After that, Chinese records keep writing the name Sanfoqi, but most historians now think it means Jambi — a different place wearing a dead empire's label.

So what did 1025 actually do? It broke a toll booth. Srivijaya's ability to force ships to stop and pay was finished, the strait opened up, and the winners were not India. The winners were Javanese agrarian kingdoms — Kahuripan, then Kediri — and, three centuries later, Majapahit. A raid meant to grab prestige redrew the map for somebody else.

The takeaway is the one that fits everything else in this series. In Southeast Asia, the Indian presence was never an army. It was a network of merchants, monks and priests, and states bought from it. Even the one time India sent a fleet, it had to borrow the merchants' route to find the back door.
