## Script (3:20 target, ~600 words at 180 wpm)

Put the Thai, Khmer, Burmese and Lao alphabets side by side and they look like four different worlds. Round loops. Hooks. Stacked marks.

They share a grandparent: a South Indian script, Late Southern Brāhmī, used in the Pallava country around Kanchi in the fourth century CE. That is roughly 1,650 years ago.

It travelled with monks, Brahmins and scribes. On Vietnam's central coast, the kingdom of Champa was writing Sanskrit in it by the 4th century, and writing its own Cham language in it within a generation. Khmer followed. Mon carried it to Burma. Thai took its letters from Khmer. The Sukhothai stone that records it is dated 1283 or 1292; scholars still argue over which. Javanese and Balinese came through a branch called Kawi.

So why not Vietnam?

Because Vietnam was never a place Indian writing had to reach. From about 111 BCE, Han China ran the Red River delta. For a thousand years the officials who governed it wrote in Chinese characters. When the Viet broke free in 939, they kept the characters.

Then they bent them. They built *chữ Nôm*: Chinese characters repurposed to write Vietnamese words. Thai did the same with Khmer letters, and Khmer did the same with Pallava letters. Vietnam did not refuse adaptation. It adapted a different donor.

And the line between the two was physical. In 192 CE a Cham leader rebelled south of the Hải Vân Pass and founded Lâm Ấp, the seed of Champa. North of that pass, Chinese-run Luy Lâu was becoming a Buddhist and bureaucratic centre. Same coast. Two alphabets. One pass between them.

For centuries the border held. Then, in 1471, Lê Thánh Tông took Champa's capital Vijaya, and the south stopped being a rival script zone.

Then came Latin letters. In the 1600s, Jesuit missionaries built a Latin-based system for Vietnamese: *quốc ngữ*. For two and a half centuries it was a missionaries' tool. In the early twentieth century, French colonial administration made it the script of government, and Nôm faded in a few decades.

So Vietnam's modern alphabet came from neither India nor China. It came from Portuguese and French hands.

Here is the point. Vietnam's alphabet is not the outlier because Vietnam rejected India. It is the outlier because the first writing to reach its heartland came up the Chinese road, and the last came over the sea from Europe.

The alphabet tells you which road the teachers came by.

## Fact-check notes (corrections to VIRAL-CONNECTIONS §12)

1. **"Every Southeast Asian alphabet is Indian."** Only true for historical scripts. Today Indonesia, the Philippines and Timor-Leste officially write in Latin, and Malaysia and Brunei use Latin and Jawi (Arabic). The script says "Thai, Khmer, Burmese and Lao," not "every."
2. **"The one mainland country."** Malaysia occupies the peninsula and is also not Indic-scripted today. The script limits the claim to Burma, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia and Vietnam.
3. **"Two thousand years."** The script begins in the 4th century CE, so about 1,650 years.
4. **"Khmer gives Thai its script in the 13th century."** The Ram Khamhaeng date is contested (1283 or 1292). The script hedges it.
5. **"Vietnam spent a millennium inside the Chinese literary world."** Broadly right, but it omits chữ Nôm, and it omits that Champa, on the same coast, used Indic script for centuries. The script includes both.
6. **"Quốc ngữ replaced a Chinese-based system."** It was a missionary tool for about 250 years. The replacement happened in the early twentieth century under French colonial rule. The script keeps those stages separate.
7. **"The Cham get their own version directly from the South Indian model."** The vault says Pallava-derived, with Cham emerging c. 350. The script says "Champa wrote Sanskrit in it" and does not claim a direct line.
