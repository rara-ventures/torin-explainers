## Script

Here's the story everyone tells about India in Southeast Asia. Monks, scribes and kings went out. Scripts, gods and law came back in. The ships carried goods and ideas, and nobody crossed the sea as a refugee.

This one doesn't fit.

**Gunavarman** was a prince, born around **367**, in a royal family the Chinese sources place in **Kashmir** ⚠verify. One 1912 history says his grandfather had been king of Kabul instead. At twenty he became a monk. At thirty his king died with no heir, and the court asked him to leave the robes and take the throne. He refused. He walked off into the hills.

Then came the sea. Chinese biographies say he went to Sri Lanka, then on to **Java**, which the sources call Shepo. He preached there ⚠verify, though it's unclear whether he stayed or only stopped. He wasn't the first to take that road. The Chinese pilgrim **Faxian** had been blown to Java in **413** and found Buddhism held in little esteem. Gunavarman was on the same route about a decade later.

Then China. Two monks in the Song capital asked **Emperor Wen** to bring him over. He came by sea to **Guangzhou**. Near the port he saw a hill that reminded him of Vulture Peak, where the Buddha preached, and he renamed it after that ⚠verify. His arrival year is disputed: one account gives **424**, another **431** ⚠verify. Either way he reached **Jiankang**, modern Nanjing, taught the Lotus Sutra, and translated the monastic codes. He died there on **28 September 431**.

Now the nuns, which is where this stops being a footnote. Chinese records say **Sinhalese nuns** sailed into Nanjing on a merchant ship called the *Nandi*. In **434** a large group was ordained there, led by a nun named **Tissara**, to carry on the order Gunavarman had begun ⚠verify. The numbers rest on a single Chinese catalogue, passed on second-hand.

Before he died, Gunavarman wrote a farewell poem. The biography tradition preserves it. The line that matters says: *I fled the chaos over the sea, to Java and to Linyi. The wind of my karma blew me to the land of Song* ⚠verify, my translation. **Linyi** was the Chinese name for the coast of **Champa**. Scholars still argue about that identification.

So ask the question he answers. Where did he run? Not to a court or a temple in India. He ran to Java and to Champa, two of the places this series calls the receivers.

The usual story says Southeast Asia was the classroom and India the teacher. His own words make Java and Champa his shelters, and the ship the road between them.

And the vault says China is a relay: it passed India's ideas on without taking India's kingship. Gunavarman is the relay's first hand. He never planned to export anything. He said no to a throne, and the sea did the rest.

## Accuracy flags

1. **Origin.** Chinese sources give Kashmir (ch. Wiki; Ding Fubao's 罽賓). Mookerji (1912) says grandson of an ex-king of Kabul. Unresolved.
2. **Birth year 367** comes from the Chinese Wikipedia page only. No primary source checked.
3. **Java: stop or stay?** Mookerji says he "visited Java on the way." The Chinese Wikipedia says he preached in Java (闍婆). Do not claim he converted a king. None of the pages I fetched says so.
4. **Arrival year.** 424 (Mookerji; Chinese Wikipedia's Guangzhou petition) vs 431 (Chinese Wikipedia's Jiankang arrival; Ding Fubao). Both can be right if he landed in Guangzhou in 424 and reached Jiankang in 431. Unconfirmed.
5. **Nuns.** The Nandi ship, the 434 ordination and Tissara appear in Mookerji and the Abhayagiri entry. The Abhayagiri page's claim of "over three thousand" ordinations is uncited. The Nandi ship's date is 426 on one page and 433 on another. Do not state a number without a primary Chinese catalogue (e.g., Biqiuni zhuan, 526).
6. **Death poem.** Its authenticity and my translation are unverified. Check against the Gaoseng zhuan text before filming.
7. **Linyi = Champa** is the common identification, but the vault's own Champa note marks it as contested (Vickery 2005).
8. **Hill renaming** near Guangzhou is local tradition in the Chinese biography, not independently dated.
9. **No English Wikipedia article** on Gunavarman exists under three titles I tried (404). English-language sourcing is thin: Mookerji (1912) and the Abhayagiri entry.
10. **Name warning:** do not confuse with the Tháp Mười Guṇavarman (Funan, 5th c.), who is a different person.
