## Script

Everything in this series has been one-directional. India sends Sanskrit, a script, a god-king cult, a law book, an epic. Southeast Asia receives it and reworks it. That's real. It's also incomplete, and the missing half is a seed.

**The object.** Areca nut, wrapped in a betel leaf with slaked lime, chewed across South and Southeast Asia. A mild stimulant — the psychoactive compound is arecoline, and the practice is addictive. Today roughly **600 million users**, concentrated in India, Bangladesh, Myanmar, Sri Lanka and the Philippines, and described in *The Lancet Oncology* in 2017 as a neglected global public-health emergency, because it causes oral and oesophageal cancer with or without tobacco.

**Where it came from.** Not India. The plant ingredients are **native to Island Southeast Asia** — the areca palm's home is the Philippines — and the habit's origin is there, not in South Asia. The oldest traces are not even a quid: two **pre-agricultural foragers on Sulawesi** show tooth wear and **arecoline preserved in their dental calculus**, one dated **25,000–16,000 years ago**, the other 7,600–6,300 years ago, from sucking the whole nut without leaf or lime.

The oldest evidence of the full quid with lime is a burial pit: **Duyong Cave, Palawan, 4,630 ± 250 years BP** — around 2680 BC. Skeletons with the red-stained dentition of lifelong chewers, and *Anadara* clam shells used as lime containers, one still holding its lime.

**The linguistics.** The reconstructed Proto-Austronesian word **\*buaq** meant simply "fruit". In Proto-Malayo-Polynesian it narrowed to mean specifically the areca nut. That shift happens inside a language where the nut matters more than the category — which is how you date a habit to a place before there's any writing. On that evidence the habit began inside Austronesian maritime culture and then **rode the Austronesian expansion outward** — and the dated path is the story: Micronesia and Near Oceania by roughly 3,500–3,000 years ago, the **Lapita** culture from about 3,600 BP, **mainland Southeast Asia by 3,000–2,500 BP**, and — this is the half that gets left out of the Indianization story — **South India and Sri Lanka by about 3,500 years ago, northern India by 1,500 BP, Madagascar by 600 BP.**

Not carried into Polynesia at all. Which is itself a fact worth a sentence: the greatest sailors in the region took the nut everywhere except the biggest ocean they crossed.

And from India it went **west** — into Persia and the Mediterranean. And here's the tell that gives the direction away: the European word **"areca" comes from Dravidian** — Malayalam *aṭaykka*, Kannada *aḍike*, Tamil *aḍaikkāy*. The plant reached European ears through southern India, from a name that wasn't Sanskrit. The Sanskrit learned vocabulary came later, and the habit's home is in the east.

**Now connect it to the rest of the series.** Every Indianized kingdom in Southeast Asia was already chewing. The ritual complexes are everywhere: in Vietnam, "matters of betel and areca" is the idiom for marriage, and the quid opens the negotiation between the groom's and the bride's families. In Assam no ritual is complete without *tamul-pan* offered to gods and guests. In Bhutan a myth says Guru Rinpoche invented the *doma* chew as a substitute for eating raw flesh. In India and Sri Lanka kings kept attendants whose job was to carry the betel box. And classical Hindu and Buddhist iconography put the nut and leaf together as male and female principles.

**So there were two flows, and they are not the same age.** Indian monks, Brahmins and scribes moved east carrying grammar, genealogy and liturgy, from around the first centuries CE. Austronesian mariners moved west carrying a crop and the ritual around it, from around 1500 BCE — and they reached India **two thousand years before the alphabet did.**

One flow left stone inscriptions, so we can name its century. The other left stained teeth, lime shells and a word that narrowed from "fruit" to "the nut", so we date it by chemistry in dental plaque.

**And the kicker, which belongs to the food chapter, not this one.** The tobacco that many chewers add is a *late* arrival from the Americas — colonial era, post-1500. A habit at least four thousand years old got a newer drug bolted onto it, and the combination is the part that kills people. The oldest case in the world didn't stay the same for four thousand years. Somebody put something new in it.
