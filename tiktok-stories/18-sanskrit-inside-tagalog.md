## Script

The Philippines is the edge case of Indianization. No temple-mountain, no god-king, no Sanskrit law code. But its language kept the receipts.

*Lahò* is the Tagalog word for eclipse. It comes from *Rāhu*, the demon in Indian astronomy who swallows the sun and moon. The demon is gone. The word stayed.

Juan R. Francisco counted **336 Sanskrit loanwords in Tagalog**, about 150 of them the source of major terms. Some are everyday: *budhi*, conscience, from *bodhi*. *Dukhâ*, suffering, from *duḥkha*. *Talà*, star, from *tārā*. *Mukhâ*, face, from *mukha*. *Gurô*, teacher, from *guru*.

Laura Junker's reading is the interesting one. The loans cluster in governance and mythology, the vocabulary of the *maginoo*, the noble class. So these words didn't arrive as conversation. They arrived as status equipment. Speak the court's words, and you were a noble.

Take the top god. *Bathalà*, the Tagalog supreme god. The standard etymology traces it to Sanskrit *bhaṭṭara*, "lord." It's widely repeated and not universally accepted. Treat it as the best guess, not a finding.

Now the oldest document. On **21 April 900 CE**, a debt was cleared at Tondo, on the Pasig delta. The Laguna Copperplate, dredged from the Lumbang River in the late 1980s ⚠verify (vault says 1987; other sources give 1989), is dated to the Śaka year 822. It records the acquittal of about **865 grams of gold**, owed by the family of a man named Namwaran. It's written in Old Malay, in Kawi script, with Sanskrit legal terms. It isn't a royal poem. It's a receipt.

That's the pattern. Indian forms came in through trade and law, not through a court commissioning Sanskrit verse.

Then Butuan. A seal cut from ivory, somewhere between the 9th and 12th centuries, names the polity itself: *Butban*, in stylised Kawi. Along the old Agusan river, *balangay* boats were buried. This was a maritime culture, trading with China, Champa, Đại Việt, Brunei, Srivijaya, Majapahit and Kambuja.

And then the problem. In 971, the *Song shi* records a place called **Ma-i**. For most of the 20th century, scholars put it on Mindoro. In 2005, Go Bon Juan argued it was Bay, in Laguna. The reason is pronunciation: the characters 麻逸 read one way in literary Hokkien and another in the vernacular. A kingdom may hinge on how a scribe heard the name.

So what did India leave here? Not a temple. Not a god-king. Not a law code. It left a script, a set of titles, a receipt, a word for eclipse, and a word for the god at the top.
