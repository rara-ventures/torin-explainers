## Script

Southeast Asia's oldest legal tradition is Indian. Thailand's *Thammasat* claims to come from Manu. Burma, Mon, Lao and Tai kingdoms wrote *dhammathats* under the same authority. Java produced the *Kutara Manawa*. That's the standard story.

Here's the problem. There was never one Manusmṛti.

More than fifty manuscripts survive, and they contradict each other. What scholars have treated as *the* text since the eighteenth century is a Calcutta manuscript with a commentator called Kulluka Bhatta attached — not the oldest witness, not a complete one. It became authoritative because William Jones translated it in 1776 and the East India Company needed a single book of "Hindu law" to administer people with. So the canonised version is an administrative choice, made 150 years before anyone argued about what India exported to Asia.

Now the Southeast Asian half, which is better.

What actually travelled was not the book. It was a slice of it. Manu's text is roughly ninety per cent about *varṇa* — who may marry whom, who may touch what, what penance restores purity. That whole apparatus was meaningless in societies without caste. So it didn't go.

What went was the other ten per cent: the *vyavahāra* section. Debt, contract, inheritance, assault, theft, evidence. Eighteen heads of litigation. Plus one enormous idea: that law is inherited rather than invented, and the king exists to protect it.

Measure it. The scholar Andrew Huxley counted how much of the early Burmese *dhammathats* is taken directly from the Manusmṛti: about four or five per cent. The rest is Pagan-era custom.

In Ayutthaya, the *Thammasat* is a Dharmasastra recast in Pali-Buddhist terms. Manu survives as an ancestral authority standing at the top of the table of contents. Caste is gone. Merit, royal edict and Thai custom do the actual work — and the Palace Law and the king's orders governed, while the *Thammasat* legitimated.

In Java and Bali, *varṇa* became *wangsa*: descent titles. Most Balinese are Sudra; claims to Ksatria or Wesya status are usually claims to Javanese ancestry, not a division of labour. In the Khmer countries, the Brahmins were a land-holding court corporation with tax exemptions and slaves, not a caste order.

So the region took the procedure and rejected the hierarchy. That is the single best argument that Southeast Asians were choosing rather than receiving. They wanted a law that made the king look legitimate. They did not want a law that reorganised their villages.

And the last twist: whoever "exported" it wasn't exporting a fixed text, because the fixed text is a British decision from 1776. The Indianisation of law is not a story about India sending a book. It's about what a region picked up, what it threw back, and what a later empire invented for its own convenience.

**B-roll:** palm-leaf *dhammathat* → Thai Three Seals Law → Ayutthaya palace law fragments → Balinese *wangsa* genealogy → Khmer land inscription → 18th-century Calcutta manuscript → Jones's 1776 title page.
