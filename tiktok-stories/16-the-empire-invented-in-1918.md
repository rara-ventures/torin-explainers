# The empire that was invented in 1918

> **HOOK 1 (Cat02 Contrarian):** The "great maritime empire" of Southeast Asia has no chronicle, no excavated capital, and a name a Frenchman gave it in 1918.
> **HOOK 2 (Cat09 Specificity):** 1918. One French scholar. Four kinds of evidence: Chinese notices, a few stones, one Indian land grant, and a guess.
> **HOOK 3 (Cat01 Curiosity):** Ask who ruled the Strait of Malacca in the year 1000, and the textbook answer is a sea empire. The sources give you a lot less than that.

## Script

Every textbook describes the same place. A sea empire called **Srivijaya**, controlling the Strait of Malacca from its base in Sumatra, taxing every ship that passed, rich enough that an Arab traveller in 903 called its king the richest on earth.

Here is the first problem. Srivijaya never wrote its own history. There is no Srivijayan chronicle. No excavation has turned up an urban core, the kind of city you would expect from a capital that dominated a strait. What we have is Chinese notices, a few old Malay stones, a Nalanda land grant, and some Arabic praise.

The name appears on the inscriptions, but the empire as a single state comes from one French scholar. **George Cœdès** formally proposed it in **1918**, by stitching those scattered sources into one state. It was a reasonable reconstruction, and it became the orthodoxy. Everything since has been an argument about whether his reconstruction describes a real state, or a loose network of ports that people later labelled as one.

Now the second problem: the word "empire" itself. The old picture says Srivijaya was a **thalassocracy**, a ruler of the sea. The newer reading, from Heng in 2013, reverses it. The fleets were not the empire. They were a logistics tool, moving goods and people for a land-based power sitting at a river mouth. The navy started as a way to force passing ships to call at the port and pay the toll. Over time, that coercion slid into raiding.

Then came the test. In **1025**, the Chola king Rājendra attacked the strait. The Cholas did not have a navy. The record shows no standing warships, only merchant shipping pressed into service. The fleet came around through the Sunda Strait, from behind, and hit Palembang during a fast monsoon window. The Srivijayan forces were watching the other door.

And the Cholas left. No garrisons. No administration. They took the jewelled gates and broke the toll monopoly. The last inscription that names Srivijaya as a power is a Chola stone from around **1030**, and the Chinese record goes quiet for half a century, with no Srivijayan envoy arriving between 1028 and 1077 ⚠verify.

So the most famous Indian naval invasion in Southeast Asian history was not an empire at sea attacking an empire at sea. It was a merchant fleet hitting a toll network, then going home. Some scholars now go further and ask whether the state fragmented after 1025, with the Chinese *Sanfoqi* pointing to Jambi rather than the Palembang core.

The capital is still open. Palembang is the orthodox answer. Muara Jambi has its supporters. Chaiya in southern Thailand has them too. The empire on the textbook map may have been a label, not a state.
