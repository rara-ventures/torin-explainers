## Script

Everyone assumes Indian influence arrived on temples, kings and scripts.

This one arrived on cloth. And it ended up as the most sacred object in a village that is supposed to be the least Indian place on the island.

**Start in western India.** Weavers around **Patan** in **Gujarat** make a silk called **patola**. It's double ikat. Ikat means you tie and dye the threads *before* you weave, so the pattern only shows up when the cloth comes off the loom. Double ikat means both the warp and the weft are tied and dyed first. It is the hardest version to get right. Only three places weave it: **India**, **Japan** and **Indonesia**.

Patola was expensive, and it travelled. In the early modern period, elites on **Timor** and in the **Maluku islands** wanted patola, or copies of it, and European merchants supplied them. ⚠verify: one Wikipedia page says the Dutch East India Company traded patola for exclusive spice rights in Indonesia, with no citation.

Now go to **Tenganan**, a village in **East Bali**. Its people are called **Bali Aga**, the original Balinese. They say they descend from a kingdom that was there before the Hindu-Javanese arrived. ⚠verify: that origin is a village tradition, not a dated record. Their cloth is **geringsing**, and it is also double ikat.

Here is the twist. Some geringsing copy patola. One motif, a frangipani flower called **jepun**, is taken straight from the Indian cloth. The textile historian John Guy writes that "some cloths display the unmistakable influence of patola." ⚠verify: page number of the Guy quote.

Now look at the village rules. Indigo is forbidden in Tenganan. The threads have to be sent to the neighbouring village of **Bugbug** to be dyed. Women do not weave while they are menstruating. The yarn is soaked in candlenut oil and wood ash for weeks. ⚠verify: the 42-day soak comes from a secondary summary.

Then look at where the cloth goes. A child gets their first geringsing at the haircut. Boys and girls are carried in one at the youth initiation. Couples sit on one at the wedding. When someone dies, the cloth covers their hips. It can't be used again, so it's usually sold.

That's a sacred cloth for every stage of life, in a village built on being old and separate.

Now the vault's question: is this Indianization? Our test is strict. A polity counts only if it adopts at least **two** of four things: an Indic script, Indic law, Indic kingship, or an Indic state cult. Trade doesn't count. Geringsing passes none of them. There is no temple, no law book and no king. What it carries is a pattern.

That's why it matters. The pattern came with the cloth, not with the court.

And here's the reversal. One theory says Balinese cloths were sent to India and copied there, which would make the copy the origin. ⚠verify: this theory is stated by Wikipedia and attributed to the Guy book, not confirmed in the book itself.

For centuries, the village that kept itself apart has been wearing an outside pattern. The real question isn't who influenced whom. It's who gets to call a design their own.

## Connection

- **[[Bali — Region]]:** this note calls the Bali Aga "an internal control for what 'not Indianized' looks like." Geringsing breaks the control. The least Indianized village's most sacred object carries an Indian design, so the control was contaminated by an object, not by a court.
- **[[thesis]] (`indianization/thesis.md`):** trade alone doesn't count. Geringsing shows an object can carry Indian motifs into a ritual without passing the two-of-four test. That is a useful edge case for the bright line: motifs can travel without the polity changing.
- **[[Trade Maritime — Vector]]:** the vector lists textiles among its goods. This adds a status step: patola moved from cargo to ritual valuables, and the Timor and Maluku demand for it runs through the same early-modern routes.
- **[[12-betel-runs-the-other-way]]:** betel went from Island Southeast Asia into India. If the copying theory holds, a Balinese design went the other way. Both are reverse flows the one-way story leaves out.
- **[[Majapahit — Region]]:** the 1365 *Nagarakretagama* lists Bali as a dependency. One geringsing source says the poem also mentions geringsing curtains on a royal carriage. ⚠verify: that claim is not on the poem's own pages, so if it holds, the Javanese court and the Bali Aga shared the same cloth.

## Accuracy flags

- ⚠verify **Patan vs Khambhat.** The patola sari page places the weavers at Patan. The ikat page places the patola at Khambat (Khambhat). Both are Gujarat, but the pages disagree on the town. The script says "around Patan" and flags it here.
- ⚠verify **Dutch spice-rights claim.** The ikat page says the VOC traded patola for exclusive spice rights with Indonesian sultanates. No citation is given.
- ⚠verify **Timor and Maluku demand.** The patola sari page cites Billore and Hägerdal (2019) for elite demand and European supply. The full text was not accessible (403), so this rests on the citation record and a Wikipedia summary.
- ⚠verify **Guy quote.** "some cloths display the unmistakable influence of patola" is quoted from Wikipedia citing Guy 1998/2009, p. 13 or p. 96. Not checked against the book.
- ⚠verify **copying theory.** Balinese cloth copied in India is stated as "one theory" on Wikipedia, with no named proponent.
- ⚠verify **Nagarakretagama geringsing curtains.** Only the geringsing page says so. The *Nagarakretagama* page and the vault's Bali note do not. Treat as unconfirmed.
- ⚠verify **Rangga Lawe sashes.** Same problem: only the geringsing page states it.
- ⚠verify **Origin of the Bali Aga.** The pre-Majapahit descent comes from the Tenganan page and the Bali Aga page. The vault's Bali note rates origin legends "low confidence." The script says "a village tradition," not a dated fact.
- ⚠verify **42-day soak.** Wikipedia attributes this to Ramseyer 1975 via a secondary summary; "up to 12 times" is from Gillow 1992. Not checked against the primary source.
- ⚠verify **Sukawana 883 inscription.** Only in the Bali Aga page, which marks it uncited. Not used in the script; kept as a lead.
- ⚠verify **Indra legend.** A village legend, not a source. Not used in the script.
