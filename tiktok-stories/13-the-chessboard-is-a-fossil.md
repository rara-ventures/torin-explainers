## Script

India invented the ancestor of chess, and Southeast Asia kept it while Europe rewrote it.

Start with the name. *Chaturanga* means "the four limbs" — the four arms of the Indian army: infantry, cavalry, elephants and chariots. It's known from around the sixth and seventh centuries CE. When it went to Persia it became *shatranj*, and when it went into Europe it became chess, and the pieces got re-cast as a medieval court: king, queen, bishop, knight, castle.

Thailand took the game from the other direction, and the pieces went somewhere completely different.

**Khun** — "lord." That's the king.
**Met** — "seed." That's the queen-equivalent, and it moves **one single square diagonally**. That is the old Persian *ferz*, the general's aide. Europe super-charged the queen around 1475, in Valencia, and the game became fast and brutal; the reason it's called "the Queen's Game" is that nobody had seen a piece move like that before. Thai chess never took that upgrade. **Thailand is still playing the pre-modern queen.**
**Khon** — "nobleman." That's the bishop-equivalent, and it moves one square diagonally or one straight ahead. Not a bishop at all. A different piece, with a different job.
**Ma** — "horse." The knight, and here it finally matches.
**Ruea** — **"boat."** Where Europe put a castle, Thailand put a ship.
**Bia** — **"cowrie shell."** Where Europe put a pawn, Thailand put *money*: the pawns are named after the shells that were currency across the Indian Ocean, and in old sets some of them were literally made from cowrie shells. The rest were cut from water-buffalo horn, white and black.

Look at what that rename does. India's four-army land force — chariots, elephants — arrives in a monsoon maritime world, and within a few generations the chariot becomes a boat and the foot soldier becomes a shell you pay with. The piece list is the cultural evidence: you can read the Thai economy off the board.

Cambodia has the same game, called **ouk** or **ouk chaktrang** — and *chaktrang* is the same word as *chaturanga*, still sitting in the Khmer language. There's a bas-relief from the Khmer empire showing people playing a chess-like game, and there is evidence that ouk has been played in Cambodia since the twelfth century. So you have a stone carving at Angkor and a grandfather in a Bangkok park, playing essentially the same thing.

And the rules do something else Europe never did. In makruk, when the pawns are gone, the game is not "win or draw" — it starts **counting**. The weaker side announces a count and the stronger side has a fixed number of moves to deliver mate or it's a draw: 64 moves if nothing is left but horses and seeds, 16 with one boat, 8 with two. It converts "you are clearly better" into "you must actually finish it." That's a gambling-market logic in a board game — the losing player buys time, the winning player is on a clock. It exists in no Western version.

Then there's the detail that tells you how it travelled. H. J. R. Murray, in *A History of Chess*, argued the game moved into Southeast Asia **with the expansion of Buddhism** — the same vector that carried Sanskrit, the scripts and the monasteries. The game came in the boat that brought the alphabet.

So the missing chapter in every "India influenced Asia" story isn't temples or texts. It's that a pastime, a money-system and a set of odds ratios came along the same route — and Thailand kept the medieval version while Europe built the modern one.
