Borobudur isn't a temple you enter. It's a book you orbit.

Around 782, a dynasty called Sailendra decided to turn a natural hill in Central Java into a 35-meter mandala. 60,000 cubic meters of andesite, no mortar, 9 stacked terraces — 6 square, 3 circular — topped by a single empty stupa.

On the walls: 2,672 panels. The bottom hidden foot — 160 panels of *Karmawibhangga*, sin and hell — was buried almost immediately, maybe for shame, maybe because the hill slipped.

Then you climb. Square galleries = Rupadhatu, the world of form. You walk 5 kilometers clockwise, reading Lalitavistara (the Buddha's life), Jataka tales, Gandavyuha — like flipping pages with your feet.

At the top, the squares end. Three circles = Arupadhatu, formlessness. 72 perforated stupas, each with a Buddha inside, staring out through stone lattice. No more stories. Only geometry.

It was built by Mahayana Buddhists who'd just invited monks from Bengal — Kumaraghosa from Gauḍi — to teach Tara worship at nearby Kalasan (778). Their tower faced Bengal; Borobudur faced the cosmos.

30 kilometers away, their Hindu cousins built Prambanan — a 47-meter Shiva tower — at the same time. Same valley, two cosmologies, no war.

By 928, Merapi ash and politics buried it. Jungle took it until Raffles' man found it in 1814.

You don't pray at Borobudur. You are the sentence moving through it.
