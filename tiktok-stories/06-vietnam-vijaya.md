For 1,200 years Champa ruled Vietnam's coast — Sanskrit, Shiva, Po Nagar's goddess.

By 1470, it was two rumps: Vijaya in the north around Quy Nhon, Panduranga in the south near Phan Rang.

Its king, Tra Toan, asked Ming China for protection against Dai Viet. Dai Viet's Le Thanh Tong — a Confucian reformer with a Ming-style bureaucracy and 150,000 troops — took the letter as casus belli. On 8 January 1471 he declared Tra Toan illegitimate.

February, 5,000 Cham war elephants ambushed the Vietnamese in the foothills near Sa Ky. The Vietnamese had dug traps. The elephants turned.

March, Vijaya itself — harbor blocked, catapults hurling stone and fire for three days. On 22 March the walls fell. The chronicle: 40–60,000 killed, 30,000 captured including Tra Toan and his family. Prisoners had left ears cut for Dai Viet ancestor temples. Tra Toan was beheaded en route north; his head was hung under the imperial ship's bow under a white flag.

North of Cu Mong Pass was annexed. The south was split into three weak dukedoms. The last, Panduranga, survived as a Vietnamese protectorate until 1832, when Minh Mang dissolved it.

Champa didn't become Muslim like Java. Central Champa split instead: half stayed Shaiva — Cham Ahiér — still praying at Po Nagar today. Half became Cham Bani — a local Islam where priests pray *for* you, so farmers don't have to fast. Same goddess, now Bani.

The kingdom fell. The mother stayed.
