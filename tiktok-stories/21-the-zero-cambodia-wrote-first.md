## Script

Here is the story everyone learns. India invented zero. Arab traders carried it west. Europe picked it up and never looked back.

It's tidy. A stone from Cambodia doesn't fit.

On an inscription from Kratié province, the number **605** is written in Khmer numerals. It's counted in the **Saka era**, so it means **683 CE**. The zero inside that 605 is a real digit. It's a placeholder that says there are no tens. Reference works cite it as the oldest firmly dated zero in the world. ⚠verify

Now the messy part. India had zero as an idea long before that. Pingala used a word for it, *śūnya*, around the **3rd or 2nd century BCE**. But he used it as a marker, not a number. The first undisputed Indian zero is on a stone at **Gwalior**, dated **876 CE**. That's almost two centuries after Cambodia.

Scholars don't agree on that gap. A Jain cosmology text, the *Lokavibhāga*, is dated **458 CE** and is said to use place value with a zero. The Bakhshali manuscript, with its black dot, got a radiocarbon range of **799 to 1102 CE**. If those dates hold, India is earlier again. ⚠verify

So the honest answer is that zero's first appearance is contested. Cambodia's is simply the oldest one we can point to with a date.

Now the calendar, which is the stranger part. The number 605 only means something because of the era it counts from. The Saka era starts in **78 CE**. Its name points to the Shaka rulers of the northwest, but historians can't say who started it. The leading candidate is a ruler named **Chashtana**. Another old legend ties the start to a king named Vikramaditya defeating the Shakas. A standard reference calls that legend an obvious fabrication. ⚠verify

So the count starts at an event nobody can name for sure.

And it travelled. Java kept Saka years for centuries after it adopted Islam. Then, in **1633**, Sultan Agung rebuilt the calendar on lunar, Islamic-style months. But he kept the Saka count. The year was **1555** at the time. One reference puts the result plainly: the Javanese year doesn't count from any actual start at all.

**Bali** still runs on Saka today, alongside its 210-day Pawukon cycle. And in **1957**, independent India made the Saka era its national calendar.

So think about what India really exported. It wasn't only the digit. It was a number system a Cambodian scribe could use to date a stone. And it was a starting point so old and disputed that Java kept counting from it through a change of religion, and Bali still does.

The digit was the easy part. What crossed the sea was a clock that started at a moment nobody can prove.

## Connection

- [[Funan Chenla — Timeline]] and [[7th Century CE — Timeline]]: the vault dates the Sambor foundation charter (K.604) to **627**. The 683 stone is a second Sambor-area date, written in numerals instead of a Sanskrit eulogy. The vault has no row for it. The Wikipedia zero entry places the stone near Sambor on the Mekong in Kratié, not at Isanapura, so keep the two sites separate until checked.
- [[Angkor — Timeline]]: the vault's 612 Old Khmer text shows the script arriving. The numerals are the next step. Angkorian inscriptions used Sanskrit number words for dates, and the Khmer numerals page says that practice lasted into the last century. Literacy meant counting in the new system, not only writing in it.
- [[Sailendra Mataram — Timeline]] (778 Kalasan, Saka 700): the vault already converts Saka years to CE. The new piece is the epoch itself. Saka 700 is a convention built on a disputed start, not a fact about a reign.
- [[Bali — Region]]: the vault lists the Saka calendar next to the 210-day Pawukon. This script explains why it lasted: Java gave up the Saka lunar-solar system in 1633 but kept its count.
- [[Persia — Region]] and [[indianization/thesis|thesis]]: the Sindhind reached Baghdad in the 770s, and the vault's own summary is that "Indian cyclic time survives, the Sanskrit apparatus does not." That is the relay pattern for calendars. The bright-line test counts script, law, kingship and cult, and has no slot for a calendar. Java's Saka count is a case the test does not cover. This is an argument, not a settled finding.

## Accuracy flags

- ⚠verify **"oldest firmly dated zero"**: stated on the Wikipedia zero and Khmer numerals pages, citing Diller 1996 ("New Zeroes and Old Khmer", ANU). Diller was not reachable this session. The Wikipedia zero page also says some 6th-century copper plates with a small "o" may be older but are of doubtful date or authenticity.
- ⚠verify **findspot**: the zero page says the stone was found near Sambor on the Mekong in Kratié, and was once part of a temple doorway. The Sambor Prei Kuk page does not mention the 605 stone. Do not place it at Isanapura on screen.
- ⚠verify **Indian dates**: Gwalior 876 CE is called the first indubitable zero. The Lokavibhāga (458 CE via Saka 380) and the Bakhshali radiocarbon range (799–1102 CE) are both disputed. Pingala's "3rd or 2nd century BCE" is a range.
- ⚠verify **Saka origin**: Chashtana is the "widely equated" candidate, and Kanishka (78 CE, now revised to 127 CE by Falk) was an older view. The Vikramaditya legend is called fabricated in a standard reference.
- ⚠verify **1555 Saka at the 1633 switch**: taken from the Javanese calendar page. Cross-check with Ricklefs 2008 before filming.
- The script's "about 200 years" gap is 683 to 876 CE: 193 years.
- The "Bali still counts" claim is about the Balinese Saka calendar in use today, not about the Saka epoch being active as a year count there. Keep the wording as written.
- Hook 6 ("about 200 years earlier") depends on the Gwalior date. If the Lokavibhāga or Bakhshali dates are accepted, the gap flips and the hook fails.
