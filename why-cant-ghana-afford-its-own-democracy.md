# Why can’t Ghana afford its own democracy?

**Source:** [https://torinrussell.com/explainers/why-cant-ghana-afford-its-own-democracy](https://torinrussell.com/explainers/why-cant-ghana-afford-its-own-democracy)

For decades, Ghana faced serious political instability. But it has run an unbroken multiparty democracy since 1992, and since 2000 power has changed hands between its two main parties three times, even as many of its neighbors in West Africa face coups and domestic volatility, and despite major economic problems. So how has Ghana managed to stabilize politically, and what impact does this have on its geopolitical dynamics?

The country's political history begins in many ways in the 15th century, when the Akan states, a group of autonomous kingdoms and polities, formed in the forest region of modern Ghana and Cote d'Ivoire.

These states flourished thanks to their production of gold. But it took another two centuries for one of them to unify the rest. By the late 1600s the Akan states were paying tribute to the powerful neighboring kingdom of Denkyira, and according to tradition, a priest named Okomfo Anokye called down a sacred golden stool from the sky in front of the assembled chiefs, where it came to rest on the lap of Osei Tutu, the ruler of Kumasi.

That act of legitimacy let him unite the Akan states, crush Denkyira at the Battle of Feyiase in 1701, and form the Asante Empire. The Empire ruled the region for the next 200 years, with a centralized bureaucracy, taxation, a standing army, and a road network radiating from Kumasi.

But in the 19th century, the British fought with the empire for control over trade, land, and the Gold Coast in general. The Asante claimed dominion over coastal areas and expected to be paid rent or tribute for British forts, while the British supported groups along the coast who the Asante viewed as their subjects and traditional rivals.

Ultimately Britain's greater firepower, logistical capability, and systematic dismantling of the Asante leadership decided the region’s fate. The British exiled the Empire’s supreme ruler, Prempeh I, to the Seychelles in 1896, crushed the War of the Golden Stool in 1900, and formally annexed the empire as a crown colony in 1902.

Across the Gold Coast, Britain generally preserved existing chiefs and ruled through them rather than governing directly like France did in neighboring Cote d'Ivoire and Upper Volta, which is now Burkina Faso. In Asante itself that took time. The British ran the territory directly for three decades before restoring rule through chiefs with the Asante Confederacy Council in 1935. Britain administered the colonial Northern Territories as a reserve of laborers, and they didn’t invest much into it even while the rest of the colony was thriving.

Ghana was the richest colony in British tropical Africa when it gained independence, as well as the world's leading cocoa producer and a major gold exporter.

The Gold Coast also had a substantial colonial-era population of teachers, doctors, lawyers, accountants, and other professionals, thanks to European missionary schools. That gave the region a functioning civil service.

But after World War II, the colony hosted a growing nationalist movement, and protests against British rule became more common. In 1957, it finally gained independence.

Unfortunately this didn’t pan out like many Ghanaians thought it would. Under President Kwame Nkrumah, the country pursued large and ambitious industrial and infrastructure projects that required the government to take on massive debt. And because the economy relied so heavily on cocoa and gold, when global prices fell, state revenues collapsed with them.

The resulting high inflation and public debt caused shortages of basic goods and lowered the quality of life for normal people, and Nkrumah centralized power, suppressed the opposition, and transformed the country into a one-party state through a 1964 referendum that officially passed with 99.9 percent of the vote and was widely regarded as rigged. It made him president for life with both the party and the country.

This kicked off decades of instability, in a repeating cycle of short-lived civilian governments and military regimes from 1966 to 1981. The driver was mostly economic collapse and rivalry inside the military rather than ethnic conflict, though regional and ethnic grievances sharpened as governments came and went.

Beginning in 1983, Ghana began to implement strict economic recovery and structural adjustment programs backed by the IMF and the World Bank, as well as severe austerity measures and market liberalization to stop economic decay, control inflation, and lay the groundwork for a stable and free market economy. That worked well enough that Ghana became the donors’ showcase for how structural adjustment should work, though it took time for the political environment to open.

Only around 1990 did Western aid donors begin actually tying financial support to human rights protections and democratic reform, and this coincided with domestic groups pressuring the government to restore multiparty democracy and repeal repressive laws.

In 1992, voters approved a new democratic constitution via referendum, and the ruling military regime, the Provisional National Defense Council, or PNDC, lifted the ban on party politics. The transition was rocky. The PNDC's head, Jerry Rawlings, won the November presidential vote with 58 percent, but the opposition alleged fraud, published a dossier called against him, and boycotted the December parliamentary elections, handing his party 189 of 200 seats and a one-party parliament for the whole first term. Rawlings won again in 1996 and only stepped down in 2001.

In Ghana, no single group is a majority. The 2021 census puts the Akan at about 46 percent, large enough to anchor national politics but not to rule alone, which prevents the kind of bipolar standoff Cote d'Ivoire has had. Counterintuitively, that fragmentation is part of what makes the democracy work, because mobilizing any single ethnic group is a losing strategy. It is only a contributing condition rather than a cause, though. Plenty of diverse countries are not democratic.

Today there are two major national parties, the New Patriotic Party or NPP, and the National Democratic Congress, or NDC. Power regularly swings between them because neither party’s base is large enough to win nationally, requiring them to compete for swing regions, which include the middle belt, Greater Accra, the north, and the Central and Western regions. No party has yet won three consecutive presidential terms.

The 1992 constitution also guarantees traditional chieftaincy and established a National House of Chiefs, letting traditional authority operate in parallel with democratic institutions. It is often stabilizing, but succession disputes are not a minor exception. In 2002 the Dagbon crisis saw one chief killed during a feud between factions, and this festered until 2019. The dispute between the Kusasi and Mamprusi in Bawku has killed more than 300 people since 2021.

The military also plays a very different role since the 1992 constitution took hold. Ghana is the seventh-largest supplier of uniformed UN peacekeepers, and some argue that successive governments have deliberately used those deployments to keep officers professionally focused and financially satisfied to avoid coups. Researchers disagree about how true this is though. Other work finds overseas deployments can just as easily breed pay grievances and mutinies.

On the geographical side, Ghana has some other strategic advantages over the rest of the region. It’s a transit corridor for landlocked neighbors like Burkina Faso, Mali, and Niger, which gives the country leverage but also exposure, because instability and trade disruption tends to flow both ways. Togo’s port of Lome is a key competitor for the same Sahel transit traffic.

But economic diversification into the oil market has not spared Ghana from volatility. Cocoa is in distress, oil output is struggling, and while record gold prices have propped up export earnings, a price spike is not the stability the country needs. Government revenue is low, and in 2022 Ghana defaulted on its debt, inflation hit 54 percent, and the IMF put public debt at about 93 percent of GDP that year. Nearly every election year results in a severe destabilization of the country’s fiscal health as well due to politically motivated overspending, which often causes capital to be wasted on projects that don’t actually get finished.

In combination with massive energy sector debt, billions owed to independent power producers and gas suppliers, and chronic losses at the state distributor, this has caused severe recurrent blackouts, known locally as dumsor. The government cleared about 1.5b dollars of that debt in 2025, but the IMF warns the blackouts return if reforms stall.

It has also left pollution unchecked. The same gold that built the Akan states is now poisoning their rivers, because when prices surge, illegal mining scales up, choking rivers with sediment far beyond what treatment plants can handle. Governments keep deploying soldiers and passing new laws, and enforcement keeps failing, partly because politicians and party financiers are themselves implicated.

Meanwhile, Ghana sits on the front line of the Sahel's jihadist crisis, but unlike Benin and Togo it has not been attacked on its own soil. Some analysts think that is partly because militant groups use northern Ghana as a rear area to rest, resupply, and get medical treatment rather than as a battleground. But that still risks spillover, and Ghana relies on food and trade with the Sahel, which makes the border very difficult to police.

Ghana is central to the Economic Community of West African States, or ECOWAS, which is meant to facilitate free trade, regional security, and infrastructure development. But ECOWAS failed to reverse the coups in Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger, which left the bloc in January 2025 to form their own Alliance of Sahel States, with Russian state-linked contractors like the Africa Corps operating alongside their armies. Fascinatingly, Ghana broke with the ECOWAS hard line and appointed a special envoy to the juntas in 2025 to keep them engaged.

But Ghana leaned hard on the IMF to escape its 2022 default, and by late 2025 that program was working, with inflation back in single digits. The country has a genuine democracy, but the electoral dynamics have made it less stable by rewarding aggressive political spending, and the recovery rests on volatile gold and cocoa earnings and a declining oil sector. Without diversifying into something more durable, Ghana stays one shock away from repeating this cycle.
