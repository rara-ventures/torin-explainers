# Why does the US still need nuclear command aircraft?

**Source:** [https://torinrussell.com/explainers/why-does-the-us-still-need-nuclear-command-aircraft](https://torinrussell.com/explainers/why-does-the-us-still-need-nuclear-command-aircraft)

For 29 straight years, the US kept at least one nuclear command and control aircraft in the sky at all times. Each one carried an Air Force general who could take command of the nation's nuclear bombers and missiles if Washington, DC and Strategic Air Command headquarters were destroyed.

And now, the US is investing in a new generation of aircraft for that mission, and at least one member of Congress wants it back in the air around the clock. So why is the US reinvesting in Cold War-era nuclear continuity, and what impact does this have on geopolitics?

After WWII, it didn’t take long for the Soviet Union to develop nuclear weapons of its own, and not just bombs like the ones dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

The development of intercontinental ballistic missiles, or ICBMs, reduced warning times to minutes. A single missile targeting Strategic Air Command headquarters in Nebraska, the predecessor of US Strategic Command, could effectively cut the link between the president and the US's nuclear forces.

The solution to this was to develop an airborne command post that would survive that link being severed and ensure that someone could initiate a launch. This program, called Looking Glass, mirrored the underground command post at Offutt Air Force Base but onboard a converted refueling aircraft.

In 1961, just after the Kennedy administration entered office, the first one took off, and for nearly three decades after, there was always one in the air, supported by aerial refuelers.

Of course, the Looking Glass mission, flown for most of its history on the EC-135C, was supported by layers of redundant nuclear command and control, including backup airborne command posts on alert at bases in Louisiana, Massachusetts, and California. If Strategic Air Command headquarters was destroyed, command would pass down a set line of succession that included the commanders at those bases.

The general aboard the Looking Glass could step in as interim commander and direct a retaliatory strike under strict, pre-written conditions and until the president or a successor could be reached.

Looking Glass would relay and originate Emergency Action Messages, or EAMs, to bombers, to missile crews, and, through the broader network, to nuclear-armed submarines.

On 24 October 1962 during the Cuban Missile Crisis, Strategic Air Command went to DEFCON 2, one step below maximum readiness. This was the first and only time any US force has gone to that alert level, and within a day, Strategic Air Command had 1436 bombers, 145 ICBMs, and about 2900 nuclear weapons on alert.

Strategic Air Command kept 65 nuclear-armed B-52s in the air at any given moment, and the Looking Glass on duty stayed up throughout, ready to issue commands if the US command structure was destroyed.

Cuba was the closest the Looking Glass ever came to being used for real, though false alarms in 1979 and 1980 also put US nuclear forces on alert. Supporters would argue that its real value was that its existence made it less likely to be used, because it denied the Soviets any hope that a surprise strike could decapitate US command.

In 1990, eight months after the Berlin Wall fell, continuous alert status ended, moving instead to a modified alert concept of random airborne sorties with ground alert the rest of the time. The basic mission remained unchanged, but the Cold War was ending, and the mission was expensive. It appeared that the nuclear threat had dramatically changed.

Strategic Air Command was deactivated in 1992 and replaced by US Strategic Command, and Looking Glass became a joint mission manned by Air Force and Navy flag officers.

Then in 1998, the Navy's E-6B Mercury, flying out of Tinker Air Force Base in Oklahoma, took over from the aging EC-135Cs. The E-6Bs handle the TACAMO, or Take Charge and Move Out, mission that relays Emergency Action Messages to submerged nuclear-armed submarines, as well as the Airborne Command Post function of the Looking Glass mission. Today they sit on round-the-clock ground alert, ready to launch within 15 minutes, and fly regularly for training and deployments rather than nonstop.

The Navy is replacing them with the new C-130-based E-130J for the submarine mission, while the Air Force takes the Looking Glass mission back and picks a new aircraft for it. At the same time, the E-4B Nightwatch, the so-called Doomsday plane that serves as a flying command center for the president and top defense leaders, is being replaced by the new E-4C.

But in 2024, Congressman Don Bacon of Nebraska, a retired Air Force brigadier general, publicly advocated to bring back 24/7 airborne Looking Glass operations at Offutt AFB. The military hasn't adopted the idea, but there's a reason he's making the case.

Even with satellites handling early warning and communications, airborne command posts are still critical. Satellites can be targeted with anti-satellite weapons, jamming, spoofing, and cyber attacks against their ground stations, and any of these could blind early warning or disrupt communications.

Not to mention, in February 2026, the New START Treaty expired, meaning that the US and Russia are no longer legally capped at 1550 deployed strategic nuclear warheads and 700 deployed missiles and bombers. On-site inspections had already stopped in 2020, and Russia suspended data exchanges in 2023, but now there isn't even a treaty to bring them back under. And without guardrails, there is now a risk of an arms race.

Some critics blame the US government for this. Putin offered to keep observing the treaty's central limits for one year after it expired if the US did the same, but the US never formally accepted this. Of course, Russia had already suspended its participation in the treaty back in 2023. Today the country says it will stick to the New START treaty limits as long as the US doesn’t expand, but a 2026 US State Department report assessed that Russia likely exceeded the deployed warhead cap in late 2025, even before the treaty expired.

The collapse of arms control raises the value of survivable nuclear command and control dramatically, because without inspections and data exchanges, each side has a much blurrier picture of the other's posture. A surreptitious upload of extra warheads could give one side greater counterforce, more ability to destroy the other side's nuclear forces during an exchange. That's why it's critical for command systems to move, fly, and survive. Staying mobile makes them hard to target, and being seen in the air sends a deterrent signal of its own.

China is also expanding its arsenal, from over 600 warheads in 2024 to possibly more than 1000 by 2030. The US says China is building an early warning counterstrike posture, meaning it could launch its missiles after detecting an incoming attack but before the warheads land, similar to the launch on warning option the US retains.

This means that the US now faces two major nuclear rivals at once, and it has to navigate those dynamics alongside the UK, France, India, Pakistan, North Korea, and Israel. Each has its own nuclear command and control mechanics as well that complicate things.

The long story short is that nuclear deterrence is growing more treacherous. China won't join arms control talks while it builds up, treaties are going away, and it is hugely important that these parties have no doubt that the US will respond to a nuclear attack. That's where aircraft like these come into play.
