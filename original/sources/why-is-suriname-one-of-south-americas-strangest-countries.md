# Why is Suriname one of South America’s strangest countries?

**Source:** [https://torinrussell.com/explainers/why-is-suriname-one-of-south-americas-strangest-countries](https://torinrussell.com/explainers/why-is-suriname-one-of-south-americas-strangest-countries)

In 1667, the Dutch and the English signed a treaty that traded one piece of land for another. The English kept a swampy little outpost we now call Manhattan. The Dutch kept a sliver of South American coast that they considered the better deal. It’s called Suriname, and 350 years later that piece of land hosts roughly 750m barrels of recoverable oil, a Chinese-owned gold mine, the highest proportion of Muslims of any country in the Americas, and the political afterlife of a criminal dictator. So with all that in mind, how does Suriname fit into geopolitics today?

With a population of only about 650k, Suriname is South America’s smallest sovereign country, and in many ways its strangest. The official language is Dutch, but the lingua franca is an English-based creole called Sranan Tongo, and the people themselves are incredibly diverse. About a quarter of Suriname’s population are Hindustanis descended from indentured laborers brought from British India in the late 1800s. Maroons, whose African ancestors escaped Dutch plantations and established self-rule in the 18th century, are another 22%. Then there are Creoles; Javanese brought from the Dutch East Indies, who are largely Muslim; Indigenous peoples; and a Chinese community whose roots go back to 1858. In fact, Suriname celebrates Chinese New Year as a national holiday while also being one of only two South American members of the Organization of Islamic Cooperation.

This mosaic only exists because when Dutch slavery ended in 1863, it was replaced by indentured servants from Java, southern China, and British India. When Suriname became independent in 1975, roughly a third of the population emigrated to the Netherlands, and today about 367k people of Surinamese background live in the Netherlands, or over half of the entire population of modern Suriname.

Five years after Suriname gained independence, a 34-year-old sergeant named Desi Bouterse seized power in a military coup. In the late 1980s, Bouterse fought a brutal civil war against Maroon rebels, and his regime was responsible for countless massacres and human rights violations, including against women and children. Eventually, the regime finally lifted the ban on opposition parties and began a transition to civilian rule. In 1987, a new constitution was approved by referendum and national elections were held, but Bouterse was allowed to remain in control of the army, and he continued manipulating the government and vying for power long after he was technically no longer in control.

In 1999, Bouterse was convicted by a Dutch court in absentia for trafficking hundreds of kilograms of cocaine. But in 2010, the National Assembly elected him president. He served two terms. But in December 2019, he was sentenced to 20 years in prison for the December Murders, in which his regime tortured and executed 15 prominent critics at a military base in Paramaribo in 1982. In December 2024, he died on the run of liver failure having never served a day in prison.

In May 2025, Bouterse’s old party narrowly won the country’s parliamentary elections, and a 71-year-old physician named Jennifer Geerlings-Simons became the country’s first female president. Interestingly, she inherited a lot from Bouterse, including political alliances and an enormous economic bet.

In October 2024, the French oil major TotalEnergies and US oil company APA Corporation made a final decision to build a 10.5b USD project called GranMorgu, a 220k barrel per day floating oil production facility. The facility is aiming to begin production in 2028, and Suriname’s projected take over the field’s life is between 16 and 26b USD. For comparison, Suriname’s entire GDP in 2024 was about 4.7b dollars. Next door in Guyana, ExxonMobil’s Stabroek Block hit 900k bpd in November 2025, and that country’s economy grew 62% in 2022. This was the highest growth rate in the world, and Suriname is only 3-5 years behind that curve.

But in Suriname, China has established a strong foothold already. Zijin Mining, a Chinese firm, bought the country’s largest gold mine for $360m in 2022. PetroChina took two offshore oil blocks in 2024, and late that year after four years of negotiation, Suriname restructured over 500m dollars in debt owed to China’s Eximbank and ICBC, making the country a test case for whether the IMF’s traditional debt architecture can coexist with Chinese bilateral lending.

Since the US removed Nicolas Maduro from power in January 2026, Western energy companies have been swarming into the region to capitalize on the situation. The US is strongarming the Venezuelan government into cooperating, Guyana is already a major focal point for Western energy extraction, and French Guiana to Suriname’s east is a literal part of France. What this means is that Suriname is the only non-aligned country in the region that China can capitalize on.

President Geerlings-Simons has moved really quickly against activities that would benefit the economy but harm the environment, pledging to protect nearly 90% of Suriname’s rainforest. The country created a Sovereign Wealth Fund in December 2024, just a few months before she took office, and today the fund has zero dollars. In October 2025, Geerlings-Simons canceled her predecessor’s plan to send every citizen a check for oil royalties.

Today, Suriname has weak institutions, a fresh authoritarian shadow, half a billion dollars in Chinese debt, and ten and a half billion in Western oil capital all in play at the same time. By 2028, Suriname will be the newest petrostate in South America, but whether it becomes the next Guyana or just the next cautionary tale about resource wealth in a tiny country will be decided in the next few years.
