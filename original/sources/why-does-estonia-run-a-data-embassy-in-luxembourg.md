# Why does Estonia run a “data embassy” in Luxembourg?

**Source:** [https://torinrussell.com/explainers/why-does-estonia-run-a-data-embassy-in-luxembourg](https://torinrussell.com/explainers/why-does-estonia-run-a-data-embassy-in-luxembourg)

You probably don't know that Estonia keeps a copy of its government data in Luxembourg. In fact, it's one of only two countries known to run a data embassy abroad. But why would Estonia want to back up its most sensitive government information in a foreign country, and what can this tell us about Estonia's geopolitical dynamics?

Estonia has had a tumultuous history. Sweden ruled the region through most of the 1600s, until Russia seized it in 1710 and Sweden formally ceded it under the Treaty of Nystad in 1721.

For nearly 200 years, Estonia was part of the Russian Empire, where Estonians were mostly peasants dominated by a Baltic German nobility and later faced aggressive Russification. But in 1917, after the tsar was overthrown, Russia's new Provisional Government gave in to mass Estonian demands and united the Estonian-inhabited lands into a single autonomous province.

It took less than a year for Estonia to declare full independence, and it then had to defend that independence in a deadly war against the very early Red Army and, in 1919, German paramilitary forces.

In 1920, the two sides signed the Tartu Peace Treaty, in which Soviet Russia recognized Estonia's independence and renounced all claims to it forever.

The Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact of 1939 changed everything, though. On paper, it was a non-aggression deal between Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union, but a secret protocol divided Eastern Europe between them. Within weeks, the Soviet Union forced Estonia to accept Soviet military bases, and the following year it issued an ultimatum, invaded, and forcibly occupied the country, annexing it as the Estonian Soviet Socialist Republic.

After three years of Nazi occupation, Soviet forces retook Estonia in 1944, beginning nearly 50 years of mass deportations, forced Russification, and oppressive rule.

But the Republic of Estonia didn't go away. From 1944 to 1992, a government in exile, based mostly in Sweden, maintained an unbroken chain of acting heads of state under the Estonian constitution, even though no foreign government ever officially recognized it. Separately, Estonian diplomats in the West, most notably at the consulate general in New York because the Us never recognized the Soviet annexation, kept representing the republic.

Estonia restored its independence in August 1991, months before the Soviet Union itself collapsed. And after new elections in 1992, the government in exile formally handed over its authority.

The memory of all of this has not gone away. Estonia is well aware of how vulnerable it is to Russian aggression, and it is hell-bent on surviving. This year, it has budgeted about 5.4% of its GDP for defense.

But tanks and artillery can't protect everything, and this is where Luxembourg comes in. See, Estonia is one of the most digitized states on the planet, and some of its most critical information only exists in digital form.

In 2013, the government began planning a dedicated data embassy after years of already storing backups in its embassies, and Russia's 2007 cyberattacks and 2014 invasion of Crimea sped that up. The plan had three parts: a government-operated cloud inside Estonia, a physical data embassy in Estonian diplomatic locations or dedicated data centers in allied countries, and a virtual data embassy to back up non-sensitive data in the public clouds of private companies. In late 2014, Estonia tested that last piece with Microsoft.

But the core of this is the physical storage of critical data, starting with ten priority registers. These include things like the treasury information system, the taxable persons register, the pension insurance register, the court filing system, the land register, the business register, and the identity documents register. Imagine if there was no backup of the land register, and no one had ground truth of who owns what land because there’s no paper copy.

This is what Estonia is trying to avoid, and today, 29 databases are backed up in Luxembourg. But as far as public records show, only one of them can actually run live from there: the State Gazette, where Estonian laws have to be published to take effect. The rest would have to be restored from backup first.

But why Luxembourg, you might be asking. Well, the Estonian government has cited several reasons. First off, it needed state-owned high security data centers certified at Tier 4, the most stable and fault-tolerant classification, and at the time, Estonia had none. It also needed very good data connections, a digitally advanced partner, and a host willing to grant the data legal immunity. Luxembourg checked all of those boxes, and it was geographically far enough from Estonia's own servers that one local disaster couldn't take out both.

As an aside, Monaco signed a similar treaty with Luxembourg in 2021 and keeps a digital twin of its government's sovereign cloud there. But for Monaco, the issue is geography. At about two square kilometers in area, it simply has no room for a survivable backup inside its own borders.

Estonia has been very deliberate in building something that will withstand an occupation of the country itself. It uses Estonian-owned equipment, a government-to-government treaty, and a public host to optimize for both control and availability.

Under the treaty, Luxembourg officials can't enter the premises without Estonia's approval, except in an emergency like a fire, and the data is legally treated as Estonian state archives. That protection has never been tested in a real crisis, and either side can exit the treaty with two years' notice.

But even then, the data can only go to Estonia's authorized representative, and if no one can identify who that is, Luxembourg has to guard it like its own archives until a legal representative of Estonia comes forward. The treaty doesn't say exactly why, but to me and many others, it reads like a lesson from the occupation, when Estonia's government in exile spent decades without recognition from any other government.

So what does a data center in Luxembourg tell us about Estonia? It tells us this is a country that has already lost its territory once, and it came back as the same state because its legal claim and its records outlived the occupation. The data embassy is Estonia's way of making sure that can happen again, now that so much of its state exists only in digital form. For now, it works more as a protected backup than a government in exile, but the logic is straightforward: even if Estonia lost its land again, it wouldn't have to lose its continuity or its claim to existence.
